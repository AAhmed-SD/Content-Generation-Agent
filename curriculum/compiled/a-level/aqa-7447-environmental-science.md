## 3.7 Research methods and fieldwork

Both papers test research methods. AQA does not set a list of experiments; instead every student must have first-hand experience of six methodologies (Me1 to Me6) and six groups of sampling technique (ST1 to ST6), with at least four days of fieldwork (or two field days plus twelve lab activities). For each technique, know its purpose, method and limitations. The section also gathers the maths skills listed in every "opportunities for skills development" sub-section.

### 3.7.1 Scientific methodologies (Me1 to Me6)

#### What you need to know

Random sampling; systematic sampling; transects (line, belt, continuous, interrupted); timing of samples; sample size; number of samples; standardised techniques; designs that allow statistical significance to be tested.

#### Explained

| Code | Methodology | When and why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Me1 | Random sampling | Area with no obvious gradient. Grid the area and use random number tables or a generator for coordinates, so every point has an equal chance and personal bias is avoided |
| Me2 | Systematic sampling | Where there is a gradient (shore, sand dune, distance from a road) or to sample at fixed intervals. Transects: a line transect records species touching a line; a belt transect uses quadrats along the line. Continuous transects sample all along; interrupted transects sample at intervals |
| Me3 | Number of samples | Enough samples to represent the variability. Plot a running mean against number of samples; when the mean stops changing much, enough samples have been taken |
| Me4 | Sample size | For example quadrat size: bigger for larger organisms or sparse vegetation; in a uniform (homogeneous) area small samples suffice. Plot species found against quadrat area (species-area curve) and choose the size where the curve levels off |
| Me5 | Sample timing | Conditions vary with weather, season and time of day (diurnal), for example light and temperature, insect activity. Sample at the same time or record conditions, and repeat over time |
| Me6 | Standard deviation and statistical significance | Use enough replicates to calculate the spread and apply a suitable statistical test |

**Standardised techniques**: same equipment, method, person (where possible) and time, so results are comparable between sites.

### 3.7.2.1 Standard sampling techniques (ST1 to ST6)

#### Explained

**Biotic sampling**:

| Code | Technique | Purpose and method | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ST2 | Quadrats: open frame, gridded, point | Frame placed on the ground; record species, percentage cover, frequency or density. Point quadrat: pins lowered through a frame; species touched recorded | Hard to estimate cover consistently; overlapping layers; point quadrats slow |
| ST4 | Pitfall traps | Container sunk level with the ground, covered against rain, catches ground-surface invertebrates | Catches active species more; predators eat others in the trap; check often |
| ST4 | Tullgren funnel | Soil sample on a mesh under a lamp; animals move away from heat and light and fall into alcohol | Only active animals; slow-moving ones may be missed or die |
| ST4 | Earthworm extraction | Dilute mustard or irritant solution poured on soil; worms surface; or hand-sort a dug soil block | Not all worms respond; irritant can harm |
| ST5 | Sweep nets | Swept through long vegetation to catch insects | Catches only those on the upper vegetation; varies with operator |
| ST5 | Beating trays | Branches tapped over a tray to dislodge invertebrates | Flying insects escape |
| ST5 | Light traps | Lamp attracts night-flying insects (moths) into a box | Attracts from an unknown area; weather affects catch |
| ST5 | Bat detectors | Convert ultrasound calls to audible sound or record sonograms | Species identification needs skill or software |
| ST6 | Pond net and kick sampling | Kick the stream bed for a fixed time upstream of a net held in the flow | Hard to standardise effort |
| ST6 | Surber sampler | Frame of fixed area on the stream bed with a net; disturb inside the frame | Only shallow riffles |
| ST6 | Colonisation media | Artificial substrate (stones in a mesh bag) left in water, colonised then lifted | Takes weeks; may not match natural habitat |

**Measures of abundance and diversity**:

- DAFOR scale: Dominant, Abundant, Frequent, Occasional, Rare. Quick but subjective.
- Species richness: number of species. Species frequency: percentage of quadrats in which a species occurs. Density: number per unit area. Percentage cover: proportion of ground covered.
- **Lincoln Index** (mark-release-recapture): N = (n₁ × n₂) ÷ m₂, where n₁ = number marked in first sample, n₂ = number in second sample, m₂ = number marked in second sample. Assumes no births, deaths or migration, marks are not lost and do not affect survival, and marked animals mix fully.
- **Simpson's Index of Diversity**: D = 1 − Σ(n ÷ N)², where n is the number of individuals of each species and N the total. Values near 1 mean high diversity. (Some versions use n(n − 1) ÷ N(N − 1).)

**Abiotic measurements (ST1)**:

| Factor | Equipment |
| --- | --- |
| Light | Light meter (lux) |
| Temperature | Thermometer or temperature probe; data logger |
| Wind speed | Anemometer |
| Humidity | Hygrometer (wet and dry bulb) |
| Turbidity | Secchi disc (depth at which it disappears) or turbidity meter |
| pH | pH meter or indicator |
| Ion concentration (nitrate) | Test kits, colorimeter or ion-selective probe |

**Edaphic (soil) factors (ST3)**:

- Texture: sedimentation (shake soil with water in a measuring cylinder; sand settles first, then silt, then clay) or sieving; plot percentages on a soil texture triangle to name the soil.
- Water content: weigh, dry at about 105 °C to constant mass, reweigh. Water content (%) = (fresh mass − dry mass) ÷ dry mass × 100 (or ÷ fresh mass, state which).
- Organic matter: heat dry soil in a furnace (about 500 °C) to burn off organic matter; loss in mass ÷ dry mass × 100.
- pH: mix soil with distilled water (and barium sulfate to settle clay) and test.
- Bulk density: dry mass ÷ volume of a core (see [11](11-soils.md)).

### 3.7.2.2 Field and laboratory activities

Suitable investigations include: population size, density, frequency, distribution and biodiversity studies; soil analysis; climate variability for renewable energy (insolation, wind speed); heat loss from containers of different insulation or volume; biotic indices (lichens for air quality, aquatic invertebrates for water quality); effect of pH on seed germination; turbidity and light penetration; nutrient concentration and algal growth; noise against distance and insulation; slope and vegetation against rain-splash erosion; effect of trees on microclimate.

**Biotic indices**: some species tolerate pollution and others do not. Lichens: shrubby (fruticose) lichens need clean air; crusty (crustose) ones tolerate more SO₂. Freshwater invertebrates: stonefly and mayfly nymphs need high oxygen; bloodworms and rat-tailed maggots survive low oxygen. A score based on which groups are present indicates pollution.

### 3.7.2.3 Specialist techniques (knowledge only)

Camera traps (motion-sensitive) and photographic databases of features (stripe or fin patterns); marking (tags, rings, collars); acoustic recording and sonograms; radio, GPS and satellite tracking; satellite sensor data (land cover, sea temperature, chlorophyll); DNA and tissue databases; indirect evidence (nests, burrows, droppings, feeding signs, owl pellets, tracks, territorial marks). See also [05](05-monitoring-ecology-succession-and-populations.md).

### Maths skills (Appendix B) and statistical tests

#### What you need to know

Units, standard form, ratios, percentages, estimation, logs; significant figures; mean, median, mode; frequency tables and histograms; probability; sampling; scatter graphs and correlation; order of magnitude; measures of spread (range, standard deviation); uncertainty; rearranging and solving equations; logarithms; graphs including kite, Sankey and radar diagrams; y = mx + c; intercepts and gradients; tangents; areas and volumes. Statistical tests: chi-squared, Student's t-test, Spearman's rank, Mann-Whitney U.

#### Explained

**Choosing a test**:

| Question | Test |
| --- | --- |
| Is there a correlation between two variables (paired measurements)? | Spearman's rank correlation |
| Is there a difference between two sets of data that are normally distributed (means)? | Student's t-test |
| Is there a difference between two sets of data that are not normally distributed (medians, ordinal data)? | Mann-Whitney U |
| Do observed frequencies differ from expected, or is there an association between categories? | Chi-squared |

**Null hypothesis**: there is no difference (or no correlation). Compare the calculated value with the critical value at p = 0.05 (5% probability that the result is due to chance). For Spearman's, t and chi-squared, a calculated value greater than the critical value means reject the null hypothesis. For Mann-Whitney U, a calculated U smaller than or equal to the critical value means the difference is significant.

**Spearman's rank**: rₛ = 1 − (6Σd²) ÷ (n(n² − 1)), where d is the difference in ranks. Range −1 to +1.

**Chi-squared**: χ² = Σ (O − E)² ÷ E. Degrees of freedom = number of categories − 1 (or (rows − 1)(columns − 1) for a contingency table).

**Standard deviation** measures spread around the mean; about 68% of normally distributed values lie within one standard deviation of the mean.

**Uncertainty**: percentage uncertainty = (uncertainty ÷ reading) × 100.

#### Worked example

Lincoln Index: 40 beetles caught and marked; next day 50 caught, 8 marked. N = 40 × 50 ÷ 8 = 250.

Simpson's: a quadrat has species A 10, B 6, C 4 (N = 20). Σ(n/N)² = 0.25 + 0.09 + 0.04 = 0.38. D = 1 − 0.38 = 0.62.

Spearman's: 6 sites, Σd² = 4. rₛ = 1 − (6 × 4) ÷ (6 × 35) = 1 − 24 ÷ 210 = 0.886. The critical value for n = 6 at p = 0.05 is 0.886, so the result is just significant; a strong positive correlation.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a significant correlation proves cause.
- Using a t-test on skewed or ranked data.
- Forgetting to state the null hypothesis and the probability level.

### 3.4.5 Species and taxonomy

*Refs: AQA 3.4.5 · Edexcel A 4.6 · Edexcel B 3.1 i to vii · OCR A 4.2.2(a) to (d) · OCR B 3.1.3(a) to (d), (f) · Eduqas C2.1(a) to (g). Level: AS & A.*

#### What you need to know

- Two organisms belong to the same species if they are able to produce fertile offspring. Courtship behaviour as a necessary precursor to successful mating; its role in recognising members of the same species, identifying a mate that is capable of breeding, forming a pair bond, synchronising mating, and becoming able to breed.
- A phylogenetic classification system attempts to arrange species into groups based on their evolutionary origins and relationships. It uses a hierarchy in which smaller groups are placed within larger groups, with no overlap between groups. Each group is called a taxon (plural taxa).
- One hierarchy comprises the taxa: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species.
- Each species is universally identified by a binomial consisting of the name of its genus and species, eg *Homo sapiens*.
- Appreciate that advances in immunology and genome sequencing help to clarify evolutionary relationships between organisms.

#### Explained

**The species concept.** A species is a group of similar organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring. A horse and a donkey can mate, but their offspring (a mule) is infertile, so horses and donkeys are separate species.

**Limits of the definition** (Edexcel B 3.1 ii, iii). It cannot be used for organisms that reproduce asexually (bacteria), for extinct organisms known only from fossils, or for populations that never meet. Some species hybridise and produce fertile young (some plants, some gulls). So biologists also use the morphological concept (similar appearance) and the phylogenetic concept (a group sharing a recent common ancestor, defined by DNA).

**Courtship.** Courtship behaviours (displays, songs, dances) are species-specific. They let animals:

- recognise a member of their own species, so they do not waste energy on matings that give no fertile offspring
- recognise a mate of the opposite sex who is sexually mature and fertile (or receptive)
- form a pair bond, so both parents raise the young
- synchronise mating so it happens when eggs are available.

**Classification.** Organisms are placed in a hierarchy: **D**omain, **K**ingdom, **P**hylum, **C**lass, **O**rder, **F**amily, **G**enus, **S**pecies. Each group contains organisms that share a more recent common ancestor the further down you go. Groups do not overlap.

Example, the grey wolf: Eukarya, Animalia, Chordata, Mammalia, Carnivora, Canidae, *Canis*, *lupus*.

**Binomial naming.** Genus (capital first letter) plus species (lower case), in italics or underlined. It is universal, so it avoids confusion from different common names.

**Five kingdoms and three domains** (OCR A, Edexcel A, Edexcel B, Eduqas).

| Kingdom | Main features |
| --- | --- |
| Prokaryotae | Unicellular; no nucleus; murein walls; 70S ribosomes |
| Protoctista | Mostly unicellular eukaryotes; some like plants, some like animals (algae, amoeba) |
| Fungi | Eukaryotic; chitin walls; saprotrophic nutrition; hyphae; spores |
| Plantae | Multicellular; cellulose walls; chlorophyll; autotrophic |
| Animalia | Multicellular; no cell walls; heterotrophic; nervous coordination |

In 1990 Carl Woese proposed three domains above the kingdoms: **Bacteria**, **Archaea** and **Eukarya**. Evidence: sequences of ribosomal RNA showed archaea are as different from bacteria as both are from eukaryotes. Archaea have different membrane lipids, different RNA polymerase (more like eukaryotes'), and histone-like proteins; bacteria have murein walls but archaea do not. This divides Prokaryotae into two domains.

**Validating new ideas** (Edexcel A, Edexcel B). A new classification is published in a scientific journal after peer review (other experts check the method and conclusions), discussed at conferences, and tested by other scientists. If repeated evidence supports it, it becomes accepted. The three-domain model went through this.

**Phylogeny.** A phylogenetic tree shows evolutionary relationships. Branch points show common ancestors; species that branch apart more recently are more closely related. Classification is now based on phylogeny.

**Evidence used to classify** (OCR B, Eduqas, Edexcel B):

- physical features (anatomy, embryology)
- DNA base sequences and the amino acid sequences of proteins (more different = more distant)
- immunological comparisons: antibodies against a protein from one species are mixed with the same protein from another; the more precipitate forms, the more similar the proteins, so the more closely related the species
- DNA barcoding (OCR B): sequencing a short, standard gene region (in animals, part of the mitochondrial gene for cytochrome c oxidase I; in plants, chloroplast genes) and matching it against a database to identify a species quickly
- gel electrophoresis of DNA or proteins (Edexcel B; method in file 25).

**Language as a model** (OCR B). Languages change over time by small changes passed on, and split when groups of speakers become separated, much as species do. Linguists group words by shared features and build family trees of languages, using the same kind of methods as phylogenetics. It is a model, not evidence for biological evolution.

#### Common mistakes

- Writing the binomial with a capital letter for the species name.
- Saying classification is based on "what organisms look like". Modern classification is based on evolutionary relationships.
- Saying "the more groups two species share, the less related they are".

### 3.4.6 Biodiversity within a community

*Refs: AQA 3.4.6 · Edexcel A 4.1, 4.2, 4.3 · Edexcel B 3.2 ii, 3.3 i · OCR A 4.2.1(a) to (d) · OCR B 3.1.3(h), 4.3.1(l) · Eduqas C2.1(h) to (j). Level: AS & A.*

#### What you need to know

- Biodiversity can relate to a range of habitats, from a small local habitat to the Earth.
- Species richness is a measure of the number of different species in a community.
- An index of diversity describes the relationship between the number of species in a community and the number of individuals in each species. Calculation of an index of diversity (d) from d = N(N − 1) ÷ Σn(n − 1), where N = total number of organisms of all species and n = total number of organisms of each species.
- Farming techniques reduce biodiversity. The balance between conservation and farming.

#### Explained

**Levels of biodiversity** (OCR A, Edexcel B): habitat diversity (the number of different habitats in an area), species diversity (species richness and evenness) and genetic diversity (the variety of alleles within a species).

**Species richness** counts species but ignores how many of each. **Species evenness** describes how equal the numbers of each species are. Two woods may each have 10 species, but if one is 95% oak it is less diverse than one with all ten in similar numbers. An index of diversity combines both.

**Endemism** (Edexcel A): an endemic species is found naturally in only one place (such as the Galápagos giant tortoise). Areas with many endemic species are priorities for conservation.

**Niche** (Edexcel A, Edexcel B). An organism's niche is its role in its habitat: what it eats, what eats it, where and when it feeds, the conditions it tolerates. No two species occupy exactly the same niche in a habitat for long: one outcompetes the other. Adaptations fit organisms to their niche.

**Farming and biodiversity.** Farming reduces biodiversity by: clearing woodland and removing hedgerows; growing monocultures; using pesticides and herbicides; draining wetlands; overgrazing; using fertilisers that favour a few fast-growing species. Ways to balance farming and conservation: keep and replant hedgerows, leave field margins uncultivated, rotate crops, use integrated pest management, maintain ponds and wetlands, pay farmers through agri-environment schemes.

#### Worked example

A pond survey found: species A 10, B 5, C 3, D 2.

**AQA / Edexcel A index:**

1. N = 20. N(N − 1) = 20 × 19 = 380.
2. Σn(n − 1) = (10 × 9) + (5 × 4) + (3 × 2) + (2 × 1) = 90 + 20 + 6 + 2 = 118.
3. d = 380 ÷ 118 = 3.22.

A higher d means more diversity. The lowest possible value is 1 (only one species).

**OCR A / Eduqas Simpson's index:** D = 1 − Σ(n/N)².

1. (10/20)² = 0.25; (5/20)² = 0.0625; (3/20)² = 0.0225; (2/20)² = 0.01.
2. Σ = 0.345. D = 1 − 0.345 = 0.655.

D lies between 0 and 1; values nearer 1 mean higher diversity.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying species richness takes numbers of individuals into account.
- Using N² instead of N(N − 1) in the AQA formula.
- Comparing an AQA index with a Simpson's index; they are on different scales.

### 3.4.7 Investigating diversity

*Refs: AQA 3.4.7 · Edexcel A 4.2 · Edexcel B 3.1 iv, v · OCR A 4.2.1(b), (e) · OCR B 3.1.3(i) · Eduqas C2.1(e), (k), (l). Level: AS & A.*

#### What you need to know

- Genetic diversity within, or between, species can be made by comparing: the frequency of measurable or observable characteristics; the base sequence of DNA; the base sequence of mRNA; the amino acid sequence of the proteins encoded by DNA and mRNA.
- Interpret data relating to similarities and differences in the base sequences of DNA and in the amino acid sequences of proteins to suggest relationships between different organisms within a species and between species.
- Appreciate that gene technology has caused a change in the methods of investigating genetic diversity; inferring DNA differences from measurable or observable characteristics has been replaced by direct investigation of DNA sequences.
- Quantitative investigations of variation within a species involve: collecting data from random samples; calculating a mean value and the standard deviation of that mean; interpreting mean values and their standard deviations.

#### Explained

**Comparing species.** Closely related species share a recent common ancestor, so they have had less time for mutations to build up. Their DNA base sequences, and so their mRNA and amino acid sequences, are more similar. Count the differences: fewer differences mean a closer relationship.

Comparing observable characteristics was the old method. Its problems: many characteristics are controlled by many genes and affected by the environment, and similar features can evolve separately (convergent evolution). DNA sequencing is now fast and cheap, and compares genes directly.

**Random sampling** (all boards). To avoid bias, choose sample sites at random: lay out two tape measures at right angles and use random numbers as coordinates. Take a large sample to reduce the effect of chance. Non-random sampling (OCR A) includes systematic sampling along a transect (when there is a gradient), opportunistic sampling (quick but biased) and stratified sampling (sampling each area in proportion to its size). Field methods are in file 23.

**Mean and standard deviation.** The mean is the average. The standard deviation (SD) measures the spread of data around the mean: about 68% of values lie within 1 SD of the mean, about 95% within 2 SD. If two samples' means ± SD overlap a lot, the difference between them is probably due to chance. Formula and test in file 26.

**Genetic biodiversity within a species** (OCR A, OCR B). Proportion of polymorphic gene loci = number of polymorphic loci ÷ total number of loci tested. A locus is polymorphic if it has more than one allele in the population (commonly with the most common allele below 99% frequency). A higher proportion means more genetic diversity. Eduqas also lists DNA fingerprinting for comparing genetic diversity (file 25).

#### Worked example

The amino acid sequence of one protein was compared in four species. Differences from species W: X 2, Y 14, Z 9. Which species is most closely related to W?

X: fewest amino acid differences, so it shares the most recent common ancestor with W. Order of relatedness to W: X, then Z, then Y.

Genetic diversity: 40 gene loci were tested in a population; 13 were polymorphic. Proportion = 13 ÷ 40 = 0.325.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying that the same amino acid sequence means identical DNA. The code is degenerate, so DNA can differ.
- Saying a large standard deviation means "the data are wrong". It means more spread.
- Choosing sample sites "randomly" by walking about.

### 3.5.3 Energy and ecosystems

*Refs: AQA 3.5.3 · Edexcel A 5.10, 5.11 · Edexcel B 10.1 ii, iii, 10.2 i, ii · OCR A 6.3.1(b) · OCR B 4.3.1(i) to (k) · Eduqas C1.5(f), (h). Level: A-level only.*

#### What you need to know

- In any ecosystem, plants synthesise organic compounds from atmospheric, or aquatic, carbon dioxide.
- Most of the sugars synthesised by plants are used by the plant as respiratory substrates. The rest are used to make other groups of biological molecules. These biological molecules form the biomass of the plants.
- Biomass can be measured in terms of mass of carbon or dry mass of tissue per given area.
- The chemical energy store in dry biomass can be estimated using calorimetry.
- Gross primary production (GPP) is the chemical energy store in plant biomass, in a given area or volume. Net primary production (NPP) is the chemical energy store in plant biomass after respiratory losses to the environment have been taken into account: NPP = GPP − R.
- This net primary production is available for plant growth and reproduction. It is also available to other trophic levels in the ecosystem, such as herbivores and decomposers.
- The net production of consumers (N), such as animals, can be calculated as N = I − (F + R), where I represents the chemical energy store in ingested food, F the chemical energy lost to the environment in faeces and urine and R the respiratory losses.
- Primary and secondary productivity is the rate of primary or secondary production, measured as biomass in a given area in a given time, eg kJ ha⁻¹ year⁻¹.
- The ways in which production is affected by farming practices designed to increase the efficiency of energy transfer by simplifying food webs to reduce energy losses to non-human food chains, and by reducing respiratory losses within a human food chain.

#### Explained

**Trophic levels.** Producers (plants, algae) → primary consumers (herbivores) → secondary consumers → tertiary consumers. Decomposers and detritivores feed on dead material from all levels.

**Biomass.** Fresh mass varies with water content, so dry mass is used. Measuring dry mass means killing the organism and drying it in an oven at about 80 °C until the mass stops changing. Usually a small sample is dried and the result scaled up.

**Calorimetry.** A known dry mass is burned in a bomb calorimeter surrounded by a known volume of water. The temperature rise of the water gives the energy released: energy = mass of water × 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ × temperature rise.

**Why only a small fraction of light becomes plant biomass.**

- Most sunlight is reflected, passes through leaves, or is the wrong wavelength.
- Light may not hit a chlorophyll molecule.
- Other factors (CO₂, temperature) limit photosynthesis.
- Energy is lost as heat in the reactions.

Typically 1 to 3% of light energy falling on a plant ends up as GPP.

**Why transfer between consumers is inefficient** (often about 10%, though it varies):

- Not all of the organism is eaten (roots, bones).
- Some is indigestible and lost in faeces.
- Energy is lost in urine (as urea).
- Most energy absorbed is used in respiration and lost as heat, especially by mammals and birds keeping a constant body temperature.

Because so much is lost at each step, food chains rarely have more than four or five levels.

**Ecological pyramids** (Edexcel B).

| Pyramid | Shows | Problems |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Numbers | Number of organisms at each level | Size ignored: one oak supports thousands of insects, so it can be inverted |
| Biomass | Dry mass per area at each level, at one time | Seasonal change; organisms killed to measure; can be inverted in oceans, where phytoplankton reproduce fast but have little standing biomass |
| Energy | Energy passing through each level per area per year | Hard to measure, but always the right way up and allows comparison of ecosystems |

**Farming practices.**

- *Simplifying food webs:* removing other species that would take energy from the crop. Herbicides remove weeds (competitors for light, water, ions); pesticides and fungicides remove pests that eat the crop. More of the NPP goes to the crop.
- *Reducing respiratory losses in livestock:* keep animals indoors in warm conditions (less energy for heat), restrict movement (less energy for muscle contraction), feed high-energy, easily digested food, slaughter young (young animals put more of their energy into growth). More of the food becomes meat. Ethical concerns: animal welfare, antibiotic overuse.

**Ruminants in the human food chain** (OCR B 4.3.1(k)). Cattle and sheep (file 45) can turn grass and other cellulose-rich plants, which humans cannot digest, into meat and milk, so they make food from land unsuitable for crops. But energy transfer from plants to cattle is inefficient (often less than 10%), so more people can be fed per hectare by eating crops directly; ruminants also release methane, a greenhouse gas (file 42).

#### Worked example

1. Grassland GPP = 21 000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹; plant respiration = 12 500 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹. NPP = 21 000 − 12 500 = 8500 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹.

2. A cow ingests 3000 kJ per day; 1800 kJ is lost in faeces and urine and 1050 kJ in respiration. N = 3000 − (1800 + 1050) = 150 kJ per day. Efficiency of conversion of ingested food to biomass = 150 ÷ 3000 × 100 = 5%.

3. Efficiency of transfer between two trophic levels = (energy in the higher level ÷ energy in the lower level) × 100. If producers have NPP 8500 and primary consumers 900 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹: 900 ÷ 8500 × 100 = 10.6%.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying energy is "used up" or "lost" as if destroyed. It is transferred to the environment as heat.
- Saying energy is recycled.
- Using fresh mass for biomass.
- Forgetting units for productivity (energy per area per time).

### 3.5.4 Nutrient cycles

*Refs: AQA 3.5.4 · Edexcel A 5.21, 6.2 · Edexcel B 10.2 iii · OCR A 6.3.1(c) · OCR B 4.3.1(h) · Eduqas C1.5(k), (l), (n) to (p). Level: A-level only.*

#### What you need to know

- Nutrients are recycled within natural ecosystems, exemplified by the nitrogen cycle and the phosphorus cycle. Microorganisms play a vital role in recycling chemical elements such as phosphorus and nitrogen.
- The role of saprobionts in decomposition. The role of mycorrhizae in facilitating the uptake of water and inorganic ions by plants. The role of bacteria in the nitrogen cycle in sufficient detail to illustrate the processes of saprobiotic nutrition, ammonification, nitrification, nitrogen fixation and denitrification. (Names of individual species are not required.)
- The main stages of the phosphorus cycle, limited to the roles of: saprobionts in decomposition; mycorrhizae in facilitating uptake of phosphate ions by plants; the movement of phosphate ions into ecosystems from rock, and back into rock in sediments.
- The use of natural and artificial fertilisers to replace the nitrates and phosphates lost by harvesting plants and removing livestock.
- The environmental issues arising from the use of fertilisers including leaching and eutrophication.

#### Explained

**Saprobionts** (saprotrophs: decomposing bacteria and fungi) secrete enzymes onto dead organisms and waste, digest them externally, and absorb the soluble products. This releases inorganic ions such as ammonium and phosphate back into the soil.

**The nitrogen cycle.** Plants need nitrogen for amino acids, proteins, nucleotides and chlorophyll, but cannot use nitrogen gas (N₂).

| Process | What happens | Organisms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nitrogen fixation | N₂ → ammonia (NH₃), using the enzyme nitrogenase; needs ATP and anaerobic conditions | Free-living bacteria (*Azotobacter*) and bacteria in root nodules of legumes (*Rhizobium*, which gets sugars from the plant: mutualism). Lightning also fixes some. |
| Ammonification | Nitrogen in proteins, urea and nucleic acids of dead organisms and waste → ammonium ions (NH₄⁺) | Saprobionts |
| Nitrification | NH₄⁺ → nitrite (NO₂⁻) → nitrate (NO₃⁻); oxidation reactions that release energy for the bacteria; needs oxygen | Nitrifying bacteria (*Nitrosomonas*, then *Nitrobacter*) |
| Assimilation | Plants absorb nitrate by active transport and use it to make amino acids | Plants |
| Denitrification | NO₃⁻ → N₂; happens in waterlogged, anaerobic soils | Denitrifying bacteria |

AQA does not require the names of species; the genus names above are given as common examples.

Farmers improve soil nitrogen by ploughing (aerates soil, so nitrification is favoured and denitrification reduced) and draining waterlogged fields (Eduqas C1.5(o)). Ploughing can also increase erosion and the loss of organic matter.

**Studying *Rhizobium*** (OCR B 4.3.1(h)). Cut open the root nodules of a legume (clover, pea): a pink colour shows leghaemoglobin, which binds oxygen so that nitrogenase (which oxygen inactivates) can work. *Rhizobium* can be cultured from a crushed, surface-sterilised nodule on a yeast-mannitol agar plate using aseptic technique (file 28). Legumes are used in crop rotation because their nodules add nitrogen compounds to the soil.

**Mycorrhizae.** Associations between fungi and plant roots. The fungal hyphae spread through the soil, greatly increasing the surface area for absorbing water and ions, especially phosphate. They also store water. The fungus receives sugars from the plant. This is a mutualistic relationship.

**The phosphorus cycle.** Phosphorus is needed for ATP, nucleic acids and phospholipids. It has no gas phase.

1. Phosphate ions are released from rocks by weathering and dissolve in water.
2. Plants absorb phosphate (helped by mycorrhizae); animals get it by eating plants.
3. Saprobionts release phosphate from dead organisms and waste (also guano from seabirds).
4. Phosphate washed into lakes and seas settles into sediments, which over geological time form new rock, later uplifted.

The cycle is slow, so phosphate is often a limiting nutrient.

**The carbon cycle** (OCR A, Edexcel A, Eduqas).

- CO₂ is removed from the air and water by photosynthesis, and dissolves in the oceans (some forming carbonate shells and rocks).
- Carbon passes through food chains as organic compounds.
- CO₂ returns by respiration of plants, animals and decomposers.
- Dead material that does not decompose (waterlogged, acidic or cold conditions) forms peat and, over millions of years, fossil fuels.
- Combustion of fossil fuels and wood releases CO₂.
- Deforestation reduces photosynthesis and, if trees are burned, releases CO₂.

Carbon sinks store carbon for long periods (oceans, forests, peat bogs). Edexcel A 5.21 asks how knowing the carbon cycle helps reduce atmospheric CO₂ (file 42).

**Fertilisers.** Harvesting crops and removing livestock takes nitrates and phosphates away, so they are not returned to the soil by decomposition. Fertilisers replace them.

- *Natural (organic):* manure, compost, sewage sludge. Release ions slowly as they decompose; add organic matter that improves soil structure; cheaper on farms with animals, but the exact ion content is unknown.
- *Artificial (inorganic):* mined or manufactured salts of nitrate, phosphate and potassium. Exact amounts are known; ions immediately available; but soluble, so easily leached.

**Leaching and eutrophication.**

1. Rain washes soluble nitrates (and some phosphates) out of the soil into rivers and lakes (leaching).
2. Nitrate is no longer limiting, so algae grow rapidly, forming an algal bloom on the surface.
3. The bloom blocks light from plants below, which die.
4. Saprobiotic bacteria decompose the dead plants and algae, and their numbers increase.
5. The bacteria respire aerobically and use up the dissolved oxygen.
6. Fish and other aerobic organisms die.

Reduce it by: applying fertiliser in the right amounts at the right time (not before heavy rain); leaving buffer strips by water; using manure; treating sewage.

#### Worked example

A farmer's field loses 120 kg of nitrogen per hectare per year in harvested crops. Ammonium nitrate (NH₄NO₃) is 35% nitrogen by mass. How much fertiliser is needed per hectare to replace the loss?

120 ÷ 0.35 = 343 kg ha⁻¹ (3 s.f.).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying nitrifying bacteria fix nitrogen.
- Saying decomposers release nitrate directly. They release ammonium (ammonification); nitrifying bacteria make nitrate.
- Saying plants absorb nitrogen gas.
- In eutrophication, saying "the algae use up the oxygen" or "fish die because of the algae". The bacteria decomposing dead material use the oxygen.
- Saying phosphorus cycles through the atmosphere.

## Populations in ecosystems, succession and conservation

Populations of different species live together in ecosystems, and their sizes are set by biotic and abiotic factors. This file covers carrying capacity, competition and predation, estimating population size, succession, and conservation, with AQA required practical 12.

### 3.7.4 Populations in ecosystems

*Refs: AQA 3.7.4, RP12 · Edexcel A 4.3, 4.16, 5.1 to 5.4, CP10 · Edexcel B 3.3 ii, iii, 10.1 i, iv, v, 10.3 i, ii, CP15, CP16 · OCR A 4.2.1(b), (h), 6.3.1(a), (d), (e), 6.3.2(a) to (c) · OCR B 4.3.1(m) · Eduqas C1.5(a) to (e), (g), (i), (j), C1.6(b). Level: A-level only.*

#### What you need to know

- Populations of different species form a community. A community and the non-living components of its environment together form an ecosystem. Ecosystems can range in size from the very small to the very large.
- Within a habitat, a species occupies a niche governed by adaptation to both biotic and abiotic conditions.
- An ecosystem supports a certain size of population of a species, called the carrying capacity. This population size can vary as a result of: the effect of abiotic factors; interactions between organisms: interspecific and intraspecific competition and predation.
- The size of a population can be estimated using: randomly placed quadrats, or quadrats along a belt transect, for slow-moving or non-motile organisms; the mark-release-recapture method for motile organisms. The assumptions made when using the mark-release-recapture method.
- Ecosystems are dynamic and are subject to change over time. The concept of succession from pioneer species to climax community. At each stage in succession, certain species may be recognised that change the environment so that it becomes more suitable for other species with different adaptations. The new species may change the environment in such a way that it becomes less suitable for the previous species. Changes that organisms produce in their abiotic environment can result in a less hostile environment and change biodiversity.
- Conservation of habitats frequently involves management of succession.
- Evaluate evidence and data concerning issues relating to the conservation of species and habitats and consider conflicting evidence. Use given data to calculate the size of a population estimated using the mark-release-recapture method.

#### Explained

**Terms.**

- *Population:* all the organisms of one species in an area at one time.
- *Community:* all the populations of different species in an area.
- *Ecosystem:* a community plus the abiotic (non-living) parts of its environment.
- *Habitat:* the place where an organism lives.
- *Niche:* the role of a species in its habitat, including its interactions with other species and the abiotic conditions it needs. Two species cannot occupy the same niche in the same habitat indefinitely.

**Carrying capacity.** The largest population an environment can support long term. A population introduced to a new area grows slowly at first (lag phase: few individuals; adjusting), then rapidly (log or exponential phase: plenty of resources), then levels off (stationary phase) as resources, predators or disease limit it around the carrying capacity. (Eduqas, OCR A.)

**Abiotic factors:** temperature, light, pH, water availability, mineral ions, oxygen, wind, soil type. Each species has an optimum range.

**Biotic factors.**

- *Intraspecific competition:* within a species. When resources are short, the population falls; with less competition it rises again; numbers fluctuate around the carrying capacity.
- *Interspecific competition:* between species for the same resource. If niches overlap, one species may outcompete the other (red squirrels replaced by grey squirrels in most of the UK).
- *Predation:* predator and prey populations cycle. Prey increase → more food for predators, so predators increase → prey eaten, so prey decrease → predators starve, so predators decrease → prey recover. Predator peaks lag behind prey peaks. Real cycles are also affected by food supply and disease.

**Density-dependent and density-independent factors** (Eduqas). Density-dependent factors have a bigger effect as the population gets denser: competition, predation, disease, parasitism. Density-independent factors affect a population whatever its density: floods, fire, extreme cold.

**Estimating population size.**

*Quadrats* (for plants and slow-moving animals):

1. Use random coordinates (two tape measures at right angles; random number generator) to place quadrats.
2. Record abundance in each: count individuals (density), estimate percentage cover, or record presence in a point frame or gridded quadrat (frequency).
3. Use enough quadrats that the running mean levels off.
4. Population estimate = mean number per quadrat × (area of habitat ÷ area of one quadrat).

*Transects* (when conditions change across an area, such as from a hedge into a field, or down a seashore):

- Line transect: record species touching the line at regular intervals.
- Belt transect: place quadrats at regular intervals along the line (continuous or interrupted).

*Mark-release-recapture* (for mobile animals):

1. Capture a sample, count them (M), mark them harmlessly (paint dot under the shell), release.
2. After time to mix back in, capture a second sample; count the total (C) and how many are marked (R).
3. Population estimate N = (M × C) ÷ R.

Assumptions: marked individuals mix fully back into the population; the mark does not affect survival (not more visible to predators) and does not rub off; no births, deaths, immigration or emigration between samples; the population is in a defined area.

**Sampling methods and techniques** (Edexcel B 10.1 iv, v, CP15; OCR A 4.2.1(b)). Choose the method for the organism and habitat: kick sampling and nets for freshwater invertebrates; pitfall traps for ground insects; pooters; sweep nets; Tullgren funnels for soil invertebrates; light traps for moths. Measure abiotic factors with light meters, thermometers, pH probes, oxygen probes. Random sampling avoids bias; systematic sampling along a transect shows patterns; stratified sampling covers each part of a habitat in proportion. More samples give a more representative estimate.

**Succession.** The change in a community over time.

*Primary succession* starts on bare ground with no soil (bare rock, sand dunes, cooled lava):

1. **Pioneer species** colonise: lichens, mosses, algae. Adaptations: spores or seeds spread by wind, tolerance of extreme conditions, ability to fix nitrogen, rapid germination, photosynthesis.
2. Pioneers break down rock and, when they die and decompose, form a thin soil with some organic matter (humus).
3. Grasses and small plants can now grow. The soil deepens, holds more water and more nutrients.
4. Shrubs, then trees, colonise. Each stage changes the abiotic environment (more shade, deeper soil, more shelter) in ways that suit new species and may make it less suitable for the previous ones, which are outcompeted.
5. A stable **climax community** forms (in the UK, usually deciduous woodland, such as oak).

As succession proceeds: biomass increases; the number of species (biodiversity) usually increases; conditions become less hostile; food webs become more complex.

*Secondary succession* starts where soil is already present but vegetation has been removed (after fire, ploughing or felling). It is faster.

*Deflected succession / plagioclimax:* human activity (grazing, mowing, burning) stops succession at an earlier stage. Heather moorland is kept by controlled burning; chalk grassland by sheep grazing.

**Conservation.**

- *Conservation* means active management to maintain or increase biodiversity, and allows sustainable human use. *Preservation* (OCR A) means protecting an area from any human interference.
- *Managing succession:* conserving a habitat at an early stage (heathland, meadow) means preventing the climax: grazing, mowing, cutting back scrub, controlled burning.
- *In situ conservation:* protecting species in their natural habitat — national parks, nature reserves, marine conservation zones, controlling invasive species, legal protection.
- *Ex situ conservation:* outside the natural habitat — zoos with captive breeding programmes (keeping studbooks to avoid inbreeding, reintroducing animals to the wild), botanic gardens and seed banks (seeds dried and frozen; tested regularly for viability; such as the Millennium Seed Bank). Advantages: protects species from immediate threats; seeds store many alleles in little space. Disadvantages: small captive populations lose genetic diversity; animals may lose natural behaviours; reintroduction fails if the habitat is still damaged; expensive.

**Conflicting evidence.** Conservation decisions balance biological evidence with economic and social needs. When evaluating data, consider sample size, how the data were collected, whether a correlation shows cause, and who funded or published the study.

#### Worked example

1. In a first sample, 40 woodlice were caught and marked. In the second sample, 50 were caught, 8 of them marked. Estimate the population.

N = (40 × 50) ÷ 8 = 250.

2. Ten 0.25 m² quadrats in a 200 m² meadow contained a total of 36 daisies. Estimate the number of daisies in the meadow.

Mean per quadrat = 3.6; per m² = 3.6 ÷ 0.25 = 14.4; total = 14.4 × 200 = 2880.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the climax community "is reached when no more species can live there".
- Saying pioneers "make soil" without saying they break down rock and decompose to add organic matter.
- Mixing up population, community and ecosystem.
- In mark-release-recapture, assuming marking "has no effect" without saying what effects it must not have.
- Placing quadrats where there is "the most" of a plant.

#### Practical

**AQA required practical 12** (also Edexcel A CP10, Edexcel B CP16, OCR A PAG3 and 6.3.1(e)): investigation into the effect of a named environmental factor on the distribution of a given species.

Example: the effect of light intensity on the distribution of ivy from the edge of a wood into its interior.

1. Lay a tape measure from the open field into the wood (a belt transect).
2. Place a quadrat at regular intervals (every 2 m).
3. In each, estimate percentage cover of ivy and measure light intensity with a light meter at the same height and time of day.
4. Repeat with several transects.
5. Plot percentage cover against light intensity and use Spearman's rank correlation coefficient to test for a correlation (file 26).

Control variables: same time of day; same weather; same person estimating cover. Risks: uneven ground, stinging plants, ticks; work in pairs; wash hands. Limitations: other factors (soil moisture, pH) also vary along the transect, so a correlation does not show light is the cause.

### Climate change: evidence, causes and models

*Refs: Edexcel A 5.12 to 5.14, 5.18, 5.20, 5.21 · Eduqas C1.5(m). Level: A-level only.*

#### What you need to know

- The different types of evidence for climate change and its causes: records of carbon dioxide concentration (ppm) and temperature, pollen in peat bogs, dendrochronology.
- The causes of anthropogenic climate change: the role of greenhouse gases (CO₂ and methane) in the greenhouse effect.
- That data can be extrapolated to make predictions, and that these are used in models of future climate change; the limitations of models.
- How scientific conclusions about controversial issues can be affected by the people and organisations making them.
- How knowledge of the carbon cycle can be applied to reducing atmospheric CO₂.

#### Explained

**Evidence for climate change.**

- *Temperature records:* thermometer records since the 1850s show a rise of about 1.2 °C in global mean temperature. Earlier records were less reliable (fewer places, different methods).
- *Dendrochronology:* trees in temperate areas lay down one growth ring per year; wide rings mean good growing conditions (warm, wet). Ring widths of old trees and timbers give a record of past climate.
- *Pollen in peat bogs:* peat forms in waterlogged, acidic conditions where decomposition is slow, so pollen grains are preserved in layers. Each species' pollen has a distinctive shape. The species present in each layer show the climate when that layer formed (different plants grow in different climates); deeper layers are older (dated by radiocarbon).
- *Ice cores:* bubbles of air trapped in Antarctic and Greenland ice give past CO₂ and methane concentrations; the ratio of oxygen isotopes in the ice gives past temperature. Records go back about 800 000 years and show CO₂ and temperature rising and falling together.
- *CO₂ records:* direct measurements since 1958 (Mauna Loa, Hawaii) show CO₂ rising from about 315 ppm to over 420 ppm, with a seasonal wave (falls in the northern summer when plants photosynthesise more).

**The greenhouse effect.** Short-wave radiation from the Sun passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth's surface, which emits longer-wave infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases (CO₂, methane, water vapour, nitrous oxide) absorb and re-emit some of this infrared, keeping the lower atmosphere warm. Without it the Earth would be about 33 °C colder. Increasing greenhouse gases enhance the effect, so temperatures rise: **anthropogenic** (human-caused) climate change.

- CO₂: burning fossil fuels; deforestation (less photosynthesis; burning trees); cement making.
- Methane: cattle and other ruminants (gut microorganisms); rice paddies; landfill; leaks from gas extraction; melting permafrost releases more (positive feedback). Methane is a much more powerful greenhouse gas per molecule than CO₂ but lasts a shorter time.

**Models and extrapolation.** Scientists extrapolate trends and use computer models that include many factors (emissions, oceans, clouds, ice) to predict future climate under different emission scenarios. Limitations: data from the past may not hold in future; future emissions depend on human choices; some processes (clouds, feedbacks from ice and permafrost) are not fully understood; extrapolating beyond the data range adds uncertainty. Models are tested by checking how well they reproduce past climate.

**Controversy and the scientific community** (Edexcel A 5.18, 5.20). Conclusions about controversial issues can be affected by who funds or publishes the research (oil companies, environmental groups, governments), by selective use of data, or by media reporting. Peer review, publication of methods and data, repetition by independent scientists, and large reviews of evidence (the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, IPCC) increase confidence. Decision-makers balance scientific evidence with economic and social factors.

**Reducing atmospheric CO₂** (Edexcel A 5.21, 5.22).

- Use renewable energy and reduce energy use.
- **Biofuels** (bioethanol from fermenting sugar cane or maize starch; biodiesel from plant oils): carbon released when burned was recently absorbed by the plants, so they are close to carbon-neutral (but growing, processing and transporting them uses energy; they may compete with food crops).
- **Reforestation:** growing trees take up CO₂ by photosynthesis and store carbon in wood. Sustainable forestry (replanting as trees are felled) and protecting peat bogs keep carbon stored.
- Carbon capture and storage.

#### Worked example

Atmospheric CO₂ was 340 ppm in 1980 and 412 ppm in 2020. Assuming a linear trend, predict the concentration in 2050. Rate = 72 ÷ 40 = 1.8 ppm per year; 2050: 412 + 30 × 1.8 = 466 ppm. Limitation: the rate of increase has itself been rising, so a linear extrapolation probably underestimates, and future emissions may change.

### Effects of climate change on organisms

*Refs: Edexcel A 5.15, 5.16, CP13 · Edexcel B 10.4 i. Level: A-level only.*

#### What you need to know

- The effects of climate change (changing rainfall patterns and changes in seasonal cycles) on plants and animals (distribution of species, development and life cycles).
- The effect of temperature on enzyme activity and its impact on plants, animals and microorganisms, including Q10.
- Practical: effects of temperature on the development of organisms.

#### Explained

- **Distribution:** species shift to cooler areas (towards the poles, or higher up mountains). Species that cannot move or that are already at the top of mountains or on islands may become extinct. Pests and disease vectors (mosquitoes) spread into new areas.
- **Seasonal cycles (phenology):** spring events happen earlier (leaves opening, flowering, egg-laying, insect emergence). If a predator's breeding no longer matches its prey's peak (birds and caterpillars), numbers fall.
- **Rainfall:** droughts and floods change habitats; drought reduces photosynthesis and crop yields.
- **Development:** in ectotherms, development rate depends on temperature; sex of some reptiles (turtles) is set by nest temperature, so warming changes sex ratios.
- **Oceans:** warmer water holds less oxygen; coral bleaching; more CO₂ dissolves, lowering pH (ocean acidification), which harms shell-forming organisms.

**Enzymes and Q10** (Edexcel A 5.16). Within limits, a 10 °C rise roughly doubles enzyme-controlled rates (Q10 ≈ 2; file 02). Warming speeds the metabolism of ectotherms and microorganisms (faster decomposition, releasing more CO₂). Above the optimum, enzymes denature. Rising temperature may favour some species and harm others.

**Evolution in response** (Edexcel A 5.17). Climate change is a selection pressure: individuals with alleles that suit the new conditions are more likely to survive and reproduce, so allele frequencies change. Species with short generations and genetic diversity are more likely to adapt; others may not adapt fast enough.

#### Practical

**CP13: investigate the effects of temperature on the development of organisms** (such as seedling growth rate, or brine shrimp hatch rate).

1. Place equal numbers of brine shrimp eggs in equal volumes of salt water at a range of temperatures (water baths or incubators, for example 15, 20, 25, 30 °C).
2. Every few hours count the number of hatched larvae (use a light: larvae swim towards it), or measure seedling height each day.
3. Plot percentage hatched (or growth) against time for each temperature; compare rates; calculate Q10.

Control variables: salt concentration, light, volume, number of eggs, pH. Ethics: brine shrimp are invertebrates, but handle humanely.

### Biodiversity, conservation and sustainable management

*Refs: Edexcel A 4.1, 4.16 · Edexcel B 3.3 ii, iii, 10.4 i to iii · OCR A 4.2.1(f) to (i), 6.3.2(c) to (e) · Eduqas C1.6(a) to (d), (f). Level: AS & A (Edexcel A Topic 4, Edexcel B 3.3, OCR A 4.2.1); A-level only (rest).*

#### What you need to know

- The factors affecting biodiversity and why species become endangered or extinct.
- The ecological, economic and aesthetic reasons for maintaining biodiversity.
- In situ and ex situ conservation (zoos, seed banks); international and local agreements.
- How ecosystems are managed to balance conservation with human needs; sustainability.

#### Explained

**Why biodiversity is falling** (OCR A 4.2.1(f), Eduqas C1.6(a)).

- **Human population growth** increases demand for land, food, water and energy.
- **Habitat loss and fragmentation:** deforestation, farming, urban development, draining wetlands.
- **Monoculture** farming; pesticides and herbicides.
- **Over-exploitation:** overfishing, hunting, logging, trade in animals and plants.
- **Pollution:** fertilisers (eutrophication), plastics, pesticides.
- **Invasive species** introduced by humans outcompete or prey on native species.
- **Climate change.**

Small populations are vulnerable: low genetic diversity, inbreeding, chance events.

**Reasons to maintain biodiversity** (OCR A 4.2.1(g), Edexcel B 3.3 ii).

- *Ecological:* species depend on each other (pollinators, food webs, nutrient cycling); keystone species have large effects; diverse ecosystems are more stable and recover better from change.
- *Economic (ecosystem services):* food, timber, fibres, fuel; medicines (file 41); genes for crop breeding (wild relatives with disease resistance); pollination of crops; soil formation; water purification; flood control; carbon storage; ecotourism.
- *Aesthetic and ethical:* enjoyment of nature and its benefits to mental health; the view that species have a right to exist and that we have a duty to future generations.

**Conservation and preservation.** Conservation is active management, allowing sustainable use; preservation protects an area from use. In situ and ex situ methods, zoos and seed banks are described in file 23. Eduqas C1.6(b): gene pools are conserved in the wild (protected habitats, wildlife corridors joining fragmented habitats) and in captivity (captive breeding with studbooks; frozen gametes and embryos; seed banks).

**Agreements** (OCR A 4.2.1(i), Edexcel B 10.4 ii).

- **CITES** (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species, 1973): bans international trade in the most endangered species (Appendix I) and controls trade in others with permits. It has reduced trade in, for example, ivory and some parrots, but illegal trade continues where enforcement is weak and demand is high.
- **Convention on Biological Diversity** (Rio, 1992): countries agree to conserve biodiversity, use it sustainably, and share the benefits of genetic resources fairly (including medicines from plants found in developing countries).
- **Countryside Stewardship Scheme** (UK; now Environmental Land Management schemes): pays farmers to manage land to benefit wildlife (hedgerows, field margins, wetlands).

**Sustainable management** (OCR A 6.3.2(d), (e); Edexcel B 10.4 iii). Sustainability means meeting present needs without compromising future generations.

- *Timber:* **coppicing** (cutting trees to near ground level so new shoots grow; harvested in rotation) and **pollarding** (cutting higher, out of reach of grazing animals); selective felling; replanting; leaving buffer zones.
- *Fishing:* quotas; minimum mesh sizes so young fish escape; closed seasons and no-take marine reserves; fish farming (with problems of disease, pollution and wild fish used as feed).

**Managed ecosystems** (OCR A 6.3.2(e)). Each balances human needs with conservation:

- **Galápagos Islands:** invasive goats, rats and plants threaten endemic species; tourism and fishing pressure. Management: national park and marine reserve, eradicating goats, controlling visitors and fishing.
- **Masai Mara** (Kenya): conflict between wildlife and the Maasai people's cattle grazing and farming. Conservancies pay local people from tourism; managed grazing.
- **Terai region** (Nepal and India): forest and grassland with tigers and rhinos; deforestation and conflict with farmers. Community forestry; wildlife corridors.
- **Peak District** and **Snowdonia** (UK): national parks with farming, quarrying, tourism and moorland conservation; managed by grazing, burning and footpath management.
- **Antarctica:** fishing and tourism; protected by the Antarctic Treaty, with no mining and controlled fishing.

**Agriculture versus conservation** (Eduqas C1.6(c)). Intensive farming increases food production but reduces biodiversity (file 14). Compromises include wildlife-friendly farming, integrated pest management and setting aside land.

**Planetary boundaries** (Eduqas C1.6(f)). A framework that sets limits for nine Earth systems within which humanity can safely operate: climate change, biodiversity loss (biosphere integrity), nitrogen and phosphorus cycles, land-system change, freshwater use, ocean acidification, ozone depletion, atmospheric aerosols, and novel entities (such as plastics and chemicals). Several boundaries, including climate change, biodiversity and the nitrogen cycle, are already thought to have been crossed. Political decisions should be informed by evidence (Eduqas C1.6(e)).

### Human population, crops and food security

*Refs: OCR B 4.3.1(l), 4.3.2(a) to (d) · Eduqas C1.6(d). Level: A-level only.*

#### What you need to know

- Farms as ecosystems; the conflict between agriculture and conservation.
- The factors that alter birth and death rates; the effect of human population growth on biodiversity.
- The ecological, economic and scientific reasons for conservation.
- Global food security.

#### Explained

**Farms as ecosystems** (OCR B 4.3.1(l)). A farm is an ecosystem managed to channel as much energy as possible into crops and livestock: weeds, pests and competitors are removed and nutrients are added. This conflicts with conservation, because hedgerow removal, monocultures, pesticides, fertilisers and drainage reduce biodiversity (file 14). Compromises: keeping hedgerows and field margins, agri-environment payments, integrated pest management, set-aside land and wildlife-friendly farming. The compensation point and crop yields are in file 15; deflected succession through land management in file 23.

**Population growth** (OCR B 4.3.2(a), (b)). Human population grows when birth rate exceeds death rate. **Food security** (OCR B 4.3.2(d)). Food security exists when everyone has access to enough safe, nutritious food. Threats: growing population; climate change; soil degradation; water shortages; pests and disease; conflict; poverty; waste (about a third of food is wasted). Solutions: higher-yield and more resilient crops (including GM); reducing waste; sustainable farming; fair trade; alternative proteins.

### OZ Gas concentrations, ozone and radicals

*Refs: OCR B OZ(i), OZ(q), OZ(r), OZ(s). Level: AS & A.*

#### What you need to know

- Calculate percentage by volume and parts per million (ppm) for gases.
- Ozone forms and breaks down naturally in the stratosphere, absorbing high-energy UV.
- In the troposphere, ozone is a pollutant that contributes to photochemical smog.
- Haloalkanes release halogen radicals by photodissociation; the ease depends on C–X bond enthalpy (C–I easiest, C–F hardest).
- Chlorine radicals catalyse ozone breakdown; other radicals (NO•, HO•) do too.
- The Sun emits mainly UV, visible and some IR; the Earth radiates IR.

#### Explained

**Gas composition.** For gases, volume percentage = mole percentage. ppm = (volume of component ÷ total volume) × 10⁶. Air is about 78% N₂, 21% O₂, 0.9% Ar, and about 0.04% (420 ppm) CO₂.

**Ozone in the stratosphere** (about 10 to 50 km up).

- Formation: O₂ + UV → 2O; O + O₂ + M → O₃ + M (M is any third molecule that carries away energy).
- Breakdown: O₃ + UV → O₂ + O; O + O₃ → 2O₂.

These reactions absorb high-energy UV (UV-B and UV-C), acting as a sunscreen. UV-B causes sunburn, skin cancer and cataracts, and damages crops and plankton.

**Ozone depletion.** Halogen radicals formed when UV breaks C–X bonds in haloalkanes catalyse ozone breakdown:

CF₂Cl₂ → •CF₂Cl + Cl•
Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂
ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂
Overall: O₃ + O → 2O₂

**Ease of photodissociation.** The C–X bond enthalpy falls from C–F to C–I. C–F bonds are too strong to be broken by the UV that reaches the stratosphere, so fluoroalkanes (HFCs) do not deplete ozone. C–Cl bonds break, so CFCs do. C–Br and C–I break even more easily, but iodo- and bromoalkanes break down lower in the atmosphere before reaching the stratosphere; bromine from halons is still very damaging.

**Ozone in the troposphere.** Near the ground ozone is a pollutant: it irritates eyes and lungs and damages plants. It forms when sunlight breaks NO₂ (from vehicles): NO₂ → NO + O; O + O₂ → O₃. With unburnt hydrocarbons, this produces photochemical smog.

**Radiation from the Sun and Earth.** The Sun, being very hot, emits mostly visible light with some UV and IR. The Earth's surface, being much cooler, emits IR.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying ozone is always harmful or always useful. It depends where it is.
- Saying CFCs deplete ozone because C–F bonds break.

### O(n) The greenhouse effect

*Refs: OCR A 4.2.4(b) · OCR B O(n). Level: AS & A (OCR B O(n) A-level).*

#### What you need to know

- Solar energy reaches the Earth mainly as visible and UV radiation; the Earth warms and radiates IR.
- Greenhouse gases absorb some of this IR, increasing the vibrational energy of their bonds.
- They pass the energy to other molecules by collisions (raising the temperature) and re-emit IR in all directions, some back to Earth.
- Bonds such as C=O, O–H and C–H absorb IR; CO₂, H₂O and CH₄ are greenhouse gases. N₂ and O₂ are not.
- Human activity has increased CO₂ and CH₄ levels, enhancing the greenhouse effect; this links to global warming and climate change.

#### Explained

A molecule absorbs IR only if its vibration changes its dipole. N₂ and O₂ are symmetrical diatomics with no change of dipole when they stretch, so they do not absorb IR. CO₂ (asymmetric stretch and bending), H₂O and CH₄ do.

How much a gas contributes depends on: how well each molecule absorbs IR, how much of the gas is present, and how long it stays in the atmosphere (residence time). Methane is a much stronger absorber per molecule than CO₂ but is present at much lower concentration and lasts about 12 years; CO₂ lasts for centuries.

Some IR wavelengths are not absorbed by water vapour (the "IR window"); CO₂ and CH₄ absorb in parts of this window, which is why rising levels matter.

**Responses.** International agreements (the Kyoto Protocol, the Paris Agreement) set targets to cut emissions. Approaches include renewable energy, carbon capture and storage (pumping CO₂ into old oil fields or deep rock, or reacting it with metal oxides to form carbonates), and more efficient processes.

**Ocean acidification (OCR B Oceans context).** The oceans absorb CO₂: CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq); CO₂(aq) + H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻; HCO₃⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + CO₃²⁻. More CO₂ makes the sea slightly more acidic, and H⁺ removes CO₃²⁻ ions, making it harder for shellfish and corals to build calcium carbonate shells. The HCO₃⁻/CO₃²⁻ system also buffers the oceans (file 11).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying greenhouse gases "trap heat" without explaining absorption of IR by bond vibrations.
- Confusing the greenhouse effect with the hole in the ozone layer.

### C2.4 The wider impact of chemistry and green chemistry

*Refs: Eduqas C2.4(a), (b) · OCR B WM(g). Level: AS & A.*

#### What you need to know

- Chemical industry and energy production have social, economic and environmental impacts.
- Principles of green chemistry and how they improve sustainability; apply them to given processes.

#### Explained

**Impacts.** Chemical industry provides medicines, fertilisers, materials and fuels, and employs many people. It also uses finite resources and energy, and can release waste and pollutants. Burning fossil fuels for energy releases CO₂, SO₂, NOₓ and particulates.

**Principles of green chemistry** (summarised; apply them to the information given):

1. Prevent waste rather than treat it afterwards.
2. Use reactions with high atom economy.
3. Use and make substances with low toxicity.
4. Use safer solvents (water, supercritical CO₂) or none.
5. Use less energy: lower temperatures and pressures, and catalysts.
6. Use renewable raw materials (feedstocks from plants).
7. Avoid unnecessary steps such as protecting groups.
8. Use catalysts rather than reagents used in large amounts.
9. Design products that break down after use (biodegradable).
10. Monitor processes to prevent pollution.
11. Design processes to reduce the risk of accidents.

**Examples.** Ibuprofen's industrial route was shortened from six steps to three, raising atom economy from about 40% to almost 80%. Supercritical CO₂ replaces organic solvents for decaffeinating coffee. Enzymes catalyse reactions at body temperature in water.

**Sustainability** means meeting present needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs.

#### Common mistakes

- Treating percentage yield and atom economy as the same thing.
- Calling a process "green" just because it uses a catalyst, without considering raw materials, energy and waste.

### 3.8.1.1 Rutherford scattering

*Refs: AQA 3.8.1.1 · Edexcel 131 · OCR A 6.4.1(a), (c) · OCR B 6.2.1(a)(ii), (b)(ii), (c)(ii) · Eduqas C3.7(a), (b). Level: A-level only (Eduqas AS & A).*

#### What you need to know

- Rutherford scattering, qualitatively.
- How understanding of the structure of the atom changed over time.
- (Eduqas C3.7(b)) Estimating the maximum Coulomb force on an alpha particle in the plum-pudding and nuclear models.

#### Explained

**The experiment** (Geiger and Marsden, 1909). A narrow beam of alpha particles from a radium source was aimed at a very thin gold foil in a vacuum. A zinc sulfide screen viewed through a microscope detected the alpha particles at different angles (each one made a tiny flash).

**Observations and conclusions.**

| Observation | Conclusion |
| --- | --- |
| Most alpha particles went straight through | Most of the atom is empty space |
| Some were deflected through small angles | There is a concentrated positive charge that repels them |
| A very few (about 1 in 8000) bounced back through more than 90° | The positive charge and most of the mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus |

**History.** Thomson's plum-pudding model (1904) had positive charge spread through the atom with electrons dotted in it. In that model the electric field inside the atom is weak (the charge is spread out), so alphas could only be deflected slightly. Rutherford's nuclear model (1911) explained large-angle scattering. Later: Bohr added electron energy levels (1913); Chadwick discovered the neutron (1932).

**Eduqas estimate.** Maximum Coulomb force on an alpha (charge 2e) from a gold atom's charge (79e):

- Plum pudding: charge spread over the atom (r ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m); maximum force at the surface: F = k(2e)(79e)/r² ≈ 3.6 × 10⁻⁶ N. Too small to reverse a fast alpha.
- Nuclear: all the charge within about 10⁻¹⁴ m: F ≈ 3.6 × 10² N, 10⁸ times larger, enough to turn it back.

**Path of an alpha** (OCR B 6.2.1(b)(ii)). Alphas follow hyperbolic paths around the nucleus. The closer the original line of travel is to the nucleus, the bigger the deflection; a head-on alpha comes straight back.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the alpha particles hit electrons and bounced back.
- Saying "most went through, so the nucleus is small" (this shows the atom is mostly empty; the large-angle back-scattering shows the nucleus is small, dense and positive).

### 3.8.1.2 α, β and γ radiation

*Refs: AQA 3.8.1.2, RP12 · Edexcel 168, 169, 170, 171, CP15 · OCR A 6.4.3(a), (b), (c) · OCR B 6.2.2(a)(i), (b)(i), (c)(ii), (c)(iii), (d)(i) · Eduqas C3.6(a), (b), (c). Level: A-level only.*

#### What you need to know

- Properties of alpha, beta and gamma radiation and how to identify them with simple absorption experiments.
- Applications: relative hazards to humans, thickness monitoring (aluminium foil, paper, steel), safe handling.
- The inverse-square law for gamma radiation: I = k/x². Verifying it experimentally.
- Background radiation: sources and correcting for it.
- Balancing risks and benefits of radiation in medicine.
- (OCR B) Absorbed dose (Gy) and effective dose (Sv).

#### Explained

| | Alpha α | Beta-minus β⁻ | Gamma γ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nature | Helium nucleus ⁴₂He | Fast electron | EM radiation (photon) |
| Charge | +2e | −e | 0 |
| Speed | About 0.05c | Up to about 0.99c | c |
| Ionising | Strongly (about 10⁴ ion pairs per cm in air) | Moderately (about 10² per cm) | Weakly (about 1 per cm) |
| Range in air | A few cm (fixed range for each source) | About 1 m (spread of ranges) | Very large; intensity falls as 1/x² |
| Stopped by | Paper or skin | A few mm of aluminium | Reduced by several cm of lead or metres of concrete (never fully stopped) |
| Deflected in fields | Yes, slightly (heavy) | Yes, strongly, opposite way to α | No |

(β⁺ particles are positrons; they annihilate almost at once with an electron, producing two gamma photons.)

**Why they differ.** Alpha particles are big, slow and doubly charged, so they interact strongly with every atom they pass, ionising heavily and losing energy quickly. Gamma photons are uncharged and interact only occasionally.

**Identifying radiation.** Use a Geiger–Müller tube and counter close to the source. Measure the count rate with nothing in between, then with paper, then with 3 mm of aluminium, then with lead. Correct each count for background. A drop with paper shows α; a further drop with aluminium shows β; what remains through aluminium (and is reduced by lead) is γ.

**Hazards.** Outside the body, alpha is the least dangerous (it cannot penetrate skin), gamma the most. Inside the body (swallowed or breathed in), alpha is the most dangerous because all its energy is deposited in a small volume of tissue, damaging cells and DNA.

**Safe handling.** Use long-handled tongs; never point a source at anyone; keep the time of exposure short; keep as far away as possible (inverse-square law for gamma); use shielding (lead-lined boxes); store sources in lead-lined containers; wash hands after.

**Thickness monitoring.** A source on one side of a sheet and a detector on the other. If the count rate changes, the thickness has changed, and rollers are adjusted. Use β for paper and thin aluminium foil (α would be stopped completely; γ would pass with almost no change). Use γ for steel sheet.

**Inverse-square law for gamma.** A point source emits gamma photons equally in all directions; the energy spreads over a sphere of area 4πx², so intensity (and count rate) I = k/x². (Alpha and beta are absorbed by air, so they do not follow this law.)

**Background radiation.** Always present. Sources: radon gas from rocks (the largest part in the UK), cosmic rays, rocks and buildings, food and drink (e.g. potassium-40), medical uses, nuclear industry and past weapons tests. Measure the background count rate (with no source, over a long time, e.g. 10 minutes) and subtract it from every reading.

**Dose** (OCR B 6.2.2(c)(ii), (iii)). Absorbed dose = energy absorbed per kilogram of tissue, in gray (1 Gy = 1 J kg⁻¹). Effective dose = absorbed dose × quality (weighting) factor, in sieverts (Sv). The quality factor is 1 for β and γ, 20 for α, because α does much more damage per joule. Risk of cancer rises roughly in proportion to effective dose (about 5% per Sv).

**Risks and benefits in medicine.** X-rays, CT scans and radioactive tracers give a dose that carries a small risk of cancer, so they are used only when the diagnostic or treatment benefit outweighs it, with the lowest dose possible.

#### Worked example

1. A gamma source gives a corrected count rate of 480 counts per minute at 0.20 m. Predict the count rate at 0.50 m.

   I ∝ 1/x²: 480 × (0.20/0.50)² = 77 counts per minute.

2. A 70 kg worker absorbs 2.1 mJ from alpha radiation. Find the absorbed and effective dose (quality factor 20).

   Absorbed dose = 2.1 × 10⁻³/70 = 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ Gy. Effective = 6.0 × 10⁻⁴ Sv (0.6 mSv).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying gamma radiation is completely stopped by lead.
- Saying alpha is the most dangerous in all cases.
- Forgetting to subtract background before testing the inverse-square law.
- Saying beta particles come from the electron shells (they come from the nucleus).

#### Practical

**AQA required practical 12** (also Eduqas specified practical): investigate the inverse-square law for gamma radiation.

1. Measure the background count with the GM tube for at least 5 minutes, with all sources away. Find the background count rate.
2. Place a sealed gamma source (cobalt-60) using tongs in a holder facing the GM tube along a metre rule.
3. Record the count over a fixed time (e.g. 2 minutes) at distances from about 5 cm to 1 m. Repeat and average.
4. Subtract the background rate to get the corrected count rate C.
5. The source is inside its holder and the GM tube detects some distance inside its window, so the true distance is x + d, where d is an unknown offset. Plot 1/√C against x: a straight line whose x-intercept gives −d. (Or plot C against 1/x² if d is negligible.)

Errors: radioactive decay is random, so count for longer at larger distances to reduce percentage uncertainty (uncertainty in a count N is about √N). Dead time of the GM tube at close range lowers counts. Safety: handle the source with tongs, keep it at arm's length, return it to its lead box promptly, do not point it at anyone, and keep exposure time short.

**Edexcel CP15** (also OCR B 6.2.2(d)(i)): absorption of gamma by lead. Place lead sheets of increasing thickness x between a gamma source and a GM tube at a fixed distance. Measure count rates, correct for background. The corrected count rate falls exponentially: C = C₀e^(−μx), where μ is the linear attenuation (absorption) coefficient. Plot ln C against x: straight line, gradient −μ. Half-value thickness = ln 2/μ.

### 3.8.1.3 Radioactive decay

*Refs: AQA 3.8.1.3 · Edexcel 168, 172, 173 · OCR A 6.4.3(a), (d) to (h) · OCR B 5.1.1(a)(iv), (b)(ii), (iv), (v), (c)(i), (ii), (d)(iv), 6.2.2(c)(i), (d)(ii) · Eduqas C3.6(a), (d) to (h). Level: A-level only.*

#### What you need to know

- Decay is random and spontaneous; each nucleus has a constant probability of decaying per unit time (the decay constant λ).
- ΔN/Δt = −λN; N = N₀e^(−λt); activity A = λN; A = A₀e^(−λt).
- Half-life T½ = ln 2/λ.
- Finding half-life from decay curves and log graphs.
- Questions may use molar mass and the Avogadro constant.
- Modelling with constant decay probability (dice, spreadsheets, iterative methods).
- Applications: storing radioactive waste, radioactive dating.

#### Explained

**Random and spontaneous.** It is impossible to predict when a particular nucleus will decay (random), and decay is not affected by external conditions such as temperature, pressure or chemical state (spontaneous). But for a large number of nuclei, the fraction that decays per second is constant: the decay constant λ (s⁻¹) is the probability that a given nucleus decays in one second.

**Activity** A is the number of decays per second, in becquerels (1 Bq = 1 decay per second). A = λN.

**Exponential decay.** The rate of decay is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei: dN/dt = −λN. Solution:

- N = N₀e^(−λt)
- A = A₀e^(−λt)
- (also count rate C = C₀e^(−λt), and mass of the parent remaining)

**Half-life** T½: the average time for the number of undecayed nuclei (or the activity) of an isotope to halve. Setting N = N₀/2 gives T½ = ln 2/λ ≈ 0.693/λ. After n half-lives, N = N₀/2ⁿ.

**Graphs.**

- N (or A) against t: exponential curve; read off T½ as the time to fall to half, then half again (check it is constant).
- ln N (or ln A) against t: straight line, ln N = ln N₀ − λt. Gradient = −λ.

**Using moles.** N = (mass/molar mass) × N_A. For example 1.0 μg of radium-226: N = (1.0 × 10⁻⁹ kg / 0.226 kg mol⁻¹) × 6.02 × 10²³ = 2.66 × 10¹⁵ nuclei.

**Modelling** (OCR A 6.4.3(f), (g), OCR B 5.1.1(c)(ii), Eduqas practical, AQA suggested).

- Dice: roll 100 dice; remove all that show a six (each has probability 1/6 of "decaying" per throw); count the remainder; repeat. A graph of number remaining against throw number is an exponential decay with a "half-life" of about 3.8 throws (ln 2/ln(6/5)).
- Spreadsheet: ΔN = −λNΔt each step; N_new = N − λNΔt.

**Applications.**

- *Radioactive dating* (OCR A 6.4.3(h)). Living things take in carbon, including a constant small fraction of carbon-14 (T½ = 5730 years). When they die, intake stops and ¹⁴C decays. Measuring the activity per gram of carbon and comparing with living material gives the age. Rocks are dated using uranium/lead or potassium/argon ratios.
- *Medical tracers*: short half-lives (hours) so the patient is not exposed for long, but long enough for the test.
- *Radioactive waste*: some isotopes have half-lives of thousands of years, so waste must be stored securely for very long times (file 19).

#### Worked example

1. Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8.0 days. Find λ, and the activity after 20 days of a sample with initial activity 4.0 × 10⁶ Bq.

   λ = 0.693/(8.0 × 86 400) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ s⁻¹. A = 4.0 × 10⁶ e^(−0.693 × 20/8.0) = 4.0 × 10⁶ × 0.177 = 7.1 × 10⁵ Bq.

2. Find the activity of 1.0 μg of radium-226 (T½ = 1600 years).

   λ = 0.693/(1600 × 3.16 × 10⁷) = 1.37 × 10⁻¹¹ s⁻¹. N = 2.66 × 10¹⁵. A = 3.6 × 10⁴ Bq.

3. Wood from an ancient boat has 0.60 of the ¹⁴C activity per gram of living wood. Find its age.

   0.60 = e^(−λt); t = −ln 0.60/λ = 0.511 × 5730/0.693 = 4200 years.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the half-life is half the time it takes for all the nuclei to decay.
- Using days for T½ but seconds for t (or the reverse).
- Saying that after two half-lives the sample has completely decayed.
- Forgetting to subtract background from count rates before finding half-life.

#### Practical

**Half-life of protactinium-234** (OCR B 5.1.1(d)(iv), OCR A). A sealed bottle contains a uranium salt in water and an organic solvent. Shaking separates protactinium-234 into the organic layer (on top). Place a GM tube next to the top layer and record counts every 10 s for about 5 minutes. Subtract background. Plot ln(count rate) against t: gradient = −λ; T½ ≈ 70 s. (Done by teachers or with simulations in many schools; the bottle must not be opened.)

### 3.8.1.4 Nuclear instability

*Refs: AQA 3.8.1.4 · Edexcel 170 · OCR A 6.4.3(c) · OCR B 6.2.2(a)(ii) · Eduqas C3.6(a). Level: A-level only.*

#### What you need to know

- The graph of N (neutron number) against Z (proton number) for stable nuclei.
- Decay modes of unstable nuclei: α, β⁺, β⁻ and electron capture.
- Changes in N and Z caused by each decay, in decay equations.
- Nuclear energy level diagrams; nuclear excited states and γ emission; technetium-99m in medical diagnosis.

#### Explained

**The N–Z plot.** Stable nuclei lie along a band. For light nuclei (Z < 20) the band follows N = Z. For heavier nuclei, more neutrons than protons are needed (N > Z) because the extra neutrons add strong-force attraction without adding electrostatic repulsion. Above Z = 83 no nucleus is fully stable.

**Decay modes and where they occur.**

| Mode | Where on the plot | Change | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| α | Very heavy nuclei (Z > 82) | Z − 2, N − 2, A − 4 | ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂α |
| β⁻ | Above the band (too many neutrons) | Z + 1, N − 1, A same | ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e + ν̄ₑ |
| β⁺ | Below the band (too many protons) | Z − 1, N + 1, A same | ¹¹₆C → ¹¹₅B + ⁰₊₁e + νₑ |
| Electron capture | Below the band (proton-rich; competes with β⁺) | Z − 1, N + 1 | ⁷₄Be + ⁰₋₁e → ⁷₃Li + νₑ |
| γ | Excited nucleus | No change in Z or N | ⁹⁹ᵐ₄₃Tc → ⁹⁹₄₃Tc + γ |

On an N–Z graph, α moves a nucleus 2 down and 2 left; β⁻ moves it 1 down and 1 right; β⁺ and electron capture move it 1 up and 1 left.

**Nuclear excited states and gamma.** After an α or β decay, the daughter nucleus is often left in an excited state (with extra energy). It drops to its ground state by emitting a gamma photon, whose energy equals the gap between nuclear energy levels (typically keV to MeV, much bigger than atomic levels). A nuclear energy level diagram shows parent and daughter levels with arrows for each decay.

**Technetium-99m** (the m means metastable). Made from molybdenum-99 (T½ = 66 h), which decays by β⁻ to Tc-99m. Tc-99m then emits a 140 keV gamma photon, with T½ = 6 hours. Ideal as a medical tracer: pure gamma (penetrates the body, little ionisation inside), easy to detect with a gamma camera, short half-life (low dose), long enough to do the scan, can be attached to many chemicals to target organs, and can be made on site in a hospital "generator".

**Nuclear valley** (OCR B). If binding energy is plotted against N and Z as a surface, stable nuclei sit along the floor of a valley. Nuclei on the valley sides decay "downhill" towards the floor by β⁻ or β⁺; very heavy nuclei at the far end decay by α.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying β⁻ decay reduces Z.
- Saying gamma emission changes the element.
- Drawing the stability band as N = Z for heavy nuclei.

### 3.8.1.5 Nuclear radius

*Refs: AQA 3.8.1.5 · OCR A 6.4.1(c), (f), (g) · Eduqas C3.7(b). Level: A-level only (Eduqas AS & A).*

#### What you need to know

- Estimating nuclear radius from the closest approach of alpha particles (using the Coulomb equation); finding it from electron diffraction.
- Typical values of nuclear radius (about 10⁻¹⁵ m).
- R = R₀A^(1/3), found from experiment; evidence that nuclear density is constant.
- Calculating nuclear density.
- The graph of intensity against angle for electron diffraction by nuclei.

#### Explained

**Closest approach.** An alpha particle fired head-on at a nucleus slows as it approaches and stops momentarily at distance r, where all its kinetic energy has become electric potential energy:

Ek = (2e)(Ze)/(4πε₀r) → r = 2Ze²/(4πε₀Ek)

This gives an upper limit for the nuclear radius (the alpha does not touch the nucleus). Limitations: the alpha's charge is spread out; the recoil of the nucleus is ignored; the strong force may act if it gets close enough.

**Electron diffraction.** High-energy electrons (hundreds of MeV) have de Broglie wavelengths about 10⁻¹⁵ m, similar to a nucleus. Fired at a thin film, they diffract around the nuclei. The intensity against angle falls from a central maximum to a first minimum (not zero) at θ, where sin θ ≈ 0.61λ/R. So R = 0.61λ/sin θ. Electrons are better than alphas because they are leptons (not affected by the strong force) and they probe the whole nucleus. At these energies, λ ≈ hc/E (relativistic).

**R = R₀A^(1/3).** Experiments show R₀ ≈ 1.2 fm (1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m; data booklet value 1.05 to 1.4 fm depending on method). Plotting R against A^(1/3) gives a straight line through the origin, gradient R₀. Or plot ln R against ln A: gradient 1/3.

**Constant density.** Volume = (4/3)πR³ = (4/3)πR₀³A, so volume ∝ A. Mass ≈ A × u. So density = Au/((4/3)πR₀³A) = 3u/(4πR₀³), the same for all nuclei: about 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³. This shows nucleons are packed together at a fixed spacing, as in a liquid drop, and that the nucleus is far denser than the atom (most of the atom's volume is empty).

#### Worked example

1. Find the closest approach of a 7.7 MeV alpha particle to a gold nucleus (Z = 79).

   r = 2 × 79 × (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)² × 8.99 × 10⁹/(7.7 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³) = 3.64 × 10⁻²⁶/1.23 × 10⁻¹² = 3.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ m.

2. Find the radius of a ¹⁹⁷Au nucleus with R₀ = 1.2 fm, and compare with example 1.

   R = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 197^(1/3) = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 5.82 = 7.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m. The closest approach is about 4 times larger, so it is only an upper limit.

3. Find the density of nuclear matter.

   ρ = 3 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷/(4π × (1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵)³) = 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the closest approach equals the nuclear radius.
- Forgetting the cube root.
- Saying the first minimum of electron diffraction by nuclei goes to zero.

## 3.1.1 Conditions for life on Earth

Life on Earth depends on a narrow set of physical conditions. This topic covers how those conditions came about, how early life then changed the planet, and how scientists work out what past conditions were like.

### 3.1.1.1 How the conditions for life came about

#### What you need to know

- An atmosphere was kept because of the Earth's gravity. Its gases mattered: carbon dioxide and methane kept the surface warm (greenhouse effect); nitrogen was the bulk gas.
- Liquid water existed because surface temperatures sat between 0 °C and 100 °C at the pressures found on Earth.
- Insolation (incoming solar radiation) and albedo (the fraction reflected) set how much energy the surface absorbed.
- Distance from the Sun put Earth in the "habitable zone".
- The Earth's orbit (nearly circular) and axial tilt and rotation kept temperature changes moderate.
- The magnetic field (magnetosphere) deflects charged particles from the Sun (the solar wind and cosmic rays), protecting the atmosphere and surface.

#### Explained

Think of each condition as a dial that had to be in the right range.

| Condition | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Gravity | A planet with too little mass (like Mars or the Moon) loses light gases to space. Earth's gravity held an atmosphere. |
| Greenhouse gases | Early Sun was about 25 to 30% dimmer. CO₂ and CH₄ trapped outgoing infrared, keeping water liquid instead of frozen. |
| Liquid water | A solvent for biochemistry, a medium for transport, and a store of heat (high specific heat capacity) that steadies temperatures. |
| Insolation and albedo | Energy absorbed = insolation × (1 − albedo). Ice and cloud raise albedo and cool; dark oceans and forests absorb more. |
| Distance from the Sun | Closer (Venus) leads to runaway greenhouse heating; further (Mars) is too cold for liquid water at the surface. |
| Orbit and axial behaviour | A near-circular orbit means small yearly changes in insolation. Rotation every 24 hours stops one side baking and the other freezing. Tilt gives seasons but spreads heat. |
| Magnetosphere | Generated by the liquid iron outer core. It shields the atmosphere from being stripped by the solar wind, and shields life from damaging particles. |

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the greenhouse effect is harmful in itself. Without it the average surface temperature would be well below freezing; the problem today is the enhanced effect.
- Saying the magnetosphere blocks ultraviolet. It deflects charged particles. Ultraviolet is absorbed by ozone.
- Confusing albedo (reflection) with absorption: high albedo means less energy absorbed.

### 3.1.1.2 How life changed the environment

#### What you need to know

- Photosynthetic bacteria (cyanobacteria), and later algae and plants, released oxygen into an atmosphere that had almost none.
- Oxygen in the upper atmosphere formed ozone (O₃), which absorbs UV and allowed life to move onto land.
- Organisms removed carbon from the atmosphere and locked it away (sequestration) in limestone, fossil fuels and organic matter.
- Living things link the biogeochemical cycles (carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus) together.

#### Explained

The early atmosphere was mainly nitrogen, carbon dioxide and water vapour, with no free oxygen. From about 2.4 billion years ago, oxygen from cyanobacteria began to build up (the Great Oxidation Event). At first it reacted with dissolved iron in the oceans and with rocks; only when those "sinks" were used up did oxygen accumulate in the air.

Oxygen allowed:

- aerobic respiration, which releases far more energy per glucose than anaerobic respiration, so larger and more complex organisms became possible
- the ozone layer: O₂ split by UV forms O atoms, which join O₂ to make O₃. Ozone absorbs UV-B and UV-C, so the land surface became safe.

Carbon sequestration lowered CO₂. Marine organisms made calcium carbonate shells and skeletons, which became limestone. Plants buried in swamps became coal; plankton buried in mud became oil and gas. This cooled the planet as the Sun brightened, which kept temperatures suitable.

Organisms link cycles: photosynthesis takes in carbon and releases oxygen; nitrogen-fixing bacteria need energy from organic carbon; decomposers release carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus together when they break down dead matter.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying plants made the first oxygen. Photosynthetic bacteria did, long before land plants.
- Saying ozone formed from carbon dioxide. It forms from oxygen.

### 3.1.1.3 Monitoring past conditions

#### What you need to know

- Early methods had limits: they relied on proxy data (indirect evidence), such as dendrochronology (tree rings) and pollen analysis, which cover limited time spans and places and need calibration.
- Improved methods include long-term data sets, electronic monitoring, ice-core analysis of trapped gas and isotopes, and better platforms: balloons, aircraft and satellites.

#### Explained

A proxy is something measurable today that depends on a past condition.

- Tree rings: wide rings mean good growing seasons (warm, wet); narrow rings mean poor ones. Counting rings gives the year. Limits: trees only live a few thousand years (longer with overlapping timber samples), only grow on land, and ring width depends on more than one factor.
- Pollen: each plant species has distinctive pollen that survives in lake and peat sediments. The mix of pollen shows the vegetation, and so the climate. Limits: some plants make far more pollen than others, and pollen can blow long distances.

Improved methods:

- Ice cores: air bubbles in Antarctic and Greenland ice are samples of past atmosphere, so CO₂ and CH₄ can be measured directly back about 800 000 years. The ratio of oxygen isotopes (¹⁸O to ¹⁶O) in the ice shows past temperature.
- Long-term data sets: measurement at one site for decades (for example continuous atmospheric CO₂ records since the 1950s) shows trends that short studies miss.
- Electronic monitoring: data loggers record continuously and unattended, reducing human error.
- Platforms: weather balloons sample the vertical profile; aircraft sample the troposphere and lower stratosphere; satellites give global coverage repeatedly.

#### Worked example

An ice core shows CO₂ of 280 ppm before industrialisation. A modern measurement is 420 ppm. Percentage increase = (420 − 280) ÷ 280 × 100 = 50%.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying ice cores show temperature directly. Temperature comes from isotope ratios (a proxy); gas concentrations are measured directly.
- Forgetting that proxies need calibrating against modern instrument records.

## Why biodiversity matters and how humans affect it

Biodiversity is the variety of life: genes, species and ecosystems. This file covers the reasons to conserve it (resources we use now, value we have not yet found, and services ecosystems provide) and the ways human activity reduces it. Biodiversity indices are in [A-level Biology 14](../../biology/14-species-taxonomy-and-biodiversity.md) and in file [23](23-research-methods-and-fieldwork.md).

### 3.1.2.1.1 Resources and sustainable habitat management

#### What you need to know

- Wild species and habitats supply resources: timber, fibres, vegetable oils, biofuels and new food crops.
- These can be harvested sustainably if the habitat is managed so that use does not exceed renewal.

#### Explained

| Resource | Examples | Sustainable management |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timber | Hardwoods, softwoods | Harvest no more than annual growth; selective logging; replanting |
| Fibres | Cotton, rattan, sisal | Harvest a fraction of wild stems; protect the plants that regrow |
| Vegetable oils | Oil palm, coconut, shea | Grow on already-cleared land; certification schemes |
| Biofuels | Wood, ethanol from sugar cane | Avoid clearing forest; use residues |
| New food crops | Wild relatives domesticated (quinoa, many fruits) | Keep wild populations as a gene source |

The key idea is that a habitat is a renewable resource only while harvest stays below its maximum sustainable yield. Once a forest or wetland is cleared, its resources stop entirely.

#### Common mistakes

- Treating all biofuels as sustainable. Clearing forest to grow them releases more carbon than they save.

### 3.1.2.1.2 Value of species not yet investigated

#### What you need to know

Habitats should be conserved even where species have not been studied, because they may be useful for:

- biomimetics: copying designs from nature
- new medicines
- physiological research
- pest control by wildlife
- genetic resources, including wild crop relatives found in Vavilov centres.

#### Explained

Most species have never been studied in detail, so we do not know what we lose when they go extinct.

- **Biomimetics**: engineers copy solutions evolved over millions of years, for example burrs that inspired hook-and-loop fasteners, termite mounds that inspired passive ventilation, and the shape of a kingfisher's beak used on high-speed train noses to reduce noise.
- **Medicines**: many drugs came first from organisms, for example aspirin from willow bark compounds, and the anticancer drugs vincristine and vinblastine from the Madagascar periwinkle. Untested species may hold more.
- **Physiological research**: unusual species help us understand body processes, for example animals that hibernate (bone and muscle loss) or that tolerate low oxygen.
- **Pest control**: predators and parasites control pests for free. Ladybirds eat aphids; some wasps parasitise caterpillars.
- **Genetic resources**: wild relatives of crops carry genes for disease resistance, drought tolerance and salt tolerance that can be bred into crops. **Vavilov centres** are regions where crops were first domesticated (for example the Fertile Crescent for wheat, the Andes for potato, Mesoamerica for maize). They hold the greatest genetic variety of those crops and their wild relatives, so they are priorities for conservation.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying Vavilov centres are seed banks. They are geographic regions of origin; seed banks are ex-situ stores.

### 3.1.2.1.3 Ecosystem services

#### What you need to know

Ecosystems provide services that support life and human society:

- control of atmospheric composition
- biogeochemical cycles
- services between species: pollination and seed dispersal
- soil formation and erosion control.

#### Explained

- **Atmospheric composition**: photosynthesis removes CO₂ and releases O₂; forests and oceans are carbon sinks; wetlands and soils store carbon.
- **Biogeochemical cycles**: decomposers recycle nutrients; nitrogen-fixing bacteria make nitrogen available.
- **Pollination**: about three quarters of leading food crops benefit from animal pollination. Losing pollinators cuts yield and quality.
- **Seed dispersal**: birds, bats and mammals carry seeds, letting plants colonise new ground and recover after disturbance.
- **Soil**: roots bind soil; leaf litter adds organic matter; earthworms mix and aerate it. Vegetation cover reduces erosion by wind and rain.

Ecosystem services are often unpriced, so they are lost when land is cleared for a priced product. Assigning a value (for example, the cost of hand-pollinating a crop) helps decision-makers.

### 3.1.2.2 Human influence on biodiversity

#### What you need to know

Humans reduce biodiversity through:

- direct exploitation (hunting, fishing, collecting)
- deliberate eradication (of pests, predators, disease vectors)
- changing abiotic factors: water, light, oxygen, nutrients, pH, temperature
- changing biotic factors: introduced species, loss of relationships between species
- habitat destruction: deforestation, agriculture, urbanisation, mineral extraction, reservoirs.

#### Explained

**Direct exploitation**: species taken faster than they reproduce. Large, slow-breeding (K-selected) species are most at risk, for example great whales, rhinos for horn, and many fish stocks.

**Deliberate eradication**: wolves, bears and birds of prey were killed in Britain to protect livestock and game. Pest control can remove species with useful roles.

**Abiotic changes** (each with an example):

| Factor | Human cause | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water | Wetland drainage, river abstraction | Wetland species lost |
| Light | Turbid water from soil erosion; artificial light at night | Less aquatic photosynthesis; disrupted insect and bird behaviour |
| Oxygen | Organic pollution, eutrophication | Fish kills |
| Nutrients | Fertiliser run-off | Nutrient-loving plants outcompete specialists of poor soils |
| pH | Acid rain, acid mine drainage | Acid-sensitive species lost from lakes |
| Temperature | Climate change, thermal pollution | Range shifts; coral bleaching |

**Biotic changes**:

- Introduced (non-native, invasive) species compete with, prey on or spread disease to natives. Examples: grey squirrels carry squirrelpox, which kills red squirrels; rats on islands eat seabird eggs; Japanese knotweed shades out native plants.
- Loss of relationships: if a pollinator or seed disperser dies out, the plant that depends on it declines too.

**Habitat destruction** is the single largest cause of biodiversity loss: clearing forest for farmland, building towns and roads, quarrying and mining, and flooding valleys for reservoirs. Fragmentation into small patches makes populations small and isolated.

#### Common mistakes

- Writing "pollution" without naming which abiotic factor changes and how.
- Treating every non-native species as harmful. Only invasive ones cause damage.

## Conservation priorities, legislation and captive breeding

Conservation money and effort are limited, so they have to be targeted. This file covers how priorities are set, the laws and agreements that protect species and sites, and how captive breeding and release (CBR) works.

### 3.1.2.3.1 Setting priorities

#### What you need to know

- The IUCN (International Union for Conservation of Nature) assesses species and publishes the Red List.
- Criteria for priority: Red List category, evolutionary uniqueness (EDGE species), endemic species, keystone species, flagship species, and how dispersed a population is.

#### Explained

**Red List categories** run from Least Concern, Near Threatened, Vulnerable, Endangered and Critically Endangered to Extinct in the Wild and Extinct. Species with too little information are Data Deficient. The threatened categories are decided by measurable criteria: how fast the population is falling, how small it is, how small its geographic range is, and the modelled probability of extinction.

**EDGE** means Evolutionarily Distinct and Globally Endangered. A species with no close relatives (for example the aye-aye or a tuatara) holds a large amount of unique evolutionary history. Losing it loses more "branch length" from the tree of life than losing one of many similar species.

**Endemic species** live in only one place, often an island or isolated mountain. If they are lost there, they are lost everywhere.

**Keystone species** have an effect on their ecosystem far larger than their numbers suggest. Examples: sea otters eat sea urchins, which would otherwise destroy kelp forests; beavers create wetlands; fig trees feed many animals when other fruit is scarce. Protecting a keystone species protects many others.

**Flagship species** are charismatic animals (giant panda, tiger, orangutan) used to raise public interest and money. Protecting their habitat protects many less popular species too.

**Population dispersal**: a species split into a few small, isolated populations is at higher risk (inbreeding, local catastrophes) than one with many connected populations.

#### Common mistakes

- Confusing keystone (ecological importance) with flagship (public appeal). Some species are both, but the reasons differ.

### 3.1.2.3.2 Legislation and protocols

#### What you need to know

- The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 (UK).
- UK site designations: SSSI, NNR, SAC, SPA, Natura 2000 sites, Ramsar sites, MNR, LNR, MPA, MCZ.
- CITES (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species), Appendices I and II.
- Regulation of exploitation by the IWC (International Whaling Commission), the EU Common Fisheries Policy (CFP) and the ITTO (International Tropical Timber Organisation).

#### Explained

**Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981**: makes it an offence to kill, injure or take listed wild animals and to pick or uproot listed plants; protects nesting birds; controls release of non-native species; and is the basis for designating SSSIs.

**Site designations**:

| Designation | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| SSSI (Site of Special Scientific Interest) | UK sites of special wildlife or geological value; owners need consent for damaging operations |
| NNR (National Nature Reserve) | Key sites managed mainly for conservation and research |
| LNR (Local Nature Reserve) | Declared by local councils for wildlife and for people to enjoy |
| SAC (Special Area of Conservation) | Protects listed habitats and non-bird species (originally under the EU Habitats Directive) |
| SPA (Special Protection Area) | Protects rare and migratory birds (originally under the EU Birds Directive) |
| Natura 2000 | The network of SACs and SPAs across Europe |
| Ramsar site | Wetland of international importance (Ramsar Convention, 1971) |
| MNR (Marine Nature Reserve) | Early UK marine protected sites |
| MPA (Marine Protected Area) | General term for protected sea areas |
| MCZ (Marine Conservation Zone) | UK marine areas protecting nationally important habitats and species |

**CITES** controls international trade:

- Appendix I: species threatened with extinction. Commercial trade is banned (for example tigers, most great apes).
- Appendix II: species that could become threatened if trade is not controlled. Trade needs an export permit showing it is not harmful to the species' survival.

**Regulating exploitation**:

- IWC: set a moratorium (pause) on commercial whaling from 1986.
- EU Common Fisheries Policy: sets catch limits (quotas), technical rules on nets, and closed areas for EU waters.
- ITTO: promotes trade in tropical timber from sustainably managed forests.

#### Common mistakes

- Mixing up SAC (habitats and non-bird species) and SPA (birds).
- Saying CITES bans all trade in Appendix II species. It controls it with permits.

### 3.1.2.3.3 Captive breeding and release

#### What you need to know

- Ex-situ conservation keeps organisms away from their natural habitat (zoos, seed banks, botanic gardens).
- Criteria for choosing CBR, breeding methods (artificial insemination, embryo transfer, managing group dynamics), soft and hard release, and post-release monitoring.

#### Explained

**When CBR is appropriate**: the wild population is too small to recover alone; the species breeds well in captivity; suitable habitat exists (or will exist) for release; the original threat has been removed. Without the last two, released animals simply die.

**Breeding methods**:

- Artificial insemination (AI): sperm from a male is placed in a female. Sperm can be frozen and moved between zoos, so genes are shared without moving animals, and males that will not mate can still father young.
- Embryo transfer: embryos are placed into a surrogate mother, possibly of a common related species, so a rare female can produce more young.
- Group dynamics: social species need the right group size and structure to breed (for example flamingos need a large flock; some primates need stable family groups).
- Studbooks record the ancestry of every animal so that matings avoid inbreeding and keep genetic diversity.

**Release methods**:

- Hard release: animals are released straight into the wild with no support.
- Soft release: animals are kept in an enclosure at the release site to get used to it, with food and shelter provided at first and gradually withdrawn. Survival is usually higher.

**Problems**: captive animals may lose wild behaviours (finding food, avoiding predators); small founder populations lose genetic variation; disease may spread to wild populations; cost is high.

**Post-release monitoring**: radio collars or tags, sightings and counts track survival, breeding and movement, so methods can be improved. Without monitoring, success is unknown.

#### Worked example

A captive population was founded by 8 animals. The fraction of heterozygosity kept per generation is 1 − 1/(2N), where N is the effective population size. With N = 8: 1 − 1/16 = 0.9375. After 5 generations: 0.9375⁵ ≈ 0.72, so about 28% of the original heterozygosity is lost. This shows why studbooks and gene exchange between zoos matter.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying CBR saves a species on its own. It only works with habitat protection and removal of the original threat.

## 3.1.2.3.4 Habitat conservation and key ecosystems

Protecting habitats (in-situ conservation) protects whole communities at once and keeps the relationships between species working. This file covers how habitats are conserved and created, what makes a reserve effective, and seven ecosystems the spec names: for each, its features, importance, threats and the conservation methods used.

### Habitat conservation methods

#### What you need to know

- In-situ conservation protects species in their natural habitats.
- Habitat creation and rewilding.
- Reserve design: area, shape, age of habitat and wildlife corridors.

#### Explained

**In-situ conservation** is usually cheaper per species than captive breeding, keeps natural behaviour and keeps evolution going. It needs control of threats (hunting, clearance, pollution).

**Habitat creation**: making new habitat, for example digging ponds, planting woodland, creating reedbeds on old gravel pits, or managed realignment of coasts to form saltmarsh.

**Rewilding**: restoring natural processes and letting the ecosystem largely manage itself, often by reintroducing missing species (for example beavers, whose dams create wetland) and reducing human management.

**Reserve design**:

| Feature | Better | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Area | Larger | Supports bigger populations, more species and large-range animals |
| Shape | Compact (near circular) | Less edge relative to area, so fewer edge effects (drying, light, disturbance, predators from outside) |
| Age | Older habitat | Older woodland has more niches (dead wood, old trees) and species that are slow to colonise |
| Corridors | Present | Hedgerows, river banks and green bridges link fragments so animals can move, find mates and recolonise |

#### Worked example

Compare edge-to-area for a 1 km by 1 km square reserve and a 4 km by 0.25 km strip of the same area. Both have area 1 km². Perimeters are 4 km and 8.5 km. The strip has more than twice as much edge per unit area, so more of it is affected by edge effects.

### The seven ecosystems

#### Temperate broadleaf woodland

- **Features**: deciduous trees (oak, ash, beech) that drop leaves in winter; layered structure (canopy, shrub layer, field layer, ground layer); rich leaf litter and soil fauna; seasonal light on the woodland floor in spring (bluebells flower before the canopy closes).
- **Importance**: biodiversity (many invertebrates on oak), timber, carbon storage, soil protection, recreation.
- **Threats**: clearance for farming and building; fragmentation; overgrazing by deer, which stops regeneration; invasive species (rhododendron); tree diseases (ash dieback); loss of traditional management.
- **Conservation**: protection as SSSI or NNR; coppicing to restore varied structure; deer control; planting native species; linking fragments with hedgerows.

#### Tropical rainforest

- **Features**: high rainfall and temperature all year; very high biodiversity and productivity; most nutrients held in living biomass, with thin, nutrient-poor soils; tall layered canopy; many epiphytes.
- **Importance**: biodiversity, medicines, timber and non-timber products, carbon store, regional rainfall (transpiration recycles moisture), indigenous peoples' homes.
- **Threats**: clearance for cattle ranching, soya and oil palm; logging; mining; roads opening up the forest; fire.
- **Conservation**: protected areas; sustainable forestry certification; extractive reserves where local people harvest products sustainably; ecotourism; **Debt for Nature Swaps**, in which part of a country's foreign debt is cancelled or bought by a conservation body in return for the country funding forest protection.

#### Tropical coral reefs

- **Features**: built by colonial corals with symbiotic algae (zooxanthellae) in their tissues; need warm (about 20 to 29 °C), clear, shallow, nutrient-poor, well-lit water; very high biodiversity.
- **Importance**: fisheries, coastal protection from waves, tourism, medicines, biodiversity.
- **Threats**: rising sea temperature causes bleaching (corals expel their algae); ocean acidification makes calcium carbonate harder to build; sediment from land clearance blocks light; nutrient pollution favours algae; overfishing of grazing fish; destructive fishing (explosives, cyanide); crown-of-thorns starfish outbreaks; physical damage by anchors and divers.
- **Conservation**: marine protected areas with no-take zones; controlling land run-off; banning destructive fishing; reducing CO₂ emissions; coral gardening (growing fragments to replant).

#### Deep-water coral reefs

- **Features**: cold-water corals (for example *Lophelia*) at hundreds of metres depth with no light and no symbiotic algae; they feed on plankton; grow very slowly (millimetres per year); form reefs that shelter fish.
- **Importance**: biodiversity, fish nursery areas.
- **Threats**: bottom trawling smashes reefs, which take centuries to regrow; oil and gas drilling; acidification.
- **Conservation**: banning bottom trawling in reef areas (marine protected areas); mapping reefs so they can be avoided.

#### Oceanic islands

- **Features**: isolated, so few species arrive; many endemic species evolve; often no native mammal predators, so birds may be flightless and ground-nesting; small populations.
- **Importance**: endemic species, evolutionary research (adaptive radiation).
- **Threats**: introduced predators (rats, cats, goats eating vegetation); habitat clearance; small ranges, so a single event can cause extinction.
- **Conservation**: eradicating invasive mammals (for example rat removal from seabird islands); biosecurity to stop new arrivals; captive breeding of endemics.

#### Mangroves

- **Features**: salt-tolerant trees on tropical coasts in the intertidal zone; aerial roots (pneumatophores) for gas exchange in waterlogged mud; prop roots trap sediment.
- **Importance**: coastal protection from storms and tsunamis; nursery areas for fish; carbon store in mud ("blue carbon"); timber and fuel; filter sediment, protecting nearby coral reefs.
- **Threats**: clearance for shrimp farms, tourism and building; pollution; changes to freshwater and sediment supply.
- **Conservation**: protection; replanting; sustainable harvesting by local communities.

#### Antarctica

- **Features**: coldest continent; mostly ice-covered; very low terrestrial biodiversity but productive surrounding ocean (krill support whales, seals and penguins).
- **Importance**: climate records in ice; global climate regulation; wildlife; science.
- **Threats**: climate change (ice shelf loss, changing sea ice affecting krill and penguins); overfishing of krill and toothfish; tourism; pollution; past whaling and sealing.
- **Conservation**: the **Antarctic Treaty 1959** sets the continent aside for peace and science; later agreements ban mineral extraction and protect the environment; regulation of fishing (catch limits for krill and toothfish); controls on tourism.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying deep-water corals bleach. They have no symbiotic algae; bleaching is a tropical coral problem.
- Saying rainforest soils are fertile. Most nutrients are in the vegetation, so cleared land loses fertility quickly.

## Monitoring, ecological principles, succession and populations

Good conservation planning needs data on what is present and how populations are changing, and an understanding of how organisms relate to their habitat. Background on succession and populations is in [A-level Biology 23](../../biology/23-populations-in-ecosystems.md). Field sampling methods are in [23](23-research-methods-and-fieldwork.md).

### 3.1.2.3.5 Ecological monitoring in conservation planning

#### What you need to know

Monitoring finds out: which species are present; population size; distribution; survival rates; age structure.

#### Explained

- **Species present**: a baseline survey lists species so changes can be detected and priorities chosen.
- **Population size**: shows whether a species is increasing or decreasing; methods include counts, quadrats and mark-release-recapture.
- **Distribution**: maps where a species occurs, to locate key sites and corridors.
- **Survival rates**: the proportion of individuals surviving a year, or of released animals surviving, shows whether a population can sustain itself.
- **Age structure**: a population with many young is growing; one with mostly old individuals and few young is not recruiting and will decline.

Repeating surveys with the same method at the same time of year makes results comparable.

### 3.1.2.3.6 New monitoring technologies

#### What you need to know

Satellite and radio tracking; DNA databases and environmental DNA (eDNA); image recognition; acoustic monitoring and sonograms.

#### Explained

| Technology | How it works | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Radio tracking | Animal wears a transmitter; receiver locates it by signal direction | Home range, survival of released animals |
| Satellite tracking | Tag sends GPS positions via satellite | Long-distance migration (turtles, albatrosses, whales) |
| DNA databases | Reference DNA sequences for species and individuals | Identify species from fragments, track illegal trade (for example ivory source) |
| eDNA | Organisms shed DNA into water or soil; a sample is filtered and sequenced | Detect rare or hidden species (great crested newts in ponds) without catching them |
| Image recognition | Software identifies species or individuals from camera-trap photos (stripe, spot or fin patterns) | Count large numbers of images quickly |
| Acoustic monitoring and sonograms | Recorders log sounds; a sonogram plots frequency against time | Identify bats, birds, whales, frogs by call |

Advantages: less disturbance, larger areas and longer periods covered, fewer skilled field workers needed. Limits: cost, battery life, tag effects on animals, eDNA cannot give population size or show whether the animal is alive nearby now.

### 3.1.3.1 Adaptation and habitat requirements

#### What you need to know

Organisms are adapted to abiotic factors (light, water, nutrients, pH, physical habitat) and biotic factors (food, control by predators, pollination, seed dispersal, biotic habitat such as trees for nesting, and other interspecies relationships).

#### Explained

A conservation plan must provide everything a species needs. For example, a bat needs insects (food), roosts (often in old trees or buildings), hedgerows to navigate along, and unpolluted water bodies for insects. Losing any one factor can cause decline. Interspecies relationships include mutualism (pollinator and flower), parasitism, predation and competition.

### 3.1.3.2 Ecological terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Species | A group of organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring |
| Taxon | Any named group in classification (species, genus, family ...) |
| Ecological niche | The role of a species: how it uses resources and interacts with abiotic and biotic factors |
| Population | All individuals of one species in an area at one time |
| Community | All populations of all species in an area |
| Ecosystem | A community and its abiotic environment, interacting |
| Biome | A large-scale ecosystem type defined by climate and vegetation (tundra, desert, tropical rainforest) |

Common mistake: saying a niche is where an organism lives. That is its habitat.

### 3.1.3.3 Ecological succession and plagioclimax

#### What you need to know

- Succession: colonisation by pioneer species, a series of seral stages (seres), modification of abiotic conditions, ending in a climax community.
- Deflected succession and plagioclimax, maintained by grazing, mowing, burning, coppicing or pollarding.
- Secondary succession.

#### Explained

**Primary succession** starts on bare ground with no soil (new volcanic rock, sand dunes, retreating glaciers).

1. Pioneer species (lichens, mosses, marram grass on dunes) tolerate harsh conditions.
2. They change abiotic conditions: weathering rock, adding organic matter when they die, so soil forms and holds water and nutrients.
3. New species colonise and outcompete the pioneers. Each stage is a sere.
4. Biodiversity, biomass and soil depth generally increase.
5. A climax community forms, stable under the local climate (in lowland Britain, usually deciduous woodland).

**Secondary succession** happens where vegetation is removed but soil remains (after fire or ploughing). It is faster because soil and seeds are present.

**Deflected succession** happens when a human activity stops succession reaching climax, holding it at a **plagioclimax**. Many valued British habitats are plagioclimaxes:

- grazing keeps chalk grassland free of scrub
- mowing for hay keeps meadows
- burning keeps heather moorland
- coppicing (cutting trees to ground level on a cycle) and pollarding (cutting above browsing height) keep a mosaic of light and shade in woodland.

Conservation of these habitats needs the management to continue.

### 3.1.3.4 Population control

#### What you need to know

- Carrying capacity; density-dependent and density-independent factors.
- Conservation uses release programmes, habitat management, culling and eradication.
- r-selected and K-selected species.

#### Explained

**Carrying capacity (K)** is the largest population an environment can support long-term. A population grows quickly at first, then slows as resources become limiting, and levels off near K (a sigmoid curve).

- **Density-dependent factors** have a stronger effect as density increases: competition for food, predation, disease and parasitism. They regulate populations around K.
- **Density-independent factors** affect a population regardless of its density: floods, drought, frost, fire, pollution events.

**Management**: release programmes raise numbers of rare species; habitat management raises K (for example more nest sites); culling reduces overabundant species (deer that prevent woodland regeneration); eradication removes invasive species (rats on islands).

| r-selected species | K-selected species |
| --- | --- |
| Many small offspring, little parental care | Few large offspring, much parental care |
| Short life, early maturity | Long life, late maturity |
| Populations fluctuate; colonise disturbed habitats | Populations stable near K |
| Examples: many insects, weeds | Examples: elephants, great apes, whales |

K-selected species are slower to recover from loss, so they are more vulnerable to exploitation.

#### Worked example

A deer population of 400 lives in a wood with a carrying capacity of 250. To bring the population to K, 150 must be removed (37.5%). If the population then grows by 20% per year without culling, the next year it would be 300, so about 50 must be culled each year to hold it at 250.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling weather a density-dependent factor.
- Saying climax communities never change. They change slowly with climate.

## The atmosphere and global climate change

The atmosphere controls how solar energy reaches and leaves the Earth. Human activity is changing its composition and so the climate. Background biology of climate change is in [A-level Biology 42](../../biology/42-climate-change-and-human-impact.md); feedback loops are revisited in [22 Sustainability](22-sustainability.md).

### 3.2.1.1 UV, visible light and IR in the atmosphere

#### What you need to know

- The origins and roles of UV and IR: insolation from the Sun, IR emitted by the Earth, thermal stratification of the atmosphere, chemical processes.
- How each wavelength is transmitted, absorbed and converted to heat or chemical energy, or reflected.

#### Explained

The Sun is very hot, so it emits mainly short wavelengths: UV, visible and near IR. The Earth is much cooler, so it emits long-wave IR.

| Radiation | Source | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UV | Sun | UV-C and most UV-B absorbed by oxygen and ozone in the stratosphere; the energy breaks bonds (chemical energy) and heats the stratosphere |
| Visible | Sun | Mostly transmitted through a clear atmosphere; some reflected by cloud, ice and pale surfaces; the rest absorbed by the surface and converted to heat; used in photosynthesis (chemical energy) |
| IR (short-wave) | Sun | Partly absorbed by water vapour and CO₂; rest reaches the surface |
| IR (long-wave) | Earth's surface | Absorbed by greenhouse gases in the troposphere, which re-emit it in all directions, some back down: the greenhouse effect |

**Thermal stratification**: the troposphere is heated from below by the warm surface, so it cools with height. The stratosphere is heated from within by ozone absorbing UV, so it warms with height. The warm layer over a cooler one is stable, so the stratosphere hardly mixes with the troposphere. This is why pollutants that reach it (like CFCs) stay for decades.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying greenhouse gases trap incoming sunlight. They absorb outgoing long-wave IR.
- Saying the hole in the ozone layer causes global warming. They are different problems with some links.

### 3.2.1.2 Global climate change

#### What you need to know

- Greenhouse gases: CO₂, CH₄, oxides of nitrogen (mainly N₂O), tropospheric ozone and CFCs; their sources.
- Effects on interconnected systems: thermohaline circulation, El Niño, sea-level rise; the cryosphere (snow, glaciers, ice sheets, ice shelves, sea ice); precipitation and wind.
- Why monitoring and predicting is difficult: data gaps, reliability of proxies, remote sensing, climate models, natural fluctuations, time lags.
- Uncertainty about ecological impacts.
- Negative feedback (low cloud, photosynthesis) and positive feedback (permafrost melt, acidification, albedo, methane hydrates, fires, cirrus cloud, soil decomposition); tipping points.
- Carbon footprints and sustainable development.

#### Explained

**Greenhouse gases**:

| Gas | Main human sources | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CO₂ | Burning fossil fuels, deforestation, cement making | Largest total effect |
| CH₄ | Rice paddies, cattle, landfill, gas leaks, coal mines | Much stronger per molecule than CO₂ but shorter-lived |
| N₂O | Fertilised soils, combustion | Long-lived |
| Tropospheric ozone | Formed from NOx and hydrocarbons in sunlight | Also a pollutant (smog) |
| CFCs | Old refrigerants, aerosols, foam | Very strong per molecule; also destroy stratospheric ozone |

**Ocean effects**:

- Thermohaline circulation is driven by density differences: cold, salty water sinks in the North Atlantic. Melting ice adds fresh water, lowering density, which could slow the circulation and cool north-west Europe (see [08](08-the-hydrosphere.md)).
- El Niño: a periodic weakening of Pacific trade winds that lets warm water spread east, changing rainfall worldwide. Warming may change its frequency or strength.
- Sea-level rise: thermal expansion of warming water, plus melting land ice (glaciers and ice sheets). Melting sea ice and floating ice shelves do not raise sea level directly, but their loss lets land ice flow faster into the sea.

**Cryosphere**: less snow cover and sea ice lower albedo; glaciers retreat, threatening dry-season water supply; ice sheets in Greenland and Antarctica lose mass; ice shelves break up.

**Precipitation and wind**: warmer air holds more water vapour, so heavier rainfall events; patterns shift, with some regions wetter and others drier; storm tracks may move.

**Why monitoring and prediction are difficult**:

- Data gaps: few long records from oceans, poles and poorer countries.
- Proxy reliability: tree rings, ice cores and sediments need calibration and have uncertainty.
- Remote sensing: satellites only since the 1970s; instruments drift and change.
- Models: the climate system is complex; clouds and feedbacks are hard to represent; models depend on future emissions, which depend on human choices.
- Natural fluctuations: volcanic eruptions, solar cycles, El Niño hide the trend in short records.
- Time lags: oceans warm slowly, so effects continue long after emissions.

**Ecological uncertainty**: species may adapt, migrate or decline; interactions (for example a predator and prey responding at different rates) are hard to predict.

**Feedback**:

- Negative (damps change): warming increases evaporation and low cloud, which reflects sunlight; higher CO₂ can increase photosynthesis, removing CO₂.
- Positive (amplifies change): permafrost melting releases CH₄ and CO₂; warmer, more acidic oceans absorb less CO₂; melting ice lowers albedo so more heat is absorbed; methane hydrates on the sea floor may release CH₄; more forest and peat fires release CO₂; more high cirrus cloud traps IR; faster soil decomposition releases CO₂.
- Tipping point: a threshold beyond which a positive feedback becomes self-sustaining and the system moves to a new state even if the original cause stops.

**Carbon footprint**: the total greenhouse gas emissions caused by a person, product or organisation, expressed as CO₂ equivalent. Sustainable development means meeting present needs without stopping future generations meeting theirs; reducing footprints (energy efficiency, renewables, less meat, less flying) is part of it.

#### Worked example

An albedo of 0.8 for fresh sea ice and 0.06 for open ocean: with 200 W m⁻² of insolation, ice absorbs 200 × 0.2 = 40 W m⁻² and ocean absorbs 200 × 0.94 = 188 W m⁻². Replacing ice with ocean increases absorption by 148 W m⁻², warming further: a positive feedback.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying melting sea ice raises sea level.
- Describing a feedback without saying whether it amplifies or reduces the original change.

## 3.2.1.3 Ozone depletion

Stratospheric ozone depletion is studied as a case of how environmental science works: a hypothesis, evidence, explanation of the pattern, and an international response. The chemistry is also in [A-level Chemistry 35](../../chemistry/35-atmosphere-ozone-and-green-chemistry.md).

### The hypothesis

#### What you need to know

- The Rowland-Molina hypothesis (1974): CFCs are persistent, reach the stratosphere, are split by UV, and release chlorine that destroys ozone.

#### Explained

CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) were used as refrigerants, aerosol propellants and in foams because they are unreactive, non-toxic and non-flammable. Being unreactive, they are not broken down in the troposphere and slowly mix up into the stratosphere over several years.

In the stratosphere, strong UV breaks a C–Cl bond, releasing a chlorine atom (a free radical):

- Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂
- ClO + O → Cl + O₂

The chlorine atom is regenerated, so it acts as a catalyst: one atom can destroy many thousands of ozone molecules before it is locked up in a stable compound. Overall: O₃ + O → 2O₂.

### Effects of increased UV-B

#### What you need to know

Effects on human health, plants and marine life.

#### Explained

- **Human health**: more skin cancer (including melanoma), cataracts, sunburn and suppressed immune response.
- **Plants**: DNA damage, reduced photosynthesis and growth, lower crop yields.
- **Marine life**: phytoplankton in surface water are damaged, reducing productivity at the base of food webs; larvae of fish and other animals near the surface are harmed.

### Collecting the evidence

#### What you need to know

- Ground-based and satellite data.
- Variation in ozone with place (spatial), time (temporal, seasonal) and altitude.

#### Explained

- **Ground stations** (for example spectrophotometers in Antarctica) measured total column ozone from the 1950s. British Antarctic Survey scientists published a large springtime fall in 1985.
- **Satellites** give global maps. Early satellite software had flagged very low values as errors, so the hole was at first missed; reprocessing confirmed it. This shows the need to question automatic data filtering.
- **Balloons (ozonesondes)** measure ozone at different altitudes, showing loss concentrated in the lower stratosphere (about 15 to 20 km).

Variability: ozone varies naturally with latitude, season, the solar cycle and weather systems, so long records are needed to see a trend.

### Why Antarctica is worst

#### What you need to know

Very low temperature, ice crystals, polar stratospheric clouds, the polar vortex.

#### Explained

1. In the Antarctic winter a strong circular wind (the polar vortex) isolates the air above the continent, so it cannot mix with warmer, ozone-rich air.
2. Temperatures in the vortex fall below about −78 °C, so polar stratospheric clouds (PSCs) of ice and nitric acid crystals form.
3. Reactions on the crystal surfaces convert stable chlorine "reservoir" compounds (HCl, ClONO₂) into forms (Cl₂) that are easily split by light.
4. In spring (September to November) sunlight returns, splits the chlorine, and catalytic destruction is very fast.
5. When the vortex breaks up in late spring, ozone-rich air flows back in.

The Arctic vortex is weaker and warmer, so loss there is smaller and less regular.

### Restoring the ozone layer

#### What you need to know

- The Montreal Protocol (1987): phasing out CFCs, use of alternatives, collection and safe disposal of existing CFCs.
- Comparing the response with the response to climate change.

#### Explained

The Montreal Protocol set dates to stop making CFCs and other ozone-depleting substances, with later amendments tightening controls and adding more chemicals. It worked because:

- the cause was clear and the science agreed
- there were few producers, so control was simple
- alternatives existed (HCFCs as a temporary step, then HFCs, hydrocarbons, ammonia)
- poorer countries got time and money to change
- old fridges and equipment were collected and CFCs recovered and destroyed.

Atmospheric chlorine is now falling and the ozone layer is slowly recovering, expected to return to 1980 levels around the middle of this century (later over Antarctica), because CFCs last decades.

**Comparison with climate change**:

| Ozone | Climate change |
| --- | --- |
| A few gases from a few industries | Emissions from almost every activity (energy, transport, farming) |
| Cheap alternatives available | Alternatives often need big investment |
| Effects clear and quick to show | Effects spread over decades, harder to attribute |
| Strong international agreement | Agreements (Kyoto, Paris) harder to enforce |

A complication: HFCs, the CFC replacements, are strong greenhouse gases; a later amendment (Kigali, 2016) phases them down.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying CFCs destroy ozone directly. Chlorine atoms released from CFCs do, as catalysts.
- Saying the ozone hole is a hole with no ozone. It is a region of greatly reduced ozone.
- Saying the hole lets heat in and causes global warming.

## 3.2.2 The hydrosphere

The hydrosphere is all the water on Earth. Fresh water is a renewable resource only if it is used no faster than it is replenished. This file covers the water cycle and human effects on it, sustainable management, the thermohaline circulation, water treatment and new sources. Aquifers and groundwater flow are covered in more geological depth in [Geology 14](../geology-ocr-h414/14-fluids-in-rocks-and-hydrogeology.md).

### 3.2.2.1 The hydrological cycle and unsustainable exploitation

#### What you need to know

- The hydrological cycle is a dynamic equilibrium of stores (reservoirs) and flows.
- Human activities change how much water is in each store and how long it stays (residence time).

#### Explained

**Stores**: oceans (about 97% of all water), ice caps and glaciers, groundwater, lakes, soil moisture, rivers, atmosphere, living things.

**Flows**: evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation, infiltration, percolation, surface run-off, throughflow, groundwater flow.

**Residence time** = volume of store ÷ rate of flow through it. Water stays about 9 days in the atmosphere, weeks in rivers, years to decades in lakes, up to thousands of years in deep groundwater and ice sheets.

In dynamic equilibrium, inputs and outputs of each store balance on average. Human activities upset this:

| Activity | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Pumping groundwater faster than recharge | Water table falls; wells dry; land subsides; salt water enters coastal aquifers |
| Abstraction from rivers | Lower flow; less water downstream; wetlands dry (for example the Aral Sea shrank when its feeder rivers were diverted for cotton irrigation) |
| Deforestation | Less interception and transpiration; faster run-off; more flooding, less infiltration |
| Urbanisation | Impermeable surfaces; rapid run-off; less groundwater recharge |
| Reservoirs | Water held longer; more evaporation in hot climates |
| Climate change | Glaciers shrink, reducing dry-season meltwater; changing rainfall patterns |

#### Worked example

A lake holds 2.4 × 10⁸ m³ and its outflow is 4.0 × 10⁶ m³ per day. Residence time = 2.4 × 10⁸ ÷ 4.0 × 10⁶ = 60 days. If a pollutant enters the lake, it will take months to flush out.

### 3.2.2.2 Sustainable management of water

#### What you need to know

Strategies for sustainable management, using examples of exploited water resources.

#### Explained

Sustainable use keeps abstraction below recharge and protects water quality. Strategies:

- abstraction licences that limit how much each user can take, based on recharge
- catchment management: planting trees and restoring wetlands to slow run-off and raise infiltration
- managed aquifer recharge: deliberately putting excess winter water back into aquifers
- efficient irrigation (drip instead of flood irrigation)
- water pricing to reduce waste
- protecting recharge areas from pollution.

### 3.2.2.3 Thermohaline circulation

#### What you need to know

How the thermohaline circulation redistributes heat and affects climate, including in the UK and North Atlantic.

#### Explained

"Thermo" means temperature, "haline" means salt. Both control seawater density.

1. The Gulf Stream and North Atlantic Drift carry warm surface water north-east from the tropics.
2. It releases heat to the air, warming western Europe (the UK is several degrees warmer than places at the same latitude in Canada).
3. The water becomes cold, and sea-ice formation and evaporation make it saltier, so it is dense and sinks near Greenland and Iceland (North Atlantic Deep Water).
4. Deep water flows south and around the globe, rising in other oceans and returning at the surface. A full circuit takes about a thousand years.

**Climate change risk**: melting Greenland ice and higher rainfall add fresh water, making the surface water less dense, so less sinks. A weaker circulation would carry less heat north, causing relative cooling of north-west Europe, changes in rainfall, and possibly faster sea-level rise on the US east coast.

### 3.2.2.4 Treating contaminated water

#### What you need to know

How litter, suspended solids, metals and odours, organic pollutants, salt and pathogens are removed.

#### Explained

| Contaminant | Method |
| --- | --- |
| Litter and large objects | Screens (metal bars) |
| Suspended solids | Settlement tanks; flocculation (adding aluminium or iron salts to make particles clump); sand filtration |
| Metals and odours | Oxidation by aeration; activated carbon adsorption; precipitation |
| Organic pollutants | Aerobic decomposition by microorganisms; activated carbon |
| Salt | Desalination: reverse osmosis (pushing water through a membrane at high pressure) or distillation; both need a lot of energy |
| Pathogens | Chlorination, ozone or UV light |

Sewage treatment stages are in [17](17-oil-pesticides-nutrients-and-metals.md).

### 3.2.2.5 Economical use and new sources

#### What you need to know

- Economical use: metering, low water-use appliances, grey water.
- New sources: rainwater catchment, new reservoirs or estuary barrages, unexploited aquifers, inter-basin transfers.

#### Explained

**Economical use**:

- Metering: paying for what you use encourages saving; also helps find leaks.
- Low water-use appliances: dual-flush toilets, aerating taps, low-flow showers, efficient washing machines.
- Grey water: reusing lightly used water from basins and showers to flush toilets or water gardens.

**New sources**:

| Source | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rainwater catchment (roofs into tanks) | Cheap, local | Depends on rainfall; storage space |
| New reservoir | Large, reliable store | Floods land and habitats; displaces people; sediment fills it |
| Estuary barrage | Stores fresh river water | Destroys mudflat habitat; blocks fish migration |
| Unexploited aquifers | Often clean, large | Risk of overuse; may be slow to recharge or saline |
| Inter-basin transfer (pipes or canals between catchments) | Moves water from wet to dry areas | Expensive; harms donor river; may spread invasive species |
| Desalination | Unlimited sea water | High energy and cost; brine waste |

#### Common mistakes

- Treating groundwater as unlimited. Deep aquifers may take thousands of years to refill ("fossil water").
- Saying the thermohaline circulation is driven by wind. Surface currents are wind-driven; the deep circulation is driven by density.

## 3.2.3 Mineral resources

Minerals from the lithosphere supply metals, industrial raw materials and building materials. They form over geological time, so they are non-renewable on human timescales. The geology of ore formation and exploration is covered in more depth in [Geology 18 Mining geology](../geology-ocr-h414/18-mining-geology.md).

### 3.2.3.1 Minerals are non-renewable

#### What you need to know

Categories: metals and their ores; industrial minerals; construction materials.

#### Explained

- **Metal ores**: rocks containing enough metal to extract at a profit, for example haematite (iron), bauxite (aluminium), chalcopyrite (copper), galena (lead), cassiterite (tin).
- **Industrial minerals**: used for their chemical or physical properties, for example china clay (paper, ceramics), salt, potash (fertiliser), gypsum (plaster), fluorite.
- **Construction materials**: sand, gravel, crushed rock (aggregate), limestone (cement), clay (bricks), building stone. Used in huge tonnages, so transport cost matters and they are dug close to where they are used.

### 3.2.3.2 Processes that concentrate deposits

#### What you need to know

Hydrothermal deposition; metamorphic processes; Proterozoic marine sediments; physical sedimentary processes; biological sedimentary processes.

#### Explained

| Process | How it concentrates | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrothermal | Hot water circulating near a magma body dissolves metals and deposits them as it cools, in veins and fractures | Cornish tin and copper veins; lead-zinc veins |
| Metamorphic | Heat and pressure recrystallise rock, forming new useful minerals | Marble, slate, garnet, graphite |
| Proterozoic marine sediments | Iron dissolved in oxygen-poor oceans precipitated when oxygen rose | Banded iron formations, the main world iron ore |
| Physical sedimentary (placers) | Dense, hard, chemically resistant minerals are concentrated by flowing water where it slows | Gold, tin (cassiterite) and diamonds in river and beach sands |
| Biological sedimentary | Organisms accumulate elements | Limestone from shells; phosphate deposits; coal from plants |

Evaporation of sea water also concentrates salts (evaporites: halite, gypsum, potash).

### 3.2.3.3 Reserves and resources; Lasky's principle

#### What you need to know

- Definitions of resource and reserve.
- Lasky's principle: as ore grade falls, the amount of ore available rises roughly logarithmically.

#### Explained

- **Resource**: the total amount of a mineral in the crust that could possibly be extracted, now or in future, including deposits not yet found or not yet economic.
- **Reserve**: the part of the resource that is known and can be extracted profitably with current technology and prices.

Reserves change: a higher price, better technology or new discoveries turn resources into reserves; falling prices or new regulations turn reserves back into resources.

**Lasky's principle**: for many metals, the total tonnage of ore increases geometrically as the minimum grade decreases. In practice, halving the cut-off grade might multiply available ore several times. So reserves can grow when lower grades become economic. But lower grade means more rock moved, more energy and more waste per tonne of metal.

#### Worked example

An ore contains 0.5% copper. To produce 1 tonne of copper, 1 ÷ 0.005 = 200 tonnes of ore must be mined and processed (ignoring losses). At 0.25%, 400 tonnes are needed: double the waste and energy.

### 3.2.3.4 Exploration techniques

#### What you need to know

Satellite imagery; seismic surveys; gravimetry; magnetometry; resistivity; trial drilling.

#### Explained

| Technique | Principle | Detects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Satellite imagery | Different rocks and minerals reflect different wavelengths; lineaments show faults | Rock types, altered zones, fractures |
| Seismic surveys | Sound waves reflect from boundaries between layers of different density and velocity | Structure, layering, traps |
| Gravimetry | Dense ore bodies give a stronger gravitational field (positive anomaly); low-density rocks (salt) give a negative one | Dense metal ores, salt domes |
| Magnetometry | Magnetic minerals distort Earth's magnetic field | Magnetite, iron ores, some igneous bodies |
| Resistivity | Current passed into the ground; metal sulfides conduct well (low resistivity) | Sulfide ores, groundwater |
| Trial drilling | Cores taken and analysed | Confirms grade, depth and size |

Remote and geophysical methods are cheap per square kilometre and narrow down targets; drilling is expensive but is the only way to prove a reserve.

### 3.2.3.5 Mine viability

#### What you need to know

Ore purity and cut-off grade; chemical form; overburden and hydrology; economics and mining costs.

#### Explained

- **Grade and cut-off grade**: the cut-off grade is the minimum grade worth processing. It depends on metal price and costs.
- **Chemical form**: some compounds are easier to process than others (oxides are often easier to smelt than sulfides; some forms need expensive chemical treatment).
- **Overburden**: the rock above the ore. A high overburden-to-ore ratio raises the cost of open-pit mining.
- **Hydrology**: water flooding a mine must be pumped out, adding cost; it may also become polluted.
- **Economics**: metal price, transport distance, energy and labour costs, political stability, taxes and the cost of restoration.

### 3.2.3.6 Environmental impacts of mining and their control

#### What you need to know

Turbid drainage water; spoil; leachate (and neutralisation); site management; restoration.

#### Explained

| Impact | Control |
| --- | --- |
| Turbid (muddy) drainage water reduces light in rivers and smothers riverbed habitats | Settlement lagoons before discharge |
| Spoil heaps: unstable, ugly, may slide | Compacting, landscaping, vegetating; stable slopes |
| Leachate: acidic water carrying dissolved metals from spoil | Collection; neutralising with lime; wetlands |
| Noise, dust, traffic | Screening banks, damping roads, working hours |
| Habitat loss | Restoration: backfilling, topsoil replacement, planting, creating lakes and nature reserves |

Acid mine drainage is covered in [17](17-oil-pesticides-nutrients-and-metals.md).

### 3.2.3.7 Securing future supplies

#### What you need to know

- Improved exploration including remote sensing; bioleaching with acidophilic bacteria; phytomining; Cradle to Cradle design.
- Advantages and difficulties of recycling.

#### Explained

- **Bioleaching**: acid-loving bacteria oxidise sulfide minerals, releasing metal ions (for example copper) into a solution from which the metal is recovered. Works on low-grade ore and waste with little energy.
- **Phytomining**: plants (hyperaccumulators) grown on low-grade ore or contaminated land take up metals into their tissue; the plants are harvested and burned, and metal is extracted from the ash.
- **Cradle to Cradle design**: products are designed from the start so that at end of life all materials can be recovered and reused, not thrown away ("cradle to grave").

**Recycling**:

| Advantages | Difficulties |
| --- | --- |
| Conserves finite reserves | Identifying and separating materials in mixed products |
| Less energy than extracting from ore (aluminium recycling uses about 5% of the energy) | Quality loss (contamination of steel with copper; downcycling) |
| Less mining damage and waste | Collection and transport cost |
| Less landfill | Needs consumer cooperation |

#### Common mistakes

- Using "reserve" and "resource" as if they mean the same thing.
- Saying recycling has no environmental cost. Collection and processing use energy.

## 3.2.4 Biogeochemical cycles

Elements that living things need are recycled between the atmosphere, oceans, rocks, soil and organisms. Human activities speed up some flows and create new ones. The biology of the nitrogen and phosphorus cycles is also in [A-level Biology 17](../../biology/17-energy-in-ecosystems-and-nutrient-cycles.md).

### 3.2.4.1 Why cycles matter

#### What you need to know

Many elements are scarce in available forms, so natural systems recycle and reuse them.

#### Explained

Only a small part of each element is in a form organisms can use (for example, nitrogen gas is 78% of the air, but most organisms cannot use N₂). Natural systems therefore reuse the same atoms repeatedly: decomposers return them from dead matter to soil and water. Human systems often take elements from a store and release them as waste in the wrong place, causing both depletion and pollution.

### 3.2.4.2 The carbon cycle

#### What you need to know

- Processes affected by humans: photosynthesis, aerobic and anaerobic respiration, combustion, exchange of CO₂ between air and sea, movement of biomass, changes in stores, dissolved CO₂ and carbonic acid, methane in the atmosphere.
- Sustainable management: alternatives to fossil fuels, carbon sequestration, carbon capture and storage (CCS), matching afforestation to deforestation, raising soil organic matter, conserving peat bogs.

#### Explained

**Stores**: rocks (limestone, fossil fuels) hold by far the most carbon; then the deep ocean; then soils, vegetation and the atmosphere.

**Human effects**:

- Combustion of fossil fuels moves carbon from a long-term rock store to the atmosphere in decades rather than millions of years.
- Deforestation reduces photosynthesis and releases carbon from trees and soil.
- Draining peat bogs lets oxygen in, so aerobic decomposition releases CO₂.
- Rice paddies, landfill and livestock increase anaerobic respiration, releasing methane.
- The oceans absorb about a quarter of emitted CO₂. CO₂ dissolves and forms carbonic acid (CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃), lowering ocean pH (ocean acidification), which harms shell-building organisms.

**Management**:

| Method | How it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Renewable and nuclear energy | Less fossil carbon released |
| Afforestation (at least matching deforestation) | Trees store carbon as they grow |
| Carbon capture and storage | CO₂ captured from power stations and pumped into porous rocks underground (old gas fields, saline aquifers) |
| Raising soil organic matter | Less ploughing, cover crops, adding compost store carbon in soil |
| Conserving and re-wetting peat bogs | Waterlogging stops decomposition so carbon stays stored |

### 3.2.4.3 The nitrogen cycle

#### What you need to know

- Human effects: the Haber process; land drainage; growing legumes; sewage and eutrophication; NOx from combustion; decomposition and ammonification.
- Consequences: eutrophication, climate change (N₂O), toxicity of NOx, photochemical smog.
- Management: less combustion, using natural nitrogen fixation instead of Haber fertilisers, biological waste management, reducing nitrate leaching.

#### Explained

Natural cycle in brief: nitrogen-fixing bacteria (free-living and in legume root nodules) and lightning convert N₂ to ammonium or nitrate; plants absorb nitrate and ammonium; decomposers turn dead matter into ammonium (ammonification); nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonium to nitrite then nitrate; denitrifying bacteria in waterlogged soils turn nitrate back to N₂.

**Human effects**:

- The Haber process fixes about as much nitrogen as all natural land fixation, as fertiliser.
- Land drainage aerates soil, reducing denitrification and increasing nitrification, so more nitrate.
- Growing legumes adds fixed nitrogen.
- Sewage and manure add ammonium and organic nitrogen to rivers.
- High-temperature combustion joins N₂ and O₂ to make NOx.

**Consequences**:

- Eutrophication: nitrate (and phosphate) washed into water cause algal blooms; when the algae die, decomposing bacteria use up oxygen; fish and invertebrates die.
- N₂O from fertilised soils is a greenhouse gas and also depletes stratospheric ozone.
- NOx irritates lungs and forms acid rain and photochemical smog with hydrocarbons in sunlight.

**Management**: lower combustion temperatures and catalytic converters; legumes and crop rotation rather than synthetic fertiliser; composting and anaerobic digestion of wastes; applying fertiliser in the right amount at the right time; buffer strips beside rivers; keeping soil covered in winter to stop nitrate leaching.

### 3.2.4.4 The phosphorus cycle

#### What you need to know

- Mobilisation of phosphate rock as fertiliser; eutrophication (with nitrates); phosphate as a limiting factor.
- Management: biological waste as fertiliser, breeding crops that use phosphate efficiently, mycorrhizal fungi.

#### Explained

The phosphorus cycle has no significant gas phase. Phosphate is released very slowly by weathering of rocks, taken up by plants, passed along food chains, and returned by decomposition. Much ends up in ocean sediments, which become rock again only over millions of years.

Phosphate is often the **limiting factor** for plant and algal growth in fresh water, because natural supplies are so low. So even small additions (from fertiliser run-off, sewage and detergents) cause eutrophication.

Humans mine phosphate rock, which is finite and concentrated in a few countries, to make fertiliser.

**Management**: recycling phosphate from manure, sewage sludge and food waste; breeding crops that take up phosphate more efficiently; encouraging mycorrhizal fungi, which extend the root system and absorb phosphate for the plant; removing phosphate at sewage works (by precipitation with iron salts).

#### Worked example

A river carries 2.5 mg dm⁻³ of nitrate with a flow of 3.0 m³ s⁻¹. Mass per second = 2.5 mg dm⁻³ × 3000 dm³ s⁻¹ = 7500 mg s⁻¹ = 7.5 g s⁻¹. Per day: 7.5 × 86 400 = 648 000 g ≈ 650 kg of nitrate.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying algae use up the oxygen in eutrophication. Bacteria decomposing dead algae do.
- Giving phosphorus an atmospheric stage.

## 3.2.5 Soils

Soil forms from weathered rock, organic matter, water and air, over hundreds to thousands of years per centimetre. It supports almost all food production, so damaging it is effectively using up a non-renewable resource. Soil sampling methods (texture, water, organic matter, pH, bulk density) are in [23](23-research-methods-and-fieldwork.md) (ST3).

### 3.2.5.1 Human effects on soil fertility

#### What you need to know

How ploughing and drainage (aeration), nutrient addition, irrigation, compaction (bulk density) and pH control change fertility.

#### Explained

| Practice | Effect on fertility |
| --- | --- |
| Ploughing | Aerates soil, kills weeds, buries residues. But speeds decomposition of organic matter, damages soil structure and soil organisms, and exposes soil to erosion |
| Drainage | Removes waterlogging, so roots get oxygen and aerobic decomposers work; soils warm faster in spring. Can dry wetlands and release carbon |
| Nutrient addition | Fertilisers and manure replace nutrients removed by crops. Excess leaches and pollutes water; organic manures also improve structure |
| Irrigation | Supplies water in dry areas. In hot climates, evaporation leaves salts at the surface (salinisation), which harms crops |
| Compaction | Heavy machinery and trampling squash pores, increasing bulk density. Less air and water infiltration, roots cannot penetrate, more run-off |
| pH control | Liming (adding calcium carbonate) raises the pH of acid soils, making nutrients available and helping soil organisms |

**Bulk density** = dry mass of soil ÷ total volume (including pores). A higher value means more compaction and fewer pores.

#### Worked example

A soil core of volume 250 cm³ has a dry mass of 400 g. Bulk density = 400 ÷ 250 = 1.6 g cm⁻³. If the mineral particle density is 2.65 g cm⁻³, porosity = 1 − (1.6 ÷ 2.65) = 0.40, so 40% pore space.

### 3.2.5.2 Soil degradation and erosion

#### What you need to know

- Erosion by rain splash, wind blow and surface run-off.
- Features that protect soil: vegetation cover, organic matter, infiltration rate.
- The Universal Soil Loss Equation (USLE).
- Human causes: ploughing vulnerable soils, removing vegetation, overgrazing, compaction, cultivating steep slopes.
- Impacts: loss of productivity, sedimentation, downstream flooding, coastal sedimentation, airborne particulates, desertification, landslides.

#### Explained

**Erosion processes**:

- Rain splash: raindrops hit bare soil, detaching particles and throwing them downslope.
- Surface run-off: water that cannot infiltrate flows over the surface, carrying soil in sheets, rills and gullies.
- Wind blow: strong wind lifts dry, fine, loose particles, especially from bare, flat fields.

**Protective features**: vegetation intercepts rain and binds soil with roots; organic matter binds particles into stable crumbs (aggregates) and holds water; high infiltration rates reduce run-off.

**USLE**: A = R × K × LS × C × P

| Factor | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| A | Predicted average annual soil loss |
| R | Rainfall erosivity (intensity and amount of rain) |
| K | Soil erodibility (texture, structure, organic matter) |
| LS | Slope length and steepness |
| C | Cover and management (vegetation, crop type) |
| P | Support practices (contour ploughing, terracing) |

Each factor is a number from tables; lowering any factor (more cover, contour ploughing) reduces predicted loss. It is an empirical model for average loss, not single storms.

**Impacts**:

- On site: loss of fertile topsoil, lower yields, more fertiliser needed.
- Off site: sediment fills reservoirs and rivers (sedimentation), raising flood risk; muddy water harms aquatic life; coastal sediment smothers coral reefs; dust storms carry particulates that harm health; in dry areas, degradation leads to desertification; on slopes, saturated soils may slide (landslides).

### 3.2.5.3 Reducing soil erosion

#### What you need to know

Long-term crops; contour ploughing; tied ridging; terracing; windbreaks; multicropping; strip cropping; mulching; increasing organic matter.

#### Explained

| Method | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Long-term crops (trees, perennials) | Soil covered all year; roots bind soil |
| Contour ploughing | Furrows run across the slope, so they hold water instead of channelling it downhill |
| Tied ridging | Ridges along the contour with cross ties, making small basins that trap water |
| Terracing | Steep slopes cut into flat steps, slowing run-off |
| Windbreaks (hedges, tree lines) | Reduce wind speed near the ground |
| Multicropping | Growing more than one crop at once, so the ground is better covered |
| Strip cropping | Bands of different crops (for example a row crop between strips of grass) across the slope; the dense strips catch soil |
| Mulching | Covering soil with straw or residues to absorb raindrop impact, reduce evaporation and add organic matter |
| Increasing organic matter | Compost and manure build stable aggregates and improve infiltration |

#### Common mistakes

- Saying ploughing always improves soil. It also speeds organic matter loss and erosion.
- Confusing the USLE factors; remember C and P are the ones farmers control directly.

#### Practical

Investigating the effect of slope and vegetation on rain-splash erosion: place trays of soil (bare and with plant cover) at set angles; drip water from a fixed height; collect splashed soil on paper around the tray and weigh it after drying. Repeat for several angles; control drop height, soil type, moisture and volume of water.

## Energy and society, features of energy resources and sustainability

Cheap, plentiful energy shaped modern society. This file covers why energy matters, the features that decide how useful a resource is, and the environmental costs of the way we use energy now. AQA expects quantitative comparison between resources.

### 3.3.1 Energy and the development of society

#### What you need to know

How energy supplies affected agriculture (mechanisation), fishing, industry, service industries, water supply and treatment, transport and domestic living standards.

#### Explained

- **Agriculture**: tractors, combine harvesters and irrigation pumps replaced human and animal labour, so a small workforce feeds many. Energy also makes fertiliser (Haber process) and pesticides.
- **Fishing**: powered boats travel further and haul larger nets; refrigeration keeps catches fresh, increasing catches (and overfishing).
- **Industry**: steam, then electricity, allowed factories, mass production and smelting of metals.
- **Service industries**: offices, computing, hospitals and communications all need reliable electricity.
- **Water supply and treatment**: pumping, treatment and sewage disposal improved public health dramatically.
- **Transport**: railways, cars, ships and aircraft let people and goods move cheaply, enabling trade and cities.
- **Domestic living**: heating, lighting, refrigeration, washing machines and cooking appliances raised living standards and freed time.

### 3.3.2 Features of energy resources

#### What you need to know

Abundance; energy density; locational constraints; intermittency; the need to convert to a form usable at the point of use.

#### Explained

| Feature | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Abundance | How much is available | Solar input vastly exceeds human use; fossil fuels finite |
| Energy density | Energy per unit mass or volume | Uranium very high; oil and coal high; wood lower; wind and solar low per unit area |
| Locational constraints | Where it can be exploited | Tidal power needs a large tidal range; geothermal needs hot rocks; HEP needs height and rainfall |
| Intermittency | Whether supply varies uncontrollably | Wind and solar vary with weather and time; fossil and nuclear are available on demand |
| Conversion | Whether it must be changed into another form for use | Most primary sources are converted to electricity or fuels; each conversion loses energy |

High energy density makes fuels easy to store and transport, which is why oil dominates transport. Low-density, intermittent sources need large areas and storage.

#### Worked example

Petrol has an energy density of about 46 MJ kg⁻¹; a lithium-ion battery about 0.6 MJ kg⁻¹. Mass of battery storing the same energy as 40 kg of petrol: 40 × 46 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 3070 kg. In practice electric motors are about three times more efficient than petrol engines, so the useful energy gap is smaller (about 1000 kg), but batteries remain much heavier.

### 3.3.3 Sustainability of current energy use

#### What you need to know

- Impacts before use: extracting fuel, processing it, making equipment, developing and operating sites, transport, embodied energy.
- Impacts from use: air pollution from fossil fuels, oil pollution, radioactive waste, noise from wind turbines, thermal pollution from steam turbines.
- Habitat damage: from extraction, siting of stations and equipment, tidal and HEP schemes, pipelines and cables.
- Depletion of non-renewable reserves.

#### Explained

**Before use**:

- Extraction: open-cast coal mines, oil wells, uranium mines disturb land and produce waste.
- Processing: refining oil, enriching uranium and making fuel rods use energy and create pollutants.
- Equipment manufacture: wind turbines need steel, concrete and rare-earth magnets; solar panels need silicon purified at high temperature.
- Site development and operation: roads, clearing land, construction traffic.
- Transport of fuel: tankers, pipelines and trains use energy and risk spills.
- **Embodied energy**: all the energy used to make, transport and build something. A renewable device must generate more than its embodied energy to give a net gain. Energy payback time = embodied energy ÷ annual output.

**From use**:

- Fossil fuel combustion releases CO₂, SO₂, NOx, particulates and CO.
- Oil spills harm marine life.
- Nuclear power produces radioactive waste that must be isolated for thousands of years.
- Wind turbines make noise and can kill birds and bats.
- Thermal power stations release warm cooling water (thermal pollution), lowering dissolved oxygen in rivers.

**Habitat damage**: from mines and wells; from building large stations; tidal barrages change estuary mudflats and block fish movement; HEP dams flood valleys and block fish migration; pipelines and cables cut through habitats.

**Depletion**: fossil fuels and uranium are finite. Reserves-to-production ratio (years left at current use) = reserves ÷ annual production; it changes as new reserves are found and use changes.

#### Worked example

A wind turbine has embodied energy of 3.0 TJ and produces 6.0 GWh per year. 6.0 GWh = 6.0 × 10⁹ × 3600 J = 2.16 × 10¹³ J = 21.6 TJ. Payback time = 3.0 ÷ 21.6 ≈ 0.14 years, about 7 weeks.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying renewable energy has no environmental impact. Manufacture, siting and habitat effects still matter.
- Mixing up energy (J, kWh) and power (W).

## 3.3.4.1 Securing future energy supplies

Future supply depends on getting more from existing resources, developing new technologies, and matching supply to demand with storage. For each resource, know how the technology works, its advantages and its problems.

### Improved technologies by resource

#### What you need to know

- Fossil fuels: secondary and tertiary oil recovery, directional drilling, oil shale and tar sands, CCS, hydraulic fracturing (fracking), coal gasification and liquefaction, methane hydrates.
- Nuclear: fission (better uranium extraction, plutonium and thorium reactors); fusion (toroidal and laser).
- Solar: photothermal, heat pumps, photovoltaic, multi-junction cells, anti-reflective surfaces, concentrating solar power.
- HEP: low-head and helical turbines.
- Wind: new horizontal-axis and vertical-axis turbines; wind-assisted ships.
- Wave power.
- Biofuels: crops, hydrogen from algae, microorganisms.
- Geothermal: low-temperature fluids, district heating.
- Tidal: barrages, lagoons, in-stream turbines.

#### Explained

**Fossil fuels**

- Primary recovery uses the natural pressure in the reservoir, getting about 10 to 20% of the oil. Secondary recovery pumps water or gas in to push more oil out. Tertiary (enhanced) recovery lowers viscosity, for example by injecting steam, CO₂ or detergents. Together these can raise recovery towards 50% or more.
- Directional drilling: the well is steered at an angle or horizontally, so one platform reaches many parts of a field, or a well runs along a thin reservoir layer.
- Oil shale and tar sands: rocks with solid or very viscous hydrocarbons. They are mined or heated in place; this needs a lot of energy and water and causes major habitat damage.
- CCS: capture CO₂ from flue gases and store it in porous rocks (see [10](10-biogeochemical-cycles.md)).
- Fracking: water with sand and chemicals is pumped at high pressure into shale to open fractures; sand keeps them open so gas flows to a horizontal well. Concerns: water use, contamination, small earthquakes, methane leaks.
- Coal gasification turns coal into a gas fuel (carbon monoxide and hydrogen), cleaner to burn and easier to capture CO₂ from; liquefaction makes liquid fuels from coal.
- Methane hydrates: methane trapped in ice crystals on continental slopes and in permafrost; a huge resource but hard and risky to extract.

**Nuclear**

- Fission: uranium-235 nuclei split, releasing heat. Improved extraction (including from seawater) increases resources. Fast breeder reactors make plutonium-239 from the common uranium-238, extending fuel many times. Thorium reactors use thorium-232, which is more abundant and produces less long-lived waste.
- Fusion: light nuclei (deuterium and tritium) join, as in the Sun. Toroidal reactors (tokamaks) hold a hot plasma in a doughnut-shaped magnetic field; laser fusion compresses fuel pellets with powerful lasers. Fuel is abundant and waste is less long-lived, but a power station that gives more energy than it uses has not yet been built.

**Solar**

- Photothermal (solar thermal): panels heat water directly.
- Heat pumps move heat from the air, ground or water into a building, using electricity; they give out about three to four times more heat than electricity used.
- Photovoltaic (PV): semiconductor cells convert light to electricity. Multi-junction cells stack layers that absorb different wavelengths, so efficiency is higher. Anti-reflective coatings reduce light lost by reflection.
- Concentrating solar power: mirrors focus sunlight to heat a fluid that drives a turbine; molten-salt storage lets it run after dark.

**HEP**: low-head turbines generate from small height differences (weirs, rivers) without large dams; helical turbines work in flowing water at low speed.

**Wind**: larger horizontal-axis turbines (HAWT) with longer blades capture more energy (power is proportional to swept area and to wind speed cubed); floating offshore turbines reach deep water. Vertical-axis turbines (VAWT) work with wind from any direction and suit turbulent urban sites. Wind-assisted ships use sails or rotors to cut fuel use.

**Wave power**: devices convert the up-and-down or back-and-forth motion of waves into electricity. Problems: storm damage, corrosion, maintenance offshore.

**Biofuels**: ethanol from fermenting sugar or starch crops, and biodiesel from oil crops; hydrogen produced by some algae; microorganisms that make biogas (methane) from waste by anaerobic digestion. Problems: land competing with food, habitat clearance.

**Geothermal**: hot rocks heat water underground. Low-temperature fluids, unsuitable for electricity, can still supply district heating (hot water piped to many buildings). Sites are limited; some release H₂S or CO₂.

**Tidal**:

- Barrage: a dam across an estuary; water passes through turbines on the ebb (and flood) tide. Predictable but very costly and changes estuary habitats.
- Lagoon: a wall encloses part of the coast, with less effect on the whole estuary.
- In-stream turbines: underwater turbines in fast tidal currents, like underwater wind turbines.

#### Worked example

Wind power ∝ v³. If wind speed rises from 8 m s⁻¹ to 10 m s⁻¹, power changes by (10 ÷ 8)³ = 1.95, nearly double.

### Supply, demand and storage

#### What you need to know

- Causes of fluctuation: weather, season, weekday and weekend, 24-hour cycle, short-term peaks (for example mealtimes, television breaks).
- Storage: pumped-storage HEP (peak shaving), rechargeable batteries, fuel cells, compressed gas, thermal storage, vehicle-to-grid (V2G), power-to-gas (P2G), the hydrogen economy.

#### Explained

Electricity must be generated at the same moment it is used. Demand peaks on winter evenings, falls at night and at weekends, and has sudden short peaks (many kettles switched on at the end of a popular programme). Wind and solar output varies with weather and season.

| Storage | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Pumped storage | Cheap off-peak electricity pumps water uphill; at peak times it flows down through turbines. Starts in seconds; "peak shaving" |
| Rechargeable batteries | Large lithium-ion arrays store surplus and release it quickly |
| Fuel cells | Hydrogen and oxygen react to give electricity, with water as waste |
| Compressed gas | Air compressed into caverns, released through turbines |
| Thermal storage | Heat stored in molten salt, water or rock |
| Vehicle-to-grid (V2G) | Parked electric cars return power to the grid at peak times |
| Power-to-gas (P2G) | Surplus electricity electrolyses water to make hydrogen (or methane) for storage |
| Hydrogen economy | Hydrogen used widely as a fuel for transport, heating and industry, made from renewable electricity |

#### Common mistakes

- Saying pumped storage generates energy. It stores it and loses about 20 to 30% in the round trip.
- Saying fusion is used in power stations today.

## 3.3.4.2 Energy conservation

The cheapest and cleanest energy is energy not used. Conservation means doing the same job with less energy (efficiency) or avoiding waste. This file covers transport, buildings and industry, including the electricity grid.

### 3.3.4.2.1 Transport

#### What you need to know

- Vehicle design: aerodynamic and hydrodynamic shape, low mass, tyre design, kinetic energy recovery (KERS, regenerative braking), low embodied-energy materials, bulk transport.
- Infrastructure and management: integrated road, rail and cycle networks; Active Traffic Management and smart motorways.
- End-of-life design: recyclable materials, easy identification, easy dismantling.

#### Explained

**Vehicle design**:

- Aerodynamics: smooth shapes reduce air resistance. Drag force rises with the square of speed, so the effect is greatest at high speed. Ships use hull shapes (hydrodynamics) and coatings to reduce water drag.
- Low mass: aluminium, composites and high-strength steel reduce the energy needed to accelerate and climb.
- Tyres: low rolling-resistance tyres and correct inflation reduce energy lost as heat.
- KERS and regenerative braking: instead of turning kinetic energy into heat in the brakes, a motor acts as a generator and charges a battery (or spins a flywheel); the energy is reused for acceleration.
- Low embodied-energy materials reduce energy used before the vehicle is driven.
- Bulk transport: one train, bus or ship carries many passengers or tonnes, so energy per passenger-km or tonne-km is much lower than for cars or lorries.

**Infrastructure**: integrated networks let people combine cycling, buses and trains (park-and-ride, cycle lanes, through ticketing). Active Traffic Management on smart motorways uses variable speed limits and opens the hard shoulder at busy times, smoothing flow so less stop-start driving and fuel waste.

**End of life**: designing vehicles so parts are easy to take apart, labelled by material, and made of recyclable materials, so materials (and their embodied energy) are recovered.

### 3.3.4.2.2 Buildings

#### What you need to know

- Design: passive solar orientation, low surface-area-to-volume ratio, high thermal mass.
- Low embodied-energy materials: rammed earth, limecrete, straw, earth-sheltered buildings.
- Low thermal transmittance (U-value): double and triple glazing, low-emissivity glass, vacuum or inert-gas glazing, wall, floor and roof insulation.
- Energy management: occupancy sensors, insulation, automatic or solar-powered ventilation, heat exchangers.
- Low-energy appliances: CFL and LED lighting, low-energy white goods.

#### Explained

**Design**:

- Passive solar: large windows facing the Sun (south in the UK) let in solar heat in winter; overhangs shade them from the high summer Sun.
- Low surface-area-to-volume ratio: compact shapes (and terraced houses or flats sharing walls) lose less heat.
- High thermal mass: dense materials (stone, concrete, earth) absorb heat by day and release it at night, smoothing temperature swings.

**Low embodied-energy materials**: rammed earth (compacted soil); limecrete (lime-based concrete instead of cement concrete, which needs high-temperature kilns); straw bales (good insulation, renewable); earth-sheltered buildings (partly underground, so soil insulates and adds thermal mass).

**Thermal transmittance**: the U-value is the rate of heat loss through 1 m² of a structure for a 1 °C temperature difference (W m⁻² K⁻¹). Lower is better.

- Double and triple glazing trap layers of gas, which conduct heat poorly.
- Low-emissivity (low-e) glass has a thin metal-oxide coating that reflects long-wave IR back into the room.
- Vacuum glazing removes the gas completely; inert-gas glazing uses argon or krypton, which conduct heat less than air.
- Cavity wall, loft and floor insulation trap air in fibres or foams.

Heat loss rate = U × A × ΔT.

#### Worked example

A window of area 2.0 m² has a U-value of 4.8 W m⁻² K⁻¹ (single glazed). Inside is 20 °C and outside 5 °C. Heat loss = 4.8 × 2.0 × 15 = 144 W. Replacing it with triple glazing (U = 0.8) gives 0.8 × 2.0 × 15 = 24 W, a saving of 120 W (83%).

**Energy management**: occupancy sensors turn off lights and heating in empty rooms; automatic ventilation opens only as needed; solar-powered vents; heat exchangers (mechanical ventilation with heat recovery) warm incoming fresh air using outgoing stale air.

**Appliances**: LEDs use far less electricity than filament lamps for the same light; compact fluorescent lamps (CFLs) were an earlier step; A-rated fridges, washing machines and dishwashers use less energy.

### 3.3.4.2.3 Industry and electricity supply

#### What you need to know

- Heat management: storing bulk hot fluids, heat exchangers, combined heat and power (CHP).
- Electricity infrastructure: high-voltage grid, peak shaving and pumped storage, ICT to coordinate supply and demand, choosing locations for new generation.

#### Explained

**Heat management**: waste heat from one process is captured by heat exchangers and used to preheat inputs or warm buildings; hot fluids are stored in insulated tanks for later use. **CHP**: a power station's waste heat (which in a conventional station is about half the fuel energy, lost in cooling towers) is piped to nearby buildings or industry. Overall efficiency can rise from about 35 to 40% to 75 to 80%.

**Electricity infrastructure**:

- The grid transmits at very high voltage (for example 400 kV), so current is low and resistive losses (P = I²R) are small.
- Peak shaving and pumped storage meet short peaks without running inefficient extra stations.
- Smart grids use ICT to balance supply and demand: smart meters, time-of-use pricing, and switching flexible loads (fridges, car charging) to times of surplus.
- New generation sited near demand reduces transmission losses; CHP needs heat users nearby.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying insulation stops heat loss completely. It reduces the rate.
- Confusing U-value (heat loss rate, lower is better) with R-value (resistance, higher is better).

## Pollutant properties, environmental factors and control principles

Pollution is the release of a substance or energy that causes harm. How harmful a pollutant is depends on its own properties and on the environment it enters. This file gives the general ideas used for every pollutant in files [16](16-air-and-thermal-pollution.md), [17](17-oil-pesticides-nutrients-and-metals.md) and [18](18-solid-waste-noise-and-ionising-radiation.md).

### 3.4.1 Properties of pollutants

#### What you need to know

State of matter; form of energy; density; persistence and degradability; toxicity; reactivity; adsorption; solubility in lipids and in water; bioaccumulation; biomagnification; synergism; mutagenic, carcinogenic and teratogenic effects.

#### Explained

| Property | Meaning and why it matters | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| State | Gases and fine particles disperse widely in air; liquids flow and soak into soil; solids stay put | SO₂ gas spreads far; spoil stays local |
| Energy form | Pollution can be energy rather than material | Noise, heat, ionising radiation, light |
| Density | Decides whether it sinks, floats or rises | Oil floats; dense gases collect in hollows |
| Persistence / degradability | How long it lasts before breaking down; persistent pollutants build up | DDT, CFCs, plastics, heavy metals persist |
| Toxicity | The dose that causes harm; often given as LD50 (dose killing 50% of a test population) | Mercury compounds very toxic |
| Reactivity | Reactive pollutants change into other substances, sometimes worse ones (secondary pollutants) | NOx forms ozone in sunlight |
| Adsorption | Sticking to surfaces of particles such as clay or organic matter; reduces mobility | Metal ions on clay |
| Lipid solubility | Dissolves in fats, so stored in fatty tissue instead of excreted | DDT, methylmercury |
| Water solubility | Dissolves in water, so spreads in rivers and groundwater; usually excreted | Nitrate |
| Bioaccumulation | Concentration builds up in an organism over its life because intake exceeds loss | Mercury in fish |
| Biomagnification | Concentration increases at each trophic level along a food chain | DDT in birds of prey |
| Synergism | Two pollutants together cause more harm than the sum of their separate effects | SO₂ and particulates in smog |
| Mutagenic | Causes mutations in DNA | Ionising radiation, benzene |
| Carcinogenic | Causes cancer | Asbestos, some hydrocarbons |
| Teratogenic | Causes birth defects in developing embryos | Methylmercury |

#### Worked example

Biomagnification: water contains 0.00005 ppm of a pesticide; zooplankton 0.04 ppm; small fish 0.5 ppm; large fish 2.0 ppm; fish-eating birds 25 ppm. Concentration factor from water to birds = 25 ÷ 0.00005 = 500 000. From large fish to birds the factor is 12.5.

### 3.4.2.1 Dispersal

#### What you need to know

Point sources and diffuse sources; the speed and direction of air and water currents.

#### Explained

- **Point source**: a single identifiable outlet, such as a factory chimney or sewage pipe. Easier to monitor and control.
- **Diffuse (non-point) source**: many small sources spread over a large area, such as fertiliser run-off from farms or vehicle exhausts. Harder to control.
- Strong winds and fast currents dilute and carry pollutants away (lower concentration locally but spread further). Still air and slow water let concentrations build up. Prevailing wind direction decides who is downwind.

### 3.4.2.2 Degradation and other environmental factors

#### What you need to know

How severity is affected by temperature, light, oxygen, pH, other chemicals, temperature inversions and adsorbent materials.

#### Explained

| Factor | Effect | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | Higher temperature speeds microbial decomposition | Sewage breaks down faster in summer |
| Light | Drives photochemical reactions; can break pollutants down | Photochemical smog forms; some pesticides are broken down by UV |
| Oxygen | Needed for aerobic decay; low oxygen slows breakdown | Organic waste persists in stagnant water |
| pH | Alkaline rocks neutralise acids | Limestone areas are less affected by acid rain |
| Other chemicals | Reactions form secondary pollutants | Ozone oxidises SO₂ and NO₂ to acids |
| Temperature inversion | A layer of warm air over cold air stops vertical mixing, trapping pollutants near the ground | Winter smogs in valleys |
| Adsorbent materials | Clay and organic matter bind pollutants, reducing mobility and toxicity | Clay binds heavy metal ions |

**Temperature inversion**: normally air cools with height, so warm surface air rises and carries pollutants up. On still, clear nights the ground loses heat fast, cooling the air next to it; or cold air drains into a valley. Warmer air above acts as a lid. Pollutants build up until the Sun heats the ground or wind breaks the inversion.

### 3.4.3.1 Principles of control

#### What you need to know

- Critical Pathway Analysis (CPA): predicting how a pollutant moves through the environment.
- Critical Group Monitoring (CGM): monitoring the people most at risk.
- Location of emissions (avoiding valleys and enclosed water bodies).
- Timing of emissions (restricting activity during temperature inversions).

#### Explained

- **CPA**: trace the likely route of a pollutant from source to people and wildlife (air, water, soil, food chains) and identify the routes giving the highest exposure. Monitoring and controls then focus there. Example: a radioactive discharge into the sea may concentrate in seaweed and shellfish eaten by local people.
- **CGM**: identify and monitor the group with the greatest exposure (for example people who eat a lot of locally caught shellfish). If they are safe, everyone else should be safer.
- **Location**: release pollutants where they disperse well: tall chimneys above inversion layers; outfalls into fast-flowing water or open sea, not enclosed lakes or valleys.
- **Timing**: restrict burning, traffic or industrial releases during inversions and still weather; release effluent on the ebb tide so it is carried out to sea.

#### Common mistakes

- Using bioaccumulation and biomagnification interchangeably. One organism over time versus increasing concentration up food chains.
- Saying tall chimneys remove pollution. They disperse it, sometimes moving the problem elsewhere (acid rain downwind).

## Air pollutants and thermal pollution

For each pollutant, know its sources, its impacts and the technologies that control it. Controls work in three ways: reducing production, reducing release, or reducing the damage done.

### 3.4.3.2.1 Smoke and PM10

#### What you need to know

- Sources; impacts on breathing, albedo and smog formation with temperature inversions.
- Controls: Clean Air Act 1956, treating coal, electrostatic precipitators, cyclones, bag filters.

#### Explained

PM10 means particles smaller than 10 micrometres; they reach deep into the lungs. Sources: burning coal and wood, diesel engines, industry, construction dust.

Impacts: respiratory and heart disease; soot darkens buildings; dark particles on snow and ice lower albedo, increasing melting; particles act as nuclei for fog droplets, and during inversions form dense smog (the London smog of December 1952 killed thousands).

| Control | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Clean Air Act 1956 | Smoke control zones; only smokeless fuels allowed; taller chimneys for industry |
| Coal treatment | Smokeless fuel made by heating coal to remove volatile tar-forming compounds |
| Electrostatic precipitator | Particles in flue gas are given a negative charge and attracted to positive plates; plates are shaken to remove dust |
| Cyclone | Gas spun in a cone; heavier particles thrown to the wall and fall out |
| Bag filter | Gas passes through fabric bags that trap particles |

### 3.4.3.2.2 Acid precipitation

#### What you need to know

- Sources of SOx, NOx and ozone.
- Impacts on buildings, organisms (lakes, forests) and humans.
- Controls: fuel desulfurisation, wet and dry flue-gas desulfurisation, catalytic converters, urea sprays.

#### Explained

Burning fossil fuels containing sulfur makes SO₂. High-temperature combustion makes NOx. In the atmosphere, oxidation (helped by ozone) forms sulfuric and nitric acids, which fall as acid rain or are deposited dry.

**Impacts**:

- Buildings: limestone and marble (calcium carbonate) dissolve; metals corrode.
- Lakes: low pH kills fish eggs and invertebrates; aluminium is released from soils and damages fish gills.
- Forests: leaching of calcium and magnesium from soil; aluminium toxicity to roots; damage to needles.
- Humans: SO₂ and acid aerosols irritate airways.

**Controls**:

- Fuel desulfurisation: removing sulfur from oil and gas before burning.
- Wet flue-gas desulfurisation: flue gas sprayed with a slurry of crushed limestone; SO₂ reacts to form calcium sulfate (gypsum), which can be sold for plasterboard.
- Dry flue-gas desulfurisation: powdered lime injected into the gas.
- Catalytic converters on vehicles reduce NOx to N₂.
- Urea (or ammonia) sprays into flue gases convert NOx to N₂ and water.
- Liming lakes reduces damage, but treats the symptom.

### 3.4.3.2.3 Oxides of nitrogen

#### What you need to know

Sources (high-temperature combustion, fertilisers); photochemical smog and climate change; controls (catalytic converters, urea sprays, fertiliser management).

#### Explained

NOx forms when N₂ and O₂ combine at high temperature in engines and power stations; fertilised soils release N₂O. NO₂ splits in sunlight, releasing oxygen atoms that form tropospheric ozone; with hydrocarbons this gives photochemical smog (brown haze, eye and lung irritation, crop damage), worst on sunny, still days in cities. N₂O is a long-lived greenhouse gas.

Controls: catalytic converters; urea sprays; lower combustion temperatures; precise fertiliser use.

### 3.4.3.2.4 Hydrocarbons

#### What you need to know

Sources (unburnt fuel, fossil fuel extraction, solvents, aerosols); greenhouse effect (methane) and photochemical smog; controls (catalytic converters, efficient combustion, vapour collection and incineration, activated carbon).

#### Explained

Volatile hydrocarbons evaporate from fuel tanks and filling stations, escape from oil and gas wells and pipelines, and come from paints, solvents and aerosols. Unburnt fuel leaves exhausts. Methane is a greenhouse gas; other hydrocarbons react with NOx in sunlight to make ozone and smog.

Controls: catalytic converters oxidise hydrocarbons to CO₂ and water; efficient combustion; vapour recovery nozzles at filling stations; collecting and burning (flaring or incinerating) vapours at industrial sites; activated carbon filters adsorb vapours.

### 3.4.3.2.5 Carbon monoxide

#### What you need to know

Source (incomplete combustion); effect (binds to haemoglobin); controls (catalytic converters, efficient combustion).

#### Explained

CO forms when carbon fuels burn with too little oxygen, for example in cold engines, faulty boilers and stoves. CO binds to haemoglobin far more strongly than oxygen (forming carboxyhaemoglobin), so blood carries less oxygen, causing headaches, unconsciousness and death. Controls: catalytic converters oxidise CO to CO₂; good air supply and maintenance of boilers; CO alarms indoors.

**Catalytic converter**: a honeycomb coated with platinum, palladium and rhodium. A three-way converter does three jobs at once: oxidises CO to CO₂, oxidises hydrocarbons to CO₂ and water, and reduces NOx to N₂. It works only once hot, and does not reduce CO₂.

### 3.4.3.2.6 Thermal pollution

#### What you need to know

Effect of temperature on dissolved oxygen; cooling towers as a control.

#### Explained

Thermal power stations and some industries use river or sea water for cooling and return it warmer. Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen, while warmer animals respire faster and need more. Fish and invertebrates that need cool, oxygen-rich water (trout, mayfly larvae) decline; algae may increase. Sudden temperature changes when stations start or stop can kill organisms.

**Cooling towers** transfer waste heat to the air (mainly by evaporation) before water is returned, so the river temperature rises much less.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying catalytic converters reduce CO₂. They produce CO₂ from CO and hydrocarbons.
- Saying acid rain is caused by CO₂. The main causes are SO₂ and NOx.

## Oil, pesticides, nutrients, acid mine drainage and heavy metals

These pollutants mainly affect water, soil and food chains. Use the property terms from [15](15-pollutant-properties-and-control-principles.md) when explaining their effects.

### 3.4.3.2.7 Oil pollution

#### What you need to know

- Sources: disposal of waste oil, tanker tank washing, tanker and other shipping accidents, refinery spills, pipeline leaks, drilling.
- Effects: toxicity, asphyxiation, loss of insulation, food chain effects.
- Controls: recycling, maintenance, bund walls, double hulls, twin engines, inert-gas systems in tanks, recirculating washing water, GPS navigation, offshore routes, oil interceptors.
- Clean-up: booms, skimmers, absorbents, polymerising materials, dispersants, steam cleaning, bioremediation.

#### Explained

Most oil reaching the sea comes from many small routine releases (waste oil poured down drains, washing tanks at sea, run-off) rather than famous tanker disasters.

**Effects**: oil floats, forming a slick. Toxic light fractions kill plankton and larvae. Oil smothers shore organisms and coats gills (asphyxiation). It mats feathers and fur so birds and sea otters lose insulation and buoyancy, and die of cold; birds swallow oil while preening. Toxins pass along food chains.

**Prevention**:

| Control | How it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Recycling waste oil | Less dumped |
| Maintenance | Fewer leaks from pipelines and engines |
| Bund walls | Walls around storage tanks contain spills |
| Double hulls | An inner hull stays intact if the outer hull is holed |
| Twin engines | A ship can still steer if one fails |
| Inert-gas systems | Tanks filled with non-flammable gas, reducing explosion risk |
| Recirculating washing water | Oily water from tank washing kept aboard, not discharged |
| GPS navigation and offshore routes | Fewer groundings; accidents away from coasts |
| Oil interceptors | Tanks in drains where oil floats and is skimmed off before water leaves |

**Clean-up**:

- Booms: floating barriers that contain a slick.
- Skimmers: collect oil from the surface.
- Absorbents (straw, peat, synthetic pads) soak up oil.
- Polymerising materials turn oil into a rubbery solid that is easy to remove.
- Dispersants break oil into small droplets that mix into the water and are degraded faster, but they can be toxic themselves and spread oil through the water column.
- Steam cleaning of rocks removes oil but also kills shore organisms.
- Bioremediation: adding nutrients (or bacteria) to speed natural breakdown by oil-eating bacteria.

### 3.4.3.2.8 Pesticides

#### What you need to know

- Properties: toxicity, systemic or contact action, specificity, persistence, lipid solubility, bioaccumulation, biomagnification, mobility, synergism.
- Direct effects, effects of concentration through food chains, and indirect effects.
- Restrictions on organochlorines and organophosphates.

#### Explained

- Contact pesticides kill organisms they touch; systemic pesticides are absorbed and carried in the plant, so they kill insects feeding on it (and may reach pollen and nectar).
- Broad-spectrum (low specificity) pesticides kill non-target species, including predators of pests and pollinators.
- Persistent, lipid-soluble pesticides (organochlorines such as DDT) bioaccumulate and biomagnify. DDT in birds of prey (peregrine falcons, sparrowhawks) caused thin eggshells, breeding failure and population crashes.
- Mobile, water-soluble pesticides reach rivers and groundwater.
- Indirect effects: herbicides remove weeds that insects and birds feed on, so populations fall without being poisoned.
- Organochlorines are banned or severely restricted in most countries; organophosphates (toxic to the nervous system of humans and wildlife) are increasingly restricted. Neonicotinoids are discussed in [19](19-agriculture.md).

### 3.4.3.2.9 Nutrients

#### What you need to know

- Inorganic nutrients (nitrates, phosphates): eutrophication; methaemoglobinaemia ("blue baby syndrome") and possible carcinogenic effects of nitrate; controls (low-solubility fertilisers, Nitrate Vulnerable Zones, removing phosphate by precipitation with iron).
- Organic nutrients: sources (sewage, manure, silage effluent, wood and paper industry waste); deoxygenation; sewage treatment stages.

#### Explained

**Inorganic**: eutrophication is described in [10](10-biogeochemical-cycles.md). Nitrate in drinking water is converted in infants' guts to nitrite, which reduces blood's oxygen-carrying ability; nitrates may form carcinogenic nitrosamines. Controls: slow-release, low-solubility fertilisers; Nitrate Vulnerable Zones where farmers must follow rules on timing and amount of fertiliser and manure; precipitating phosphate at sewage works by adding iron salts.

**Organic**: organic matter is food for aerobic bacteria, which use oxygen as they decompose it. The **Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD)** measures this. High-BOD effluent (silage effluent is extremely high) deoxygenates rivers, killing fish and invertebrates. Downstream of a discharge there is an oxygen "sag" that recovers as the river reaerates.

**Sewage treatment**:

1. Preliminary treatment: screens remove large objects; grit traps let sand and grit settle.
2. Primary sedimentation: slow flow lets suspended solids settle as sludge.
3. Secondary (biological) treatment: aerobic microorganisms break down dissolved organic matter, either in activated sludge tanks (air bubbled through) or trickling filter beds (sewage sprayed over stones coated with microbes). Then secondary sedimentation removes the microbial sludge.
4. Tertiary treatment (where needed): phosphate removal by iron salts; microfilters; UV disinfection.
5. Sludge treatment: anaerobic digestion produces methane (biogas) for energy and a stable residue used as fertiliser.

### 3.4.3.2.10 Acid mine drainage

#### What you need to know

Oxidation of sulfides; low pH and mobilisation of metals; collection and neutralisation with lime.

#### Explained

Mining exposes sulfide minerals (especially pyrite, FeS₂) to air and water. They oxidise (helped by bacteria) to form sulfuric acid. The acid water dissolves toxic metals (iron, copper, zinc, cadmium, aluminium). When it reaches rivers, iron precipitates as orange ochre that smothers the bed, and acidity and metals kill life. It can continue for centuries after a mine closes, especially when pumping stops and old workings flood. Control: collect drainage, neutralise with lime (raising pH so metals precipitate), and settle the precipitate in lagoons; or pass water through constructed wetlands.

### 3.4.3.2.11 Heavy metals

#### What you need to know

- Lead: neurotoxicity, lipid solubility, bioaccumulation and biomagnification; uses that have been stopped; storing waste at high pH.
- Mercury: the same properties; its chemical form (elemental, inorganic or organic) affects its toxicity.

#### Explained

**Lead** damages the nervous system, especially in children (lower IQ, behaviour problems). Past sources: leaded petrol, lead paint, lead water pipes, lead shot and fishing weights. These uses have been phased out. Lead waste is stored at high pH, where lead compounds are insoluble and do not leach.

**Mercury**: elemental mercury vapour is toxic when breathed; inorganic mercury salts damage the kidneys; organic methylmercury, formed by bacteria in sediments, is the most dangerous. It is lipid-soluble, crosses the blood-brain barrier and the placenta (teratogenic), bioaccumulates and biomagnifies in fish. Minamata (Japan) in the 1950s is the classic example of poisoning from eating contaminated fish. Sources: coal burning, gold mining, industrial processes.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying sewage treatment removes nitrate in the secondary stage. Standard secondary treatment removes organic matter; nutrient removal is tertiary.
- Treating all mercury as equally toxic.

3. Explain why DDT caused population declines in birds of prey but not in the insects it was sprayed on. (4)
4. Describe the stages of sewage treatment that remove organic matter. (4)

### 3.4.3.2.12 Solid wastes

#### What you need to know

- Domestic waste: landfill, incineration, recycling, composting; evaluate each.
- Specialist wastes: high-, intermediate- and low-level radioactive waste (sealed storage, monitoring, encapsulation, absorbers, cooling); asbestos (sealed storage); cyanide (incineration).

#### Explained

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Landfill | Cheap; takes mixed waste; landfill gas (methane) can be captured for energy | Uses land; leachate can pollute groundwater; methane leaks; smell, pests; sites running out |
| Incineration | Reduces volume by about 90%; energy from waste; kills pathogens | Air pollutants (dioxins, particulates, CO₂); toxic ash; discourages recycling; high capital cost |
| Recycling | Saves resources and energy; less landfill | Needs sorting and collection; quality loss; markets fluctuate |
| Composting | Turns organic waste into soil improver; less methane than landfill | Needs separation; only organic waste; odours if badly managed |

The waste hierarchy (reduce, reuse, recycle, recover, dispose) ranks options; see [22](22-sustainability.md).

**Modern landfill design**: clay and plastic liners, leachate collection pipes and treatment, gas collection, daily cover with soil, capping and monitoring after closure.

**Radioactive waste**:

| Category | Source | Management |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High-level (HLW) | Spent fuel, reprocessing liquids; hot and highly radioactive | Cooled in water ponds for years; vitrified (made into glass); stored in steel containers; long-term plan is a deep geological repository |
| Intermediate-level (ILW) | Fuel cladding, reactor parts, resins | Encapsulated in cement in steel drums; stored; future deep disposal |
| Low-level (LLW) | Lightly contaminated clothing, tools, paper | Compacted and placed in near-surface engineered facilities |

Principles: sealed storage, absorbers (shielding with concrete, lead, water), cooling (for heat-producing waste), and continued monitoring.

**Asbestos**: fibres cause lung disease and cancer; wetted, double-bagged and sealed, buried in special landfill cells.

**Cyanide**: highly toxic; destroyed by high-temperature incineration (or chemical oxidation).

### 3.4.3.2.13 Noise

#### What you need to know

- Principles of sound: hearing range, the logarithmic decibel scale, loudness, threshold of hearing.
- Effects on objects (acoustic fatigue, shock waves) and on organisms (hearing damage, stress, behaviour, wildlife disturbance and feeding).
- Sources and controls for aircraft, railways, road traffic, industry and domestic noise.

#### Explained

Humans hear roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Sound intensity level is measured in decibels (dB), a logarithmic scale: dB = 10 log₁₀(I ÷ I₀), where I₀ is the threshold of hearing. An increase of 10 dB is a tenfold increase in intensity (and is heard as roughly twice as loud); +3 dB is a doubling of intensity. The dB(A) scale weights frequencies to match human hearing.

**Effects**: acoustic fatigue (repeated vibration cracks materials) and sonic booms (shock waves) can damage structures. In humans, prolonged noise above about 85 dB(A) damages hearing; noise also causes stress, sleep loss and poorer concentration. Wildlife: birds sing louder or at different times; noise masks predator or prey sounds; marine mammals are disturbed by ship noise and sonar; animals avoid noisy areas, losing feeding grounds.

| Source | Controls |
| --- | --- |
| Aircraft | Siting airports away from homes; insulating nearby homes; flight paths avoiding towns; minimum altitudes; night flight bans; quieter engine design (high-bypass turbofans) |
| Railways | Polishing track smooth; damping rails and wheels; fairings (shields) over wheels; lubricating curves; composite brake blocks that keep wheels smooth |
| Road traffic | Low-noise (porous) road surfaces; acoustic fences and earth banks; insulation of buildings; aerodynamic vehicle shapes |
| Industry | Ear protection; anti-vibration mats; limiting working times; quieter alternative processes |
| Domestic | Insulation between homes; volume limiters |

#### Worked example

Two identical machines each produce 80 dB. Together the intensity doubles, so the level = 80 + 10 log₁₀2 = 80 + 3 = 83 dB, not 160 dB.

### 3.4.3.2.14 Ionising radiation

#### What you need to know

- Uses: weapons, nuclear electricity, ship propulsion, manufacturing, healthcare, agriculture.
- Principles: half-life, Relative Biological Effectiveness (RBE), exposure versus contamination, activation products, becquerel, gray, sievert.
- Effects: free radicals, DNA damage, acute and chronic effects, somatic and genetic (gonadic) effects.
- Control: ALARA, BATNEEC, risk-benefit; closed sources, absorbers, inverse square law, reducing exposure time, monitoring workers.
- Environmental monitoring: CPA, sampling (dust, soil, water, seaweed, molluscs, fish, milk, vegetables, meat), CGM.

#### Explained

**Uses**: nuclear weapons; nuclear power; nuclear-powered submarines and ships; manufacturing (thickness gauges, checking welds, sterilising equipment); healthcare (X-rays, radiotherapy, tracers, sterilising); agriculture (irradiating food to kill bacteria, sterile-male insect release, tracers in fertiliser studies).

**Units and terms**:

- Half-life: the time for half the radioactive nuclei (or activity) in a sample to decay.
- Becquerel (Bq): activity, one decay per second.
- Gray (Gy): absorbed dose, one joule absorbed per kilogram.
- Sievert (Sv): equivalent or effective dose = absorbed dose × RBE (radiation weighting). Alpha has a high RBE (about 20) because it ionises densely; beta and gamma have an RBE of 1.
- Exposure: radiation from a source outside the body. Contamination: radioactive material on or inside the body (inhaled, swallowed); alpha emitters are most dangerous inside the body.
- Activation products: substances made radioactive by absorbing neutrons, for example in reactor steel.

**Effects**: radiation ionises water in cells, forming free radicals that damage DNA. Acute effects follow a large dose quickly (radiation sickness, burns, death). Chronic effects follow lower doses over time (cancer, cataracts). Somatic effects harm the exposed person; genetic (gonadic) effects damage sperm or egg cells and may affect offspring.

**Control**:

- ALARA: keep doses As Low As Reasonably Achievable.
- BATNEEC: Best Available Techniques Not Entailing Excessive Cost.
- Risk-benefit: a use is justified only if benefits outweigh risks (medical scans).
- Practical protection: closed (sealed) sources; absorbers (lead, concrete, water shielding); distance (gamma intensity falls with the inverse square of distance); short exposure time; film badges or dosimeters to monitor workers.

**Environmental monitoring**: CPA identifies pathways from a site to people (for example discharges to sea concentrating in seaweed and shellfish; deposition onto grass, then into milk); samples of dust, soil, water, seaweed, molluscs, fish, milk, vegetables and meat are measured regularly; CGM focuses on the most exposed people.

#### Worked example

A worker receives 2.0 mGy absorbed dose from gamma rays (RBE 1) and 0.1 mGy from alpha particles (RBE 20). Equivalent dose = 2.0 × 1 + 0.1 × 20 = 4.0 mSv.

Inverse square law: at 1 m a gamma dose rate is 40 µSv h⁻¹. At 4 m it is 40 ÷ 4² = 2.5 µSv h⁻¹.

### 3.4.3.3 New pollution control technologies

#### What you need to know

Prevention; monitoring impacts; leachate collection; satellite monitoring of oil spills; GPS tracking of ships; heavy metal adsorption by polymers; phytoremediation; bioremediation of hydrocarbons.

#### Explained

- Prevention: redesigning processes so pollutants are not made at all.
- Monitoring: continuous sensors and data loggers detect releases early.
- Leachate collection at landfill sites stops groundwater pollution.
- Satellites detect oil slicks (radar sees the calmer water surface) and identify illegal discharges; GPS (and automatic identification systems) tracks ships so polluters can be found.
- Polymer adsorbents bind heavy metal ions from water.
- Phytoremediation: plants take up metals or break down organic pollutants in soil.
- Bioremediation: bacteria break down hydrocarbons; adding nutrients and oxygen speeds it.

## 3.5.1 Agriculture

Agriculture replaces natural ecosystems with managed agroecosystems designed to channel as much energy as possible into a few useful species. This file covers how farmers control the agroecosystem, the energy and productivity of farming, its environmental impacts, the social and economic forces on it, and more sustainable methods. Energy transfer background is in [A-level Biology 17](../../biology/17-energy-in-ecosystems-and-nutrient-cycles.md).

### 3.5.1.1 Agroecosystems

#### What you need to know

- Choosing species to suit the environment and technology available.
- Controlling biotic factors: cultural pest control, predators, biological control, sterile males, pheromone traps, genetic resistance.
- Pesticide properties (toxicity, specificity, persistence, solubility, contact or systemic action); insecticide groups (organochlorines, organophosphates, pyrethroids, neonicotinoids).
- Antibiotics in livestock (growth promotion, prevention, treatment).
- Protecting pollinators; soil biota (detritivores, decomposers).
- Food chain energy losses and food conversion ratios (FCRs).
- Controlling abiotic factors: temperature, light, water, nutrients, topography and relief, pH, CO₂, soil fertility, salinity.

#### Explained

**Species selection**: crops and livestock are chosen for the local climate and soil and the machinery and inputs available (rice in flooded paddies; drought-tolerant sorghum in dry areas; sheep on steep upland pasture).

**Biotic control** (pests, weeds and diseases):

| Method | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Cultural control | Farming practice that disrupts pests: crop rotation, timing of sowing, removing crop residues |
| Natural predators | Encourage ladybirds, hoverflies, ground beetles with field margins and beetle banks |
| Biological control | Release a natural enemy (for example parasitic wasps against whitefly in glasshouses) |
| Sterile male technique | Release many males sterilised by radiation; females mate but lay infertile eggs |
| Pheromone traps | Sex attractants lure males to traps, or confuse mating |
| Genetic resistance | Breeding or GM crops that resist pests or disease |

**Pesticides**: properties as in [17](17-oil-pesticides-nutrients-and-metals.md). Groups:

- Organochlorines (DDT): persistent, lipid-soluble, biomagnify; now banned in most countries.
- Organophosphates: less persistent but highly toxic to nervous systems, including in humans.
- Pyrethroids: synthetic versions of a natural plant insecticide; low toxicity to mammals but very toxic to fish and aquatic insects.
- Neonicotinoids: systemic; taken into all tissues including pollen and nectar; linked to harm to bees, so outdoor use is restricted in the UK and EU.

**Antibiotics**: given to livestock to treat disease, to prevent it in crowded conditions, and in the past to speed growth. Overuse selects for resistant bacteria that can spread to humans. Growth promotion is banned in the UK and EU.

**Pollinators**: maintained by flower-rich margins and hedges, nest sites, and reducing harmful pesticides.

**Soil biota**: earthworms (detritivores) mix and aerate soil; bacteria and fungi (decomposers) recycle nutrients. Ploughing and pesticides reduce them.

**Energy losses and FCR**: only about 10% of energy passes from one trophic level to the next; the rest is lost in respiration, heat, excretion and uneaten parts. Eating plants directly feeds more people per hectare than eating animals. FCR = mass of feed ÷ mass gain of animal (lower is more efficient). Poultry and fish have lower FCRs than beef cattle because they are smaller-boned, and fish are ectotherms (no energy used keeping warm).

**Abiotic control**: glasshouses raise temperature and CO₂ and add light; irrigation and drainage control water; fertilisers supply nutrients; terracing alters topography; liming controls pH; leaching or drainage controls salinity.

#### Worked example

A pig gains 90 kg after eating 270 kg of feed. FCR = 270 ÷ 90 = 3.0. A chicken with FCR 1.7 needs 1.7 × 90 = 153 kg of feed for the same gain, 117 kg less.

### 3.5.1.2 Genetic manipulation and agricultural energetics

#### What you need to know

- Selective breeding, cloning, genetic modification (GM).
- Productivity, efficiency, intensive versus extensive farming, energy subsidies, energy ratios.
- Stocking density and monocultures.

#### Explained

- **Selective breeding**: choosing parents with desirable traits over many generations (high-yield wheat, dairy cattle). Slow, limited to traits within the species, can reduce genetic diversity.
- **Cloning**: making genetically identical copies (cuttings, tissue culture, embryo splitting). Keeps desirable traits exactly but gives no variation, so a disease can wipe out the whole crop.
- **GM**: transferring genes between species (pest-resistant Bt crops, herbicide-tolerant crops, vitamin-enriched rice). Fast and precise; concerns include gene flow to wild relatives, effects on non-target insects, and corporate control of seed.

**Energetics**:

- Productivity: output per hectare (or per animal) per year.
- Intensive farming uses high inputs (fertiliser, pesticides, machinery, housing) to get high output per hectare. Extensive farming uses low inputs over large areas with lower output per hectare.
- Energy subsidy: energy added by humans (fuel, fertiliser manufacture, machinery, transport) on top of solar energy.
- Energy ratio = energy in food produced ÷ energy subsidy input. Traditional systems often have ratios well above 1; intensive systems (especially intensive livestock and glasshouse crops) may have ratios below 1, using more fossil energy than they produce as food.

**Stocking density**: more animals per area gives more output but increases disease, stress, pollution from manure and overgrazing.

**Monocultures**: one crop over large areas allows efficient machinery but provides ideal conditions for specialist pests and diseases, reduces wildlife, and needs more pesticides and fertiliser.

#### Worked example

A wheat farm uses 18 GJ ha⁻¹ of fuel, fertiliser and machinery energy and produces 7 tonnes ha⁻¹ of grain containing 15 GJ t⁻¹. Energy ratio = (7 × 15) ÷ 18 = 105 ÷ 18 ≈ 5.8.

### 3.5.1.3 Environmental impacts of agriculture

#### What you need to know

Habitat clearance, draining wetlands, ploughing grassland, genetic contamination, soil erosion; pesticides, nutrients, greenhouse gases (CH₄, CO₂, N₂O); unsustainable irrigation and changes to evapotranspiration.

#### Explained

- Habitat loss: hedgerow removal, drained wetlands, ploughed old grassland, cleared forest.
- Genetic contamination: genes from GM or highly bred crops spread to wild relatives.
- Soil erosion and compaction (see [11](11-soils.md)).
- Pesticides kill non-target species; nutrients cause eutrophication.
- Greenhouse gases: CH₄ from ruminants and rice paddies; CO₂ from fuel, ploughing and clearance; N₂O from fertilised soils.
- Irrigation can lower water tables, empty rivers and lakes, and cause salinisation. Replacing forest with crops changes evapotranspiration and so local rainfall.

### 3.5.1.4 Social, economic and political factors

#### What you need to know

Consumer choice (social, cultural, religious, ethical); subsidies, guaranteed prices and quotas; trade and economic controls.

#### Explained

- Consumer choices change demand: dietary rules of different faiths, vegetarianism, demand for organic, free-range or locally grown food, and concern about food miles.
- Subsidies (payments to farmers), guaranteed minimum prices and quotas shape what is grown. Past production subsidies encouraged overproduction and intensification; newer schemes pay for environmental management (hedgerows, margins, wetlands).
- Trade rules, tariffs and world prices affect whether farming is profitable; cheap imports can undercut sustainable local farms.

### 3.5.1.5 Sustainable agriculture

#### What you need to know

Less pesticide and antibiotic use; cultural pest control (weeding, mulching, rotation, barrier crops, companion crops, habitats for predators, polyculture); integrated pest management; natural nutrient supply (nitrogen fixers, decomposition, rotation, recycling organic matter, permaculture, legumes, protecting soil biota); low tillage; water management; social impacts in rural areas.

#### Explained

- Integrated pest management (IPM): monitor pest numbers and act only above a threshold, using cultural and biological methods first and selective pesticides as a last resort.
- Crop rotation breaks pest and disease cycles and, with legumes, adds nitrogen.
- Barrier crops block pest movement; companion crops repel pests or attract predators (for example strong-smelling plants beside vegetables).
- Polyculture (several crops together) reduces pest spread and uses resources more fully.
- Permaculture designs farms as self-sustaining systems copying natural ecosystems (perennials, layered planting, recycling all wastes).
- Low (or zero) tillage leaves residues on the surface, protecting soil, saving fuel and keeping soil biota and organic matter.
- Water management: drip irrigation, rainwater harvesting, mulches to cut evaporation.
- Rural social impacts: sustainable methods are often more labour-intensive, which can support rural jobs, though yields may be lower.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying an energy ratio below 1 means a farm makes a loss. It means more energy (mostly fossil) is put in than is harvested as food energy.
- Saying GM and selective breeding are the same.

## 3.5.2 Aquatic food production systems

Fish and shellfish are a major source of protein. Wild fisheries are a renewable resource only if catches stay within what stocks can replace. This file covers marine productivity, fishing methods and their impacts, how stocks are assessed and managed, and aquaculture.

### 3.5.2.1 Marine productivity

#### What you need to know

Nutrient availability; the photic and aphotic zones; productivity of the open ocean, coastal waters and upwelling areas.

#### Explained

- **Photic zone**: the top layer (down to about 200 m in clear water, much less in turbid water) where enough light reaches for photosynthesis. Below is the **aphotic zone**, with no photosynthesis; life there depends on organic matter sinking from above.
- Nutrients (nitrate, phosphate, iron) sink out of the photic zone in dead organisms and faeces. In the open ocean a warm surface layer sits over cold deep water and they hardly mix, so surface waters are nutrient-poor. The open ocean has low productivity per square metre despite its huge area ("ocean desert").
- **Coastal waters** receive nutrients from rivers and mixing by tides and waves in shallow water, so productivity is higher.
- **Upwelling areas** (for example off Peru and north-west Africa): winds push surface water offshore and cold, nutrient-rich deep water rises to replace it, giving very high productivity and huge fisheries. When El Niño weakens upwelling off Peru, anchovy catches collapse.

### 3.5.2.2 Fishing

#### What you need to know

- Methods: pelagic and demersal trawling, purse seining, drift netting, long lining, traps for shellfish.
- Impacts: overfishing, changes in population age structure, by-catch, ghost fishing, damage to the seabed and reefs, food web effects.
- Estimating fish populations and maximum sustainable yield (MSY): biomass, recruitment, growth, natural mortality and catch; catch size, catch per unit effort, mean size and age; breeding success from egg and larva surveys.
- Ways to reduce impacts: quotas, net design, effort restrictions, no-take zones and closed seasons, minimum and maximum landing sizes, captive rearing and release, biodegradable or tracked gear.

#### Explained

| Method | Target | Description | Problems |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pelagic trawl | Mid-water shoals (herring, mackerel) | Cone-shaped net towed through the water | By-catch of dolphins |
| Demersal (bottom) trawl | Seabed fish (cod, plaice) and shrimp | Net dragged along the seabed | Destroys seabed habitats and reefs; high by-catch |
| Purse seine | Surface shoals (tuna, sardines) | Net encircles a shoal then is closed at the bottom | Can catch dolphins and juvenile tuna |
| Drift net | Surface fish | Long curtain of net drifting freely | Huge by-catch of seabirds, turtles, mammals; large drift nets banned in many waters |
| Long line | Tuna, swordfish, cod | Line with thousands of baited hooks | Seabirds (albatrosses) and turtles hooked |
| Traps (pots) | Crabs, lobsters | Baited cages on the seabed | Low impact, though lost pots ghost fish |

**Impacts**:

- Overfishing: catch exceeds the rate of replacement, so stocks decline (for example the collapse of the north-west Atlantic cod fishery in the early 1990s).
- Age structure: large old fish are removed first, so populations become dominated by young fish; fewer, smaller breeding fish produce fewer eggs.
- By-catch: unwanted species and undersized fish caught and usually discarded dead.
- Ghost fishing: lost or abandoned nets and pots keep catching animals for years.
- Seabed damage from trawls and dredges.
- Food web effects: removing predators or prey changes whole ecosystems (fewer forage fish means less food for seabirds and seals).

**Stock assessment**: a stock grows by recruitment (young fish joining) and growth of individuals, and shrinks by natural mortality and catch. Scientists estimate:

- total catch and catch per unit effort (CPUE): catch divided by fishing effort (for example tonnes per day at sea). Falling CPUE suggests a falling stock.
- mean size and age of fish caught (from scales or ear bones, otoliths): falling values suggest overfishing.
- breeding success from surveys of eggs and larvae in plankton samples.

**MSY**: the largest catch that can be taken year after year without reducing the stock. Population growth is fastest at about half the carrying capacity, so harvesting to keep the stock near that level gives the MSY. Fishing harder than this lowers future catches.

**Management**:

| Measure | How it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Quotas (total allowable catch) | Limit the mass landed |
| Net design (larger mesh, square-mesh panels, escape hatches, turtle excluder devices) | Lets young fish and non-target species escape |
| Effort restriction | Limits days at sea, boat numbers or engine power |
| No-take zones and closed seasons | Protect breeding areas and spawning times; adults spill over into fished areas |
| Minimum landing size | Fish can breed at least once before capture |
| Maximum landing size | Protects large, highly fertile females |
| Captive rearing and release | Restocks depleted populations |
| Biodegradable gear, tracked gear | Lost gear stops fishing or can be recovered |

#### Worked example

A fleet caught 12 000 t in 3000 boat-days in one year and 8000 t in 4000 boat-days five years later. CPUE fell from 4.0 to 2.0 t per boat-day: the stock has probably halved even though catches only fell by a third.

### 3.5.2.3 Aquaculture

#### What you need to know

- Extensive and intensive aquaculture.
- Principles: species and stock selection, breeding, disease control, competition, nutrition, temperature, dissolved oxygen, light, water flow.
- How far aquaculture can replace fishing (food needs, trophic efficiency).
- Reducing impacts: site selection, organic waste control, lower stocking density, preventing escapes, controlling feed, reducing pesticide and antibiotic use.

#### Explained

- **Extensive**: low stocking density in ponds or lagoons, with natural food; low input and output (for example carp in ponds, mussels on ropes).
- **Intensive**: high stocking density in tanks or sea cages with manufactured feed; high output (for example salmon farms).

Farmers choose fast-growing, disease-resistant species with low FCRs; control breeding to produce young; prevent disease (vaccination, low density); remove competitors and predators; feed balanced diets; and keep temperature, oxygen (aeration), light and water flow at the best levels.

**Can it replace fishing?** Herbivores and filter feeders (carp, tilapia, mussels, oysters) can be farmed efficiently. Carnivores (salmon, prawns) are often fed fishmeal and fish oil from wild fish, so they are at a high trophic level and may use more wild fish than they produce, though plant-based feeds are reducing this.

**Impacts and solutions**:

| Impact | Solution |
| --- | --- |
| Uneaten food and faeces cause eutrophication and deoxygenate the seabed | Site in areas with strong currents; lower density; control feeding; rotate sites |
| Disease and parasites (sea lice) spread to wild fish | Lower density; cleaner fish that eat lice; vaccines |
| Pesticides and antibiotics pollute water and select for resistance | Reduce use; biological controls |
| Escaped farmed fish compete and interbreed with wild fish | Stronger cages; sterile stock |
| Mangroves cleared for shrimp ponds | Careful site selection |

#### Common mistakes

- Saying MSY is reached by keeping the stock at its maximum size. Growth is fastest at intermediate size.
- Saying aquaculture always takes pressure off wild stocks.

### Check questions

1. Explain why upwelling areas support large fisheries. (3)
2. Explain why the open ocean has low productivity per square metre. (3)
3. Explain how bottom trawling can reduce the future productivity of a fishery. (3)
4. Catch per unit effort fell from 5.6 to 3.2 tonnes per boat-day over ten years. Calculate the percentage decrease and explain what it suggests. (3)
5. Explain why a minimum landing size can help a fish stock recover. (2)
6. Explain why harvesting at maximum sustainable yield keeps a population at about half its carrying capacity. (3)
7. Evaluate intensive salmon farming as a way of meeting demand for fish. (6)

### Mark scheme

1. Winds move surface water offshore (1); cold nutrient-rich deep water rises (1); nutrients in the photic zone allow high phytoplankton productivity supporting fish (1).
2. Warm surface water floats on cold deep water / stratified so little mixing (1); nutrients sink below the photic zone (1); so phytoplankton growth limited by nutrients (1).
3. Destroys seabed habitats and reefs used for shelter / feeding / spawning (1); high by-catch of young fish (1); fewer recruits in future (1).
4. (5.6 − 3.2) ÷ 5.6 × 100 = 43% (2); suggests the stock has declined / overfishing (1).

## 3.5.3 Forest resources

Forests supply materials and services. Managing them for one product (usually timber) can reduce their other values. This file covers what forests provide, how plantation design affects productivity and biodiversity, and the causes, effects and sustainable alternatives to deforestation. The rainforest ecosystem is also in [04](04-habitat-conservation-and-key-ecosystems.md).

### 3.5.3.1 Resources and ecosystem services

#### What you need to know

- Resources: timber, fuel, food, fibres, medicines.
- Services: atmospheric regulation, habitats, hydrological and climate regulation, soil conservation, recreation.

#### Explained

| Resource | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Timber | Construction, furniture, paper pulp |
| Fuel | Firewood and charcoal (the main fuel for cooking for many people in low-income countries) |
| Food | Fruits, nuts, honey, bushmeat |
| Fibres | Rattan, bamboo, latex (rubber) |
| Medicines | Quinine (cinchona bark), many traditional and modern drugs |

Services:

- Atmospheric regulation: forests take in CO₂ and release O₂; they store large amounts of carbon.
- Habitat: forests hold most terrestrial species.
- Hydrological regulation: canopies intercept rain; roots and litter increase infiltration; this slows run-off, reducing floods and keeping rivers flowing in dry periods.
- Climate regulation: transpiration adds water vapour, producing rain further inland; evaporative cooling; low albedo.
- Soil conservation: roots bind soil; litter protects the surface and adds organic matter.
- Recreation and tourism.

### 3.5.3.2 Productivity and biodiversity of managed forests

#### What you need to know

How productivity and biodiversity are affected by non-indigenous species, single-species plantations, close planting and simple age structure.

#### Explained

Commercial plantations are designed for maximum timber output:

| Feature | Effect on productivity | Effect on biodiversity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Non-indigenous species (for example Sitka spruce in the UK, eucalyptus in the tropics) | Fast growth, high yield | Few native species are adapted to use them |
| Single species (monoculture) | Uniform, efficient harvesting | Few niches; pests and disease spread easily |
| Close planting | Trees grow tall and straight competing for light; more trees per hectare | Dark understorey with few plants; little ground flora |
| Simple age structure (all trees planted at once) | Whole stand harvested together (clear-felling) | No old trees, dead wood or variety of habitats |

So plantations have high timber productivity but low biodiversity compared with natural forest.

### 3.5.3.3 Deforestation and sustainable forestry

#### What you need to know

- Causes: harvesting faster than the maximum sustainable yield; clearance for other land uses.
- Effects on resources, biodiversity, hydrology (less interception and transpiration, more run-off), soil and climate (albedo, carbon, rainfall downwind).
- Sustainable management: mixed species, indigenous species, mixed ages, selective logging.

#### Explained

**Causes**: logging faster than trees regrow; clearance for cattle ranching, soya, oil palm, mining, roads, settlements and reservoirs; fuelwood collection around towns.

**Effects**:

- Resources: loss of future timber, fuel, food and medicines.
- Biodiversity: habitat loss and fragmentation; extinction of endemic species.
- Hydrology: less interception and transpiration means more rain reaches the ground quickly and runs off, causing floods, erosion and lower dry-season flow.
- Soil: erosion; nutrient loss (especially in rainforests, where most nutrients are in the biomass); laterisation (hard crusts) in some tropical soils.
- Climate: burning and decay release stored carbon as CO₂; higher albedo of cleared land slightly changes local energy balance; less transpiration means less rain downwind (much rain in the western Amazon comes from moisture recycled by forests further east).

**Sustainable forestry**:

- Mixed species: more habitats and less vulnerable to pests.
- Indigenous species: support native wildlife.
- Mixed ages (continuous cover): some trees always standing, varied habitat, no bare soil.
- Selective logging: only some mature trees removed, leaving the forest structure and canopy. Damage is reduced by directional felling, planned extraction routes and removing climbers first.
- Harvesting at or below the maximum sustainable yield (annual growth increment).
- Certification schemes let buyers choose timber from well-managed forests.

#### Worked example

A forest has a standing volume of 240 m³ ha⁻¹ and grows by 6 m³ ha⁻¹ per year. Sustainable annual harvest ≈ 6 m³ ha⁻¹. With a 30-year cycle, selective logging could remove 30 × 6 = 180 m³ ha⁻¹ per cycle at most, about 75% of the standing volume, but leaving more improves biodiversity and regrowth.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying deforestation increases rainfall locally. It reduces transpiration and so rainfall downwind.
- Saying plantations are as good as natural forest because tree cover is the same.

## 3.6 Sustainability

This section pulls the course together. Natural systems have run for billions of years by keeping dynamic equilibria, using renewable energy and recycling materials. Human systems are often linear and unstable. The circular economy applies natural principles to human activity. Expect synoptic questions that draw on every other topic.

### 3.6.1 Dynamic equilibria

#### What you need to know

- 3.6.1.1 Negative feedback resists change: climate (cloud and albedo; photosynthesis and CO₂), the hydrological cycle, density-dependent control of populations.
- 3.6.1.2 Positive feedback amplifies change: permafrost melting, ocean acidification, falling albedo, methane hydrate release, forest and peat fires, cirrus cloud formation, soil decomposition.
- 3.6.1.3 Tipping points: natural processes that human activity may make self-sustaining (fires, methane hydrates, permafrost).
- 3.6.1.4 Diverse systems (coral reefs, rainforests) resist change better than low-diversity human systems and agroecosystems.

#### Explained

A dynamic equilibrium has continuous flows in and out but stays roughly constant overall, because negative feedback corrects any change.

**Negative feedback examples**:

- Warming → more evaporation → more low cloud → more sunlight reflected → cooling.
- More CO₂ → faster photosynthesis → CO₂ removed.
- Hydrological cycle: more evaporation → more rain, balancing stores over time.
- Population above carrying capacity → more competition and disease → more deaths → population falls (density-dependent control; see [05](05-monitoring-ecology-succession-and-populations.md)).

**Positive feedback examples** (warming leading to more warming): permafrost melting releases CH₄ and CO₂; warmer, more acidic oceans absorb less CO₂; ice melt lowers albedo; methane hydrates destabilise; drier forests and peat burn, releasing CO₂; more cirrus cloud traps IR; faster soil decomposition releases CO₂ (see [06](06-the-atmosphere-and-global-climate-change.md)).

**Tipping points**: once a threshold is crossed, a positive feedback keeps going without further human input. Examples: permafrost carbon release once thawing is widespread; widespread drought and fire turning rainforest into savanna; methane hydrate release.

**Diversity and stability**: in a species-rich ecosystem, many species fill similar roles, and food webs have many links, so if one species declines, others take over (functional redundancy). Coral reefs and rainforests have kept going through many past changes. Monoculture farms and towns depend on few species and on constant human inputs; a single pest or supply failure can collapse them.

### 3.6.2 Energy

#### What you need to know

- 3.6.2.1 Natural processes run on renewable energy, mainly solar: the hydrological, carbon and nitrogen cycles, atmospheric and ocean circulation.
- 3.6.2.2 Natural processes use low energy-density sources, which can be concentrated to higher densities.
- 3.6.2.3 Most natural processes work at low temperatures (photosynthesis, enzymes, nitrogen fixation, compared with the Haber process).
- 3.6.2.4 Carbon footprints and sustainable development reconsidered in the light of copying natural energy systems.

#### Explained

- Solar energy drives evaporation (water cycle), photosynthesis (carbon cycle), winds and ocean currents. It is effectively unlimited and causes no pollution.
- Sunlight is spread thinly (low energy density), but natural systems concentrate it: photosynthesis stores it in sugars, food chains concentrate it in animals, and over geological time it was concentrated into fossil fuels. Human systems could do the same: solar panels and wind turbines over large areas, storage in batteries or hydrogen.
- Biological processes use enzymes to work at about 0 to 40 °C and normal pressure. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert N₂ to ammonia at soil temperature; the Haber process needs about 450 °C and 200 atmospheres, using about 1 to 2% of world energy. Copying low-temperature processes (biotechnology, enzyme catalysts) saves energy.
- A sustainable society would run on renewable energy, concentrate it efficiently, and use low-temperature processes, cutting carbon footprints.

### 3.6.3 Material cycles

#### What you need to know

- 3.6.3.1 Many human systems are linear: fossil fuels are used up; minerals are dispersed and mixed, so they are hard to recover.
- 3.6.3.2 Natural systems use a small set of elements to build monomers and polymers (carbohydrates, proteins) and reuse simple raw materials.
- 3.6.3.3 Natural wastes are non-toxic or do not build up: molecules are biodegradable.

#### Explained

A linear economy is "take, make, dispose": resources are extracted, made into products and thrown away. Metals end up spread through landfill in tiny amounts; complex mixtures (alloys, composites, electronics) are hard to separate.

Natural systems build everything from a few elements (C, H, O, N, P, S and a few others), joined into a few types of monomer (sugars, amino acids, nucleotides) and then into polymers. When organisms die, decomposers break polymers back to simple molecules that are reused. No natural waste accumulates indefinitely, because something can always break it down. In contrast, many synthetic materials (plastics, persistent pesticides) are not biodegradable.

### 3.6.4 The circular economy

#### What you need to know

- 3.6.4.1 Principles: cycling materials; renewable energy; supporting natural ecosystems; separating technical and biological materials; diversity and connected systems; designing for reuse at end of life; optimum rather than maximum production (pollinators and decomposers versus agroecosystems); design technologies. Applied to land use, water, minerals, waste, pollution control, energy and food.
- 3.6.4.2 Biocapacity and ecological footprints; regional comparisons using the WWF Living Planet Report, the Living Planet Index and footprint calculations.

#### Explained

**Circular economy principles**:

- Materials are kept in use for as long as possible, then recovered.
- **Biological materials** (food, wood, natural fibres) are designed to return safely to the soil (compost, anaerobic digestion).
- **Technical materials** (metals, plastics) are designed to be recovered and remanufactured without loss of quality, kept separate from biological materials.
- Energy comes from renewable sources.
- Systems are diverse and connected, so they are resilient.
- Products are designed for disassembly and reuse (see Cradle to Cradle in [09](09-mineral-resources.md)).
- Aim for optimum production that keeps the system healthy, not maximum short-term output (for example keeping pollinators and soil decomposers rather than maximising yield with chemicals that destroy them).

**Applications**:

| Area | Circular approach |
| --- | --- |
| Land use | Multiple uses, such as agroforestry; protecting ecosystem services |
| Water | Conservation, reuse of grey water, catchment management |
| Minerals | Recovering metals from low-grade ores and waste (bioleaching, phytomining) |
| Waste | Waste hierarchy: reduce, reuse, repurpose, recycle |
| Pollution control | Moving from end-of-pipe treatment to not producing pollutants at all (for example fuel cells that emit only water) |
| Energy | Renewables; low-temperature manufacturing |
| Food | Natural nutrient supply, biological pest control, soil protection (see [19](19-agriculture.md)) |

**Biocapacity and ecological footprint**:

- Biocapacity: the area of productive land and sea available to produce resources and absorb wastes, measured in global hectares (gha).
- Ecological footprint: the area needed to supply a population's consumption and absorb its CO₂ (also in gha).
- If footprint exceeds biocapacity, the population is in ecological deficit, relying on imports or using up natural capital. The world as a whole currently uses resources faster than the Earth regenerates them.
- The WWF Living Planet Report tracks footprints and the **Living Planet Index**, an average of trends in monitored vertebrate populations since 1970, which shows large declines, especially in tropical regions and fresh water.

#### Worked example

A country has a population of 60 million, a footprint of 4.5 gha per person and a biocapacity of 1.2 gha per person. Deficit per person = 3.3 gha. Total deficit = 3.3 × 60 × 10⁶ = 1.98 × 10⁸ gha. The country would need 4.5 ÷ 1.2 = 3.75 times its own biocapacity to be self-sufficient.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling any change in a system "positive feedback" because the outcome is good. Positive means the change is amplified.
- Saying recycling alone is a circular economy. Design for reuse, renewable energy and reducing consumption are also needed.

## Topic 1 Planet Earth (Paper 1)

Every observation we make is made from a spinning, slightly squashed planet, through a layer of air. This topic covers the Earth's shape and size, its internal layers, the latitude and longitude system, the main reference lines on its surface, and how the atmosphere affects what we see in the sky.

### 1.1 and 1.2 Shape and size of the Earth

#### What you need to know

- The Earth is an oblate spheroid: a sphere slightly flattened at the poles and bulging at the equator.
- The mean diameter of the Earth is about 13 000 km (given on the data sheet). Use it in calculations of scale.

#### Explained

The Earth spins once a day. Material at the equator moves fastest and tends to bulge outwards, so the equatorial diameter is about 43 km more than the pole-to-pole diameter. That difference is tiny (about 0.3%), so from space the Earth looks round; but it is measurable and matters for precise mapping and for satellite orbits. It is also why the equatorial bulge can be pulled on by the Sun and Moon, causing precession (Topic 3).

Evidence that the Earth is round: the curved shadow on the Moon during a lunar eclipse; ships disappearing hull first over the horizon; different stars being visible from different latitudes; the Sun's noon altitude changing with latitude (used by Eratosthenes, Topic 3).

#### Worked example

How many Earth diameters fit into the mean Earth-Moon distance of 380 000 km? 380 000 ÷ 13 000 ≈ 29. So the Moon is roughly 30 Earth diameters away.

Circumference of the Earth = π × d = 3.14 × 13 000 ≈ 41 000 km.

#### Common mistakes

- Describing the Earth as a perfect sphere, or as "egg-shaped". Oblate spheroid means flattened at the poles.
- Getting the bulge the wrong way round: it bulges at the equator.

### 1.3 The Earth's internal divisions

#### What you need to know

- The four main layers and their features: crust, mantle, outer core and inner core.

#### Explained

| Layer | Depth | State | Composition and features |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Crust | Surface to about 5 to 10 km under oceans, 30 to 70 km under continents | Solid | Rocks rich in silicon and oxygen; thinnest layer; broken into tectonic plates |
| Mantle | To about 2900 km | Solid rock, but hot enough to flow very slowly over millions of years | Silicate rocks rich in iron and magnesium; convection currents drive plate movement |
| Outer core | About 2900 to 5100 km | Liquid | Mainly molten iron and nickel; moving liquid metal generates the Earth's magnetic field |
| Inner core | About 5100 km to the centre (about 6400 km) | Solid | Iron and nickel; hottest part (around 5000 to 6000 °C) but solid because of the enormous pressure |

Seismic waves from earthquakes give the evidence: S-waves cannot travel through liquid, and they do not pass through the outer core, so it must be liquid. The magnetic field generated in the outer core produces the magnetosphere, which protects the Earth from the solar wind (Topic 10).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the mantle is liquid magma. It is mostly solid rock that flows extremely slowly.
- Saying the inner core is liquid because it is hottest. Pressure keeps it solid.

### 1.4 and 1.5 Latitude, longitude and surface reference points

#### What you need to know

- Use latitude and longitude to give positions on Earth.
- Use these reference lines and points: the Equator, Tropic of Cancer, Tropic of Capricorn, Arctic Circle, Antarctic Circle, Prime Meridian, North Pole and South Pole.

#### Explained

Latitude is the angle north or south of the Equator, measured from the centre of the Earth: 0° at the Equator to 90° N at the North Pole and 90° S at the South Pole. Lines of equal latitude (parallels) are circles parallel to the Equator.

Longitude is the angle east or west of the Prime Meridian (which passes through Greenwich, London): 0° to 180° E or W. Lines of equal longitude (meridians) run from pole to pole.

A position is written latitude first, for example London ≈ 51.5° N, 0.1° W. Angles can be split into minutes (1° = 60′).

| Reference | Latitude or longitude | Astronomical meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equator | 0° latitude | Directly below the celestial equator; the Sun is overhead at noon at the equinoxes |
| Tropic of Cancer | About 23.5° N | Furthest north the Sun is ever overhead (June solstice) |
| Tropic of Capricorn | About 23.5° S | Furthest south the Sun is ever overhead (December solstice) |
| Arctic Circle | About 66.5° N | North of here there is at least one day a year when the Sun does not set, and one when it does not rise |
| Antarctic Circle | About 66.5° S | The same for the southern hemisphere |
| Prime Meridian | 0° longitude | Reference for longitude and for Greenwich Mean Time (Topic 4) |
| North Pole | 90° N | Directly below the north celestial pole; Polaris almost overhead |
| South Pole | 90° S | Directly below the south celestial pole |

The tropics and polar circles exist because the Earth's axis is tilted by about 23.5° to the perpendicular of its orbit. 23.5° + 66.5° = 90°.

#### Worked example

Place A is at 52° N, 4° W and place B at 52° N, 11° E. They are on the same parallel; the difference in longitude is 4 + 11 = 15°. Since the Earth turns 360° in 24 hours, 15° is 1 hour of time (Topic 4).

#### Common mistakes

- Swapping latitude and longitude. Latitude goes north-south (think "ladder rungs" that run across); longitude lines run from pole to pole.
- Adding longitudes on the same side of the meridian. Two places at 10° W and 4° W differ by 6°, not 14°.

### 1.6 Effects of the atmosphere on observations

#### What you need to know

- How the Earth's atmosphere affects sky colour, causes skyglow (light pollution) and causes twinkling (seeing).

#### Explained

Sky colour: sunlight is white, a mix of all colours. Air molecules scatter short-wavelength (blue) light much more than long-wavelength (red) light. Scattered blue light reaches our eyes from every direction, so the daytime sky looks blue. At sunrise and sunset, light passes through much more air; most blue has been scattered out of the direct beam, so the Sun and the sky near it look orange and red. On the Moon, with no atmosphere, the sky is black even in daytime. Scattering is also why stars cannot be seen in the daytime: the bright scattered sunlight drowns them out.

Skyglow (light pollution): artificial light from streetlights, buildings and cars shines upwards and is scattered by the air, dust and water droplets, making the night sky glow orange or grey. This reduces contrast, so faint stars, the Milky Way, galaxies and nebulae disappear. In a city only a few dozen stars may be visible; at a dark site, thousands. (Causes and effects are developed in 6.6.)

Twinkling (seeing): the atmosphere has moving pockets of air at different temperatures and densities, which refract light by slightly different amounts. Starlight crossing them is bent this way and that, so stars appear to flicker and jump. "Seeing" describes how steady the air is: good seeing means little twinkling and sharp telescope images. Planets twinkle less than stars because they show a tiny disc rather than a point, so the effects average out. Twinkling is worst near the horizon, where light passes through the most air.

Other effects: clouds and humidity block or dim light; the atmosphere absorbs most wavelengths outside visible and radio (Topic 13); refraction lifts objects near the horizon slightly.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the sky is blue because it reflects the oceans.
- Saying stars twinkle because they flicker in brightness themselves. The effect is caused by our atmosphere.
- Calling twinkling "light pollution". Light pollution is artificial light; seeing is turbulence.

## Topic 9 Exploring the Moon (Paper 2)

Telescopes, spacecraft and astronauts have told us much more about the Moon than the naked eye can. This topic covers the Moon's interior compared with the Earth's, the differences between its near and far sides, how the far side was explored, why getting to the Moon needs rockets, and the theories of the Moon's origin.

### 9.1 The Moon's internal structure compared with the Earth's

#### What you need to know

- The Moon's major internal divisions, in comparison with those of the Earth.

#### Explained

Evidence comes from seismometers left by Apollo astronauts (which recorded moonquakes and impacts), from measurements of the Moon's gravity by orbiting spacecraft, from its average density, and from how it wobbles as it rotates.

| | Earth | Moon |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crust | Thin: 5 to 70 km; granite (continents) and basalt (oceans) | Thicker relative to size: about 30 to 50 km, thicker on the far side; anorthosite highlands with basalt in the maria |
| Mantle | About 2900 km thick, solid but flowing slowly (convection drives plate tectonics) | Most of the Moon's volume; rigid and cold in the upper part; a partly molten zone near the base |
| Core | Large: about 55% of Earth's radius; liquid outer core of iron and nickel generates a strong magnetic field; solid inner core | Very small: about 20% of the Moon's radius; iron-rich, probably with a solid inner core and a fluid outer layer; no global magnetic field today |
| Mean density | About 5.5 g/cm³ | About 3.3 g/cm³ (so the Moon has little iron) |
| Activity | Active: plate tectonics, volcanoes, earthquakes | Geologically almost "dead": no plates, no active volcanoes; weak moonquakes |

The Moon is small, so it lost its internal heat much faster than the Earth. Its low density and small core are important evidence for the Giant Impact Hypothesis (9.5).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Moon has no core.
- Saying the Moon has a magnetic field like Earth's. It has only weak patches of magnetised rock.

### 9.2 and 9.3 The near and far sides

#### What you need to know

- The major differences in appearance between the near and far sides of the Moon.
- How information has been gathered about the far side.

#### Explained

| Near side | Far side |
| --- | --- |
| About 31% covered by dark maria | Very few maria (about 1 to 2%) |
| Large smooth lava plains | Heavily cratered bright highlands almost everywhere |
| Thinner crust | Thicker crust, which made it harder for lava to reach the surface |
| | Contains the South Pole-Aitken basin, one of the largest impact basins in the Solar System |

The reason for the difference is linked to the crust: lava from the interior could break through the thinner near-side crust and flood the large impact basins, but rarely reached the surface through the thicker far-side crust.

Because the Moon's rotation is synchronous (2.7), the far side can never be seen from Earth (except the small extra areas shown by libration, 2.8). It was first seen in 1959 when the Soviet probe Luna 3 flew round the Moon and sent back photographs. Since then:

- Crewed Apollo missions in lunar orbit (from Apollo 8, 1968) photographed it directly; astronauts were the first humans to see it.
- Orbiters such as Lunar Orbiter, Clementine, Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) and GRAIL mapped the whole surface in detail, measured heights with lasers, and mapped variations in gravity to find the crust's thickness.
- Landers and rovers: China's Chang'e 4 made the first soft landing on the far side (2019), using a relay satellite positioned beyond the Moon to send signals to Earth, since the Moon blocks direct radio contact. Chang'e 6 returned the first far-side samples (2024).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the far side cannot be photographed. It has been mapped completely by spacecraft.
- Explaining the lack of maria by the far side "facing away from the Earth's gravity" without mentioning crust thickness.

### 9.4 Escape velocity and rockets

#### What you need to know

- A spacecraft travelling to the Moon must reach the Earth's escape velocity (or nearly so), and the energy needed can only be provided by rockets.

#### Explained

Escape velocity is the minimum speed an object needs at the surface to escape a body's gravity completely without further propulsion. For the Earth it is about 11.2 km/s (about 40 000 km/h). A spacecraft going to the Moon must reach nearly this speed: it does not need to escape the Earth's gravity entirely (the Moon is still within the Earth's gravitational influence), but it needs almost the same energy.

Why rockets: a rocket carries its own fuel and oxidiser, so it works in the near-vacuum of space and does not need air (jet engines need oxygen from the air). It works by pushing hot exhaust gases out of the back at high speed; by Newton's third law, the gases push the rocket forwards. Enormous amounts of energy are needed to lift the spacecraft's mass to that speed, so most of a launch rocket's mass is propellant. Multi-stage rockets drop empty fuel tanks and engines as they go, so less mass needs to be accelerated. Launching eastwards, closer to the equator, uses the Earth's rotation to give a little extra speed.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying spacecraft need rockets because there is no gravity in space.
- Giving escape velocity in m/s without converting correctly: 11.2 km/s = 11 200 m/s.

### 9.5 Theories of the Moon's origin

#### What you need to know

- The Giant Impact Hypothesis, and alternatives including Capture Theory and Co-accretion Theory.

#### Explained

Giant Impact Hypothesis (the most widely accepted): about 4.5 billion years ago, soon after the Earth formed, a Mars-sized body (sometimes called Theia) struck the young Earth a glancing blow. The impact melted and vaporised much of the impactor and the Earth's outer layers. A disc of debris, mostly from the mantles, was thrown into orbit around the Earth and gathered together (accreted) to form the Moon. The iron cores of the two bodies mostly merged with the Earth.

Evidence for it:

- The Moon has a small iron core and low density, matching formation from mantle-rock debris.
- Moon rocks have almost identical oxygen isotope ratios to Earth rocks, suggesting a shared source.
- Moon rocks are depleted in volatile elements (easily evaporated substances such as water), as expected after extreme heating.
- Evidence of a global "magma ocean" early in the Moon's history, consistent with fast formation from hot debris.
- Computer simulations show such an impact can produce a moon of the right size and the Earth-Moon system's angular momentum (spin and orbit).

Alternatives:

- Capture Theory: the Moon formed elsewhere in the Solar System and was captured by the Earth's gravity as it passed. Problems: capture of such a large body needs a very special situation, such as a third body or the Earth's early atmosphere to slow it down; and it would not explain the matching isotopes.
- Co-accretion Theory: the Earth and Moon formed together, side by side, from the same cloud of material orbiting the Sun. Problems: it would give the Moon an iron core and density similar to the Earth's, which it does not have; and it does not easily explain the system's angular momentum.
- (Fission Theory, in which a fast-spinning Earth flung off the Moon, is an older idea, also rejected.)

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Giant Impact Hypothesis has been proved. It is the best-supported theory, but some questions remain (for example, why the Moon and Earth are so similar if much of the Moon should come from Theia).
- Mixing up co-accretion (formed together) and capture (formed elsewhere).

### 11.1, 11.6, 11.8, 11.9 and 11.11 Bodies of the Solar System

#### What you need to know

- Use data about the names and relative locations of planets, dwarf planets and Small Solar System Objects (asteroids, meteoroids and comets).
- The principal characteristics of the planets: relative size, relative mass, surface temperature, atmospheric composition, presence of moons and ring systems.
- Use information about the size of the Solar System; use the AU, light year and parsec (see also Topic 7).
- Most bodies orbit the Sun in or close to the plane of the ecliptic.

#### Explained

Definitions (International Astronomical Union):

- Planet: orbits the Sun, has enough mass for its gravity to pull it into a nearly round shape, and has cleared the neighbourhood of its orbit of other material.
- Dwarf planet: orbits the Sun and is nearly round, but has not cleared its orbit, and is not a moon. Ceres (in the asteroid belt), Pluto, Haumea, Makemake, Eris.
- Small Solar System Objects (SSSOs): everything else orbiting the Sun: asteroids (rocky or metallic bodies, mostly in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter), comets (icy bodies) and meteoroids (small fragments).

Planet characteristics (data sheet values; all given in the exam):

Patterns: the four inner terrestrial planets are small, dense and rocky with few or no moons and no rings; the four outer giant planets are large, low-density, mostly gas and ice, with many moons and ring systems. Temperature generally falls with distance, except Venus, which is hotter than Mercury because of its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere.

Size: Neptune is 30 AU from the Sun; the Kuiper Belt extends to about 50 AU; the heliosphere's edge is over 100 AU; the Oort Cloud perhaps 2000 to 100 000 AU. Light takes about 4 hours to reach Neptune and over a year to reach the outer Oort Cloud.

Most planets, the asteroid belt and the Kuiper Belt objects orbit close to the plane of the ecliptic (within a few degrees; Mercury about 7°, Pluto about 17°), and all planets orbit in the same direction. This flatness is evidence that they formed from a flat, spinning disc (12.3). The Oort Cloud is an exception: it is a spherical shell, so long-period comets arrive from all directions.

#### Worked example

How many times further from the Sun is Saturn than Mars? 9.5 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 6.3.

Jupiter's diameter is 143 ÷ 12.8 ≈ 11 times the Earth's, but its mass is only 318 times, not 11³ ≈ 1400 times, so its density is much lower.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling Pluto a planet. It is a dwarf planet (since 2006).
- Saying Mercury is the hottest planet. Venus is, because of its greenhouse atmosphere.
- Saying only Saturn has rings. All four giant planets do.

### 11.2, 11.3, 11.4 and 11.5 Comets, the Kuiper Belt, the Oort Cloud and the heliosphere

#### What you need to know

- The structure of comets: nucleus, coma and tails.
- Orbits of short-period comets and their origin in the Kuiper Belt.
- Orbits of long-period comets and their origin in the Oort Cloud.
- The location and nature of the Kuiper Belt, Oort Cloud and heliosphere.

#### Explained

Structure of a comet:

- Nucleus: a solid body a few km across of ice (water, carbon dioxide, ammonia, methane) mixed with dust and rock: a "dirty snowball". Very dark surface.
- Coma: as the comet approaches the Sun (within a few AU), heat makes the ices sublime (turn straight to gas), releasing gas and dust into a huge glowing cloud around the nucleus, which can be larger than the Sun.
- Tails: two kinds. The dust tail is curved, yellowish-white, made of dust pushed away gently by radiation pressure of sunlight, and lags behind along the orbit. The ion (gas or plasma) tail is straight and bluish, made of ionised gas swept out by the solar wind; it points directly away from the Sun. Tails can be millions of km long. Because both are pushed by the Sun, a comet moving away from the Sun travels tail first.

Orbits:

- Short-period comets have periods of less than 200 years, orbit fairly close to the ecliptic, mostly in the same direction as the planets, and have elliptical orbits reaching out to around the outer planets or the Kuiper Belt. Most come from the Kuiper Belt (and the scattered disc beyond it), deflected inwards by Neptune's gravity. Example: Halley's comet (76 years), 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko (6.4 years).
- Long-period comets have periods of thousands to millions of years, very elongated orbits, and come from all directions and at any angle to the ecliptic. They come from the Oort Cloud, nudged inwards by passing stars or by the Galaxy's tidal pull.

Regions:

- Kuiper Belt: a flat, doughnut-shaped belt of icy bodies beyond Neptune, from about 30 to 50 AU, close to the plane of the ecliptic. Contains dwarf planets such as Pluto and Makemake and many smaller objects, leftovers from the formation of the Solar System.
- Oort Cloud: a hypothetical, roughly spherical cloud of trillions of icy bodies surrounding the Solar System at roughly 2000 to 100 000 AU. Never directly observed: its existence is inferred from the orbits of long-period comets.
- Heliosphere: the huge bubble in interstellar space blown by the solar wind. The termination shock is where the solar wind slows sharply (about 90 AU); the heliopause, the boundary where the solar wind meets the interstellar medium, is about 120 AU from the Sun. Voyager 1 crossed it in 2012 and Voyager 2 in 2018.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a comet's tail points behind it. It points away from the Sun.
- Saying the Oort Cloud has been photographed.

### 11.7 Formation and current position of the gas giants

#### What you need to know

- The main theories for how the gas giant planets in our Solar System formed and how they reached their current positions.

#### Explained

Formation: beyond the "snow line" (about 3 to 5 AU from the young Sun), it was cold enough for water and other compounds to freeze into ice, so there was much more solid material. Rocky and icy planetesimals collected quickly into large cores of about 10 Earth masses. Their gravity was then strong enough to pull in huge envelopes of hydrogen and helium gas from the surrounding disc before the young Sun blew the gas away. This is the core accretion model (12.3 describes it and the alternative, disc instability).

Current positions: the giant planets probably did not form exactly where they are now. Models (such as the "Nice model") suggest that gravitational interactions with the remaining planetesimals, and with each other, made the giants migrate: Jupiter moved slightly inward, while Saturn, Uranus and Neptune moved outwards. Uranus and Neptune may have swapped places. This migration scattered many small bodies, perhaps causing the Late Heavy Bombardment of the inner planets and shaping the Kuiper Belt. The "Grand Tack" idea suggests Jupiter moved inwards towards Mars's orbit and then back out again, which would explain why Mars is small and the asteroid belt has little mass.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying giant planets formed close to the Sun and then moved out. Their cores formed beyond the snow line where ices were available.

### 11.10 Meteoroids and meteorites

#### What you need to know

- The origin and structure of meteoroids and meteorites.

#### Explained

Meteoroids are small rocky or metallic particles in space, from dust grains up to about a metre across (larger bodies are asteroids). Most come from comets (dust released as the ice evaporates, forming meteor streams, 5.7) or from collisions between asteroids, and a few are chipped off the Moon or Mars by impacts.

A meteorite is a meteoroid that survives its passage through the atmosphere and reaches the ground. Main types:

- Stony meteorites (most common, over 90% of falls): mainly silicate rock. Chondrites contain small round grains called chondrules and are some of the oldest, least changed material in the Solar System (about 4.6 billion years old); some carbonaceous chondrites contain water and organic compounds.
- Iron meteorites: mostly iron-nickel metal, from the cores of asteroids that were broken apart. Show crystal patterns when cut and etched.
- Stony-iron meteorites: a mixture of metal and rock, from the boundary between core and mantle of a broken asteroid.

They are studied because they show what the early Solar System was made of and how old it is.

#### Common mistakes

- Confusing the three terms: meteoroid (in space), meteor (light in the sky), meteorite (on the ground).

### 11.12 Transits of Venus and the size of the AU

#### What you need to know

- How transits of Venus, as proposed by Halley, were used to find the size of the astronomical unit and so the absolute size of the Solar System.

#### Explained

Kepler's third law gave the relative distances of all the planets in AU, but nobody knew how big an AU was in km. Edmond Halley proposed in 1716 that timing a transit of Venus across the Sun from widely separated places on Earth would give the answer.

The principle is parallax: observers at different latitudes see Venus cross the Sun along slightly different paths (chords), because they view it from slightly different positions. The separation between the paths depends on the distance to Venus and the Sun. Each observer times the transit from start to end; the duration tells them the length of their chord, and hence where it lies on the Sun's disc. Knowing the distance between the observers on Earth (in km), the angular difference between the paths, and Venus's distance relative to the Earth's from Kepler's third law (0.72 AU), the Earth-Sun distance in km can be calculated.

Transits of Venus are rare: they come in pairs 8 years apart, more than a century apart (1761 and 1769; 1874 and 1882; 2004 and 2012; next 2117). Expeditions were sent all over the world in 1761 and 1769 (including Captain Cook's voyage to Tahiti). Results were affected by the "black drop effect", which made exact contact times hard to judge, but gave an AU of about 150 million km, within a few per cent of today's value (now measured accurately by radar).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a transit of Venus can happen at every inferior conjunction. Venus's orbit is tilted, so it usually passes above or below the Sun.

### 11.13 The origin of water on Earth

#### What you need to know

- The main theories for the origin of Earth's water.

#### Explained

The early Earth formed inside the snow line, where it was too hot for ice to be included in large amounts, and it was very hot after formation and the Giant Impact. So where did its oceans come from? Main theories:

- Delivered by comets: icy comets striking the young Earth. But measurements of the ratio of deuterium (heavy hydrogen) to normal hydrogen in most comets (for example comet 67P measured by Rosetta) show it is higher than in Earth's oceans, so comets cannot have supplied most of the water.
- Delivered by asteroids: carbonaceous chondrite asteroids from the outer asteroid belt contain water in their minerals with a deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio close to that of Earth's oceans. This is the favoured source of much of Earth's water.
- From within the Earth (outgassing): water was locked in minerals in the rocks that formed the Earth and was released by volcanoes as the planet cooled, condensing as rain to fill the oceans.

Most scientists think the water came from a mixture of these sources.

#### Common mistakes

- Stating that comets definitely brought Earth's water. Isotope evidence suggests they were not the main source.

### 11.14 to 11.17 The eye, objectives and simple telescopes

#### What you need to know

- The human eye is limited in astronomy by its small aperture and limited sensitivity in low light.
- The objective of a telescope captures and focuses light so the image can be magnified by an eyepiece.
- Convex (converging) lenses and concave (converging) mirrors can collect and focus light.
- Simple telescopes combine an objective (lens or mirror) with an eyepiece.

#### Explained

The eye's pupil is at most about 7 mm across in the dark, so it collects very little light; and the eye cannot store light over a long exposure, as a camera can. So many faint objects are invisible to the eye, and fine detail cannot be resolved.

A telescope has two optical parts:

- The objective (or primary): a large converging lens or concave mirror that collects light from a distant object and brings it to a focus, forming a real image at its focal point. The bigger it is, the more light it collects.
- The eyepiece: a small, short-focal-length converging lens (or set of lenses) that magnifies this image for the eye, like a magnifying glass.

A convex (converging) lens refracts parallel light rays from a distant object so they meet at the focal point. A concave mirror reflects parallel rays to a focus. The distance from the lens or mirror to the focus is its focal length.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the main purpose of a telescope is magnification. Collecting more light (and resolving detail) is more important.
- Calling a concave mirror diverging. A concave mirror converges light.

### 11.18 and 11.25 Telescope designs; reflectors versus refractors

#### What you need to know

- The basic designs of the Galilean refractor, Keplerian refractor, Newtonian reflector and Cassegrain reflector. Detailed ray diagrams are not needed.
- The advantages of reflecting telescopes compared with refractors, in terms of chromatic aberration, very long focal lengths, large apertures and multiple mirrors.

#### Explained

| Design | Objective | Eyepiece and layout | Image |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Galilean refractor | Convex lens at the front of a tube | Concave (diverging) lens, placed before the focus | Upright; narrow field of view; used by Galileo |
| Keplerian refractor | Convex lens | Convex (converging) lens, placed after the focus | Inverted; wider field; the basis of modern refractors |
| Newtonian reflector | Concave (parabolic) primary mirror at the bottom of an open tube | A small flat secondary mirror at 45° near the top reflects light out to an eyepiece in the side of the tube | Inverted; simple and cheap; popular with amateurs |
| Cassegrain reflector | Concave primary mirror with a central hole | A small convex secondary mirror reflects light back down through the hole to an eyepiece or camera behind the primary | Compact tube with long effective focal length; used in most large professional telescopes |

Advantages of reflectors over refractors:

- Chromatic aberration: a lens refracts different colours by different amounts, so they focus at slightly different points, giving coloured fringes round bright objects. Mirrors reflect all colours the same way, so reflectors have no chromatic aberration.
- Very long focal lengths: high magnification needs a long focal length; a refractor with a long focal length needs a very long, awkward tube. Reflector designs such as the Cassegrain fold the light path, so a long focal length fits in a short tube.
- Large apertures: a large lens is very heavy, can only be supported at its edge, and sags under its own weight, distorting the image; the glass must be flawless all the way through. A mirror can be supported across its whole back, only its front surface must be accurate, and it can be made thin and light. The largest refractor is about 1 m; reflectors are over 10 m.
- Multiple mirrors: very large primaries can be built from many smaller hexagonal segments (for example the Keck telescopes, JWST), which is impossible with lenses.

Disadvantages of reflectors: the secondary mirror blocks some light and causes diffraction spikes; open tubes let in dust and air currents; mirrors need recoating and aligning (collimation).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying reflectors are better because they magnify more. Magnification depends on focal lengths.
- Saying the Galilean telescope uses two convex lenses. Its eyepiece is concave.

### 11.19 to 11.23 Telescope properties

#### What you need to know

- Light grasp is directly proportional to the area of the objective, so to the square of its diameter.
- Aperture is related to the diameter of the objective.
- Field of view is the circle of sky visible through the eyepiece, in degrees or arcminutes.
- Resolution is better (finer) for a larger objective, and worse at longer wavelengths.
- Use magnification = fₒ ÷ fₑ.

#### Explained

- Aperture: the diameter of the objective lens or mirror (the "size" of a telescope).
- Light grasp (light-gathering power): how much light the telescope collects, proportional to the area of the objective, so to the diameter squared. Doubling the diameter collects 4 times as much light, showing fainter objects.
- Resolution (resolving power): the ability to show fine detail and separate close objects (such as a close double star). The smallest angle that can be resolved is proportional to wavelength ÷ diameter, so a larger aperture gives better resolution, and observing at longer wavelengths (such as radio) gives worse resolution for the same size of dish (13.25). Atmospheric seeing often limits ground-based resolution.
- Field of view: the circle of sky seen through the eyepiece. Higher magnification gives a smaller field of view. Wide fields suit large objects (the Pleiades, the Andromeda Galaxy); narrow fields suit planets and double stars.
- Magnification = focal length of objective ÷ focal length of eyepiece. A long-focal-length objective and short eyepiece give high magnification. Changing the eyepiece changes the magnification. Too much magnification just gives a larger, dimmer, blurrier image ("empty magnification").

#### Worked example

1. A telescope has fₒ = 1200 mm. With a 25 mm eyepiece, magnification = 1200 ÷ 25 = 48×. With a 6 mm eyepiece, 200×.
2. Light grasp: a 200 mm telescope compared with the eye's 7 mm pupil: (200 ÷ 7)² ≈ 816 times as much light.
3. A 300 mm telescope compared with a 100 mm telescope: (300 ÷ 100)² = 9 times the light grasp, and 3 times better resolution.
4. A student wants 150× with an eyepiece of 8 mm. fₒ = 150 × 8 = 1200 mm.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying light grasp is proportional to diameter. It is proportional to diameter squared.
- Dividing fₑ by fₒ.
- Saying a larger aperture gives a larger field of view.

### 11.24 Galileo's early observations

#### What you need to know

- The importance of Galileo's early telescope observations in establishing the heliocentric model.

#### Explained

From 1609 Galileo Galilei used his own small refracting telescopes (about 20× to 30× magnification) and saw:

- Four moons orbiting Jupiter (now called the Galilean moons). This showed that not everything orbits the Earth, and that a moving planet can keep its moons (an objection to a moving Earth had been that it would leave the Moon behind).
- The phases of Venus: Venus shows a full set of phases, from crescent to nearly full, and appears much larger as a crescent than when gibbous. This is only possible if Venus orbits the Sun. In the Ptolemaic model, Venus always lies between the Earth and the Sun and could only ever show crescent phases. This was strong evidence against the Ptolemaic model.
- Mountains and craters on the Moon, and spots on the Sun that moved (showing the Sun rotates): heavenly bodies were not perfect, unchanging spheres.
- Many more stars than can be seen with the naked eye, and that the Milky Way is made of countless stars.

These observations supported the heliocentric model of Copernicus and Kepler, though they did not by themselves rule out Brahe's hybrid model. Galileo's support for heliocentrism brought him into conflict with the Church authorities of the time.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying Galileo invented the telescope. He improved it and was among the first to use it for astronomy.
- Saying the moons of Jupiter proved the Earth goes round the Sun. They showed not everything orbits the Earth; the phases of Venus were the stronger evidence.

### 11.26, 11.27 and 11.28 Space probes

#### What you need to know

- Advantages and disadvantages of fly-by, orbiter, impactor and lander probes.
- An example of each with its target and major discoveries: fly-by New Horizons (outer Solar System); orbiter Juno (Jupiter) or Dawn (Vesta and Ceres); impactor Deep Impact (comet Tempel 1); lander Philae (comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko).
- A space probe must reach (close to) the Earth's escape velocity, which needs rockets (see 9.4).

#### Explained

| Type | What it does | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fly-by | Passes close to a body once and continues on its way; may visit several bodies using gravity assists | Cheaper; can visit several targets; needs no fuel to slow down | Only a brief look; only one side may be seen; no long-term monitoring |
| Orbiter | Fires rockets to slow down and go into orbit | Studies the whole body over months or years; maps the surface; observes changes and seasons | Needs extra fuel to brake into orbit, so heavier and costlier; cannot sample the surface directly |
| Impactor | Deliberately crashes into a body | Reveals material beneath the surface; can test methods of deflecting asteroids | One-off; the impactor is destroyed; needs another craft to observe |
| Lander | Lands on the surface (some carry rovers) | Direct measurements, chemical analysis, close-up images, can drill or sample | Very complex and risky landing; studies only one small area (unless a rover); most expensive |

Examples:

- New Horizons (fly-by): launched 2006, flew past Pluto in 2015, giving the first detailed images: a young, smooth plain of nitrogen ice (Sputnik Planitia), mountains of water ice, and a thin hazy atmosphere. In 2019 it flew past the Kuiper Belt object Arrokoth, a "contact binary" of two lobes stuck together.
- Juno (orbiter): in orbit around Jupiter since 2016 in a polar orbit. Discovered huge cyclone clusters at the poles, measured Jupiter's strong and irregular magnetic field, and found that its core is "fuzzy", spread out rather than compact.
- Dawn (orbiter): orbited the asteroid Vesta (2011 to 2012) and the dwarf planet Ceres (2015 to 2018), the first craft to orbit two bodies, using ion engines. Found that Vesta is a layered protoplanet with an iron core, and found bright salt deposits (sodium carbonate) in Occator crater on Ceres, evidence of briny water below the surface.
- Deep Impact (impactor): in 2005 it released a 370 kg copper impactor into comet Tempel 1 while the main craft watched. The cloud of ejected material showed the nucleus is fluffy, porous and covered in fine dust, with water ice and organic material underneath.
- Philae (lander): carried by the Rosetta orbiter, Philae landed on comet 67P in 2014, the first soft landing on a comet. Its harpoons failed and it bounced into a shadowed crack, so its batteries ran out after about 60 hours, but it detected organic molecules on the surface and found it was harder than expected. Rosetta showed the comet's water has a different deuterium ratio from Earth's oceans (11.13).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying all probes land. Most are fly-bys or orbiters.
- Mixing up Rosetta (orbiter) and Philae (lander).

### 11.29 and 11.30 Crewed exploration and Apollo

#### What you need to know

- Advantages and disadvantages of direct observation by crewed missions.
- The main features of the Apollo programme to land astronauts on the Moon.

#### Explained

Crewed missions: advantages: humans can make decisions on the spot, adapt to the unexpected, choose the most interesting samples, repair equipment, and do complex experiments; they inspire the public. Disadvantages: very much more expensive (life support, food, water, air, shielding, safe return); risk to life (radiation, accidents); limited by human endurance; the spacecraft must return to Earth, so it carries much more mass. Robotic probes can go further, for longer, and to places too hostile for people.

The Apollo programme (USA, NASA):

- Aim: to land astronauts on the Moon and return them safely, set by President Kennedy in 1961, in a "space race" with the Soviet Union.
- Hardware: the Saturn V three-stage rocket (the most powerful ever flown at the time) launched the Command and Service Module (CSM, which stayed in lunar orbit with one astronaut) and the Lunar Module (LM, which took two astronauts to the surface and back up to the CSM). Only the small Command Module returned to Earth, splashing down in the ocean.
- Key missions: Apollo 1 (1967, a fire killed three astronauts in a ground test); Apollo 8 (1968, first humans to orbit the Moon and see the far side); Apollo 11 (July 1969, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin made the first landing, in the Sea of Tranquility); Apollo 13 (1970, an oxygen tank exploded, landing cancelled, crew returned safely); Apollo 15 to 17 used the Lunar Roving Vehicle; Apollo 17 (1972) was the last.
- Results: six successful landings, twelve astronauts walked on the Moon; about 382 kg of rock and soil returned, used to date the lunar surface and to support the Giant Impact Hypothesis; seismometers and laser reflectors left on the surface (the reflectors are still used to measure the Earth-Moon distance precisely).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying all three astronauts landed. One stayed in orbit in the Command Module.
- Saying Apollo 13 landed on the Moon.

## 1.1 Minerals

Rocks are made of minerals: naturally occurring, inorganic solids with a definite chemical composition and an ordered crystal structure. This topic covers how the named minerals form, the tests used to identify them, and the laboratory techniques that image them at a tiny scale.

### 1.1a How minerals form, and identifying them

#### What you need to know

- Minerals form by:
  - crystallisation from a melt (quartz, feldspar, mica, olivine, augite);
  - metamorphic recrystallisation (calcite, garnet);
  - crystallisation from evaporating water (halite);
  - crystallisation as cement from pore waters flowing through sediment (quartz, calcite);
  - crystallisation from hydrothermal fluids in veins and faults (gangue minerals quartz and calcite; ore minerals haematite and galena).
- Use tests of physical properties (colour, hardness, streak, cleavage, lustre) and reaction with 0.5 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid to identify and distinguish the minerals on the data sheet; interpret data sheet information.

#### Explained

Ways minerals form:

- From a melt: as magma or lava cools, atoms join into crystals of silicate minerals. Olivine and augite (dark, iron- and magnesium-rich) crystallise at higher temperatures and are typical of basalt and peridotite; feldspar, mica and quartz crystallise at lower temperatures and are typical of granite.
- Metamorphic recrystallisation: heat and pressure rearrange the atoms of existing minerals into new or larger crystals without melting. Calcite in limestone recrystallises into larger interlocking crystals in marble; garnet grows in schists as new mineral from clay minerals at high temperature and pressure.
- From evaporating water: when sea water or salty lake water evaporates, the dissolved salts become concentrated and crystallise. Halite (rock salt) forms this way, as do gypsum and other evaporites.
- As cement: water moving through the pore spaces of buried sediment carries dissolved silica or calcium carbonate, which crystallises between the grains as quartz or calcite cement, binding the sediment into rock.
- From hydrothermal fluids: hot, mineral-rich water, often heated by nearby magma, flows along cracks such as joints and faults. As it cools, minerals crystallise and fill the cracks, forming mineral veins. The economically valuable ore minerals (haematite, an iron ore; galena, a lead ore) are mixed with worthless gangue minerals (quartz, calcite).

Physical tests:

| Property | Meaning and method |
| --- | --- |
| Colour | Often unreliable, because small impurities change it (quartz can be clear, white, pink, purple) |
| Streak | Colour of the mineral's powder, made by scraping it on an unglazed white tile; more reliable than colour. Haematite gives a red-brown streak even when the crystal is black or silvery; galena gives a grey-black streak |
| Hardness | Resistance to scratching, on Mohs' scale from 1 (talc) to 10 (diamond). Simple tools: fingernail about 2.5, copper coin about 3.5, steel pin or knife about 5.5. A mineral that scratches another is harder |
| Cleavage | Tendency to break along flat planes of weakness in the crystal structure. Mica has one perfect cleavage (peels into thin sheets); feldspar two at about 90°; calcite three not at right angles (rhombs); halite and galena three at 90° (cubes); quartz has no cleavage (it fractures with curved, glassy surfaces) |
| Lustre | How the surface reflects light: metallic (galena, some haematite), glassy/vitreous (quartz), pearly (mica, feldspar faces), dull/earthy |
| Acid test | A drop of dilute (0.5 mol dm⁻³) hydrochloric acid fizzes on calcite (carbon dioxide released); no reaction with quartz |
| Density | Felt by "hefting" in the hand: galena (about 7.5 g/cm³) feels much heavier than quartz (2.65 g/cm³) of the same size |
| Taste | Halite tastes salty (only on advice; generally not recommended in the lab) |

Typical identification features:

| Mineral | Key features |
| --- | --- |
| Quartz | Hardness 7 (scratches steel), glassy lustre, no cleavage, no acid reaction, often white or clear |
| Feldspar | Hardness 6, white, cream or pink, two cleavages, blocky crystals |
| Mica | Hardness 2.5 to 3, splits into thin flexible sheets; black (biotite) or silvery (muscovite) |
| Olivine | Hardness 6.5 to 7, olive green, glassy, granular |
| Augite | Hardness 6, dark green to black, two cleavages near 90° |
| Calcite | Hardness 3 (scratched by copper coin), rhombic cleavage, fizzes in dilute acid |
| Garnet | Hardness 6.5 to 7.5, red-brown rounded crystals in metamorphic rocks |
| Halite | Hardness 2.5, cubic crystals and cleavage, salty, dissolves in water |
| Haematite | Red-brown streak, metallic or earthy lustre, dense |
| Galena | Grey metallic lustre, cubic cleavage, very dense, grey-black streak, hardness 2.5 |

#### Worked example

A mineral scratches a copper coin but is scratched by a steel pin, has a red-brown streak and feels heavy. Hardness between about 3.5 and 5.5; red-brown streak; dense: haematite.

A white mineral is scratched by a copper coin and fizzes in dilute acid: calcite (not quartz, which is harder and does not fizz).

#### Common mistakes

- Relying on colour alone.
- Saying quartz has cleavage.
- Saying calcite is a gangue mineral only. It is gangue in veins, but also forms by other routes (cement, metamorphism).

### 1.1b Modern laboratory techniques

#### What you need to know

- Modern laboratory techniques can image mineral samples at a small scale and determine their chemistry, for example the scanning electron microscope (SEM) and the electron microprobe. (Access to these is not required.)

#### Explained

- Scanning electron microscope (SEM): a focused beam of electrons is scanned across a sample. Electrons scattered or emitted from the surface are detected to build a highly magnified image (thousands of times), showing tiny crystals, grain surfaces, microfossils and textures that a light microscope cannot resolve.
- Electron microprobe: an electron beam is focused on a tiny spot of a polished sample; the atoms emit X-rays with energies characteristic of each element. Measuring these gives the exact chemical composition of individual mineral grains, or even zones within a single crystal (for example how a crystal's composition changed as a magma cooled).

These techniques let geologists identify minerals too small to test by hand, and understand the conditions in which rocks formed.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying an SEM uses light. It uses an electron beam.

## 1.2 Igneous rocks and processes

Igneous rocks form when molten rock (magma underground, lava at the surface) cools and crystallises. This topic covers how to describe and classify them, why crystal size depends on cooling rate, how magma viscosity controls volcanic eruptions and volcano shape (with the jelly lava flow investigation), and the different igneous bodies and their structures.

### 1.2a and 1.2b Describing and classifying igneous rocks

#### What you need to know

- Igneous rocks have diagnostic properties: colour and texture (crystal size, equicrystalline or porphyritic, crystal orientation).
- Peridotite, basalt, andesite and granite can be classified by texture and mineralogy.
- Identify the named rocks in hand specimen, exposures, diagrams and photomicrographs from colour, crystal size (coarse > 3 mm, fine < 1 mm), random orientation, phenocrysts and groundmass, and mineralogy.

#### Explained

Texture terms:

- Crystalline and interlocking: crystals grew into each other as the melt solidified, leaving no gaps. Crystals are randomly oriented (pointing in all directions), unlike the aligned crystals in many metamorphic rocks.
- Crystal size: coarse-grained (crystals over 3 mm, easily seen); medium (1 to 3 mm); fine-grained (under 1 mm, need a hand lens or microscope).
- Equicrystalline (equigranular): crystals are all about the same size.
- Porphyritic: large crystals (phenocrysts) set in a much finer groundmass. This shows two stages of cooling: slow cooling deep down grew the large crystals, then the magma rose and the rest cooled quickly.
- Glassy: no crystals, from very fast cooling (obsidian; not a named rock in this spec).

Mineralogy and colour: rocks rich in silica and pale minerals (quartz, feldspar) are light-coloured (felsic, or acid); rocks rich in iron and magnesium minerals (olivine, augite) are dark (mafic, or basic; ultramafic for peridotite).

| Rock | Crystal size | Colour | Main minerals | Silica content |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Granite | Coarse | Pale: pink, white, grey with black specks | Quartz, feldspar, mica | High (acid / felsic) |
| Andesite | Fine, often porphyritic (feldspar or augite phenocrysts) | Medium grey | Feldspar, augite (some mica) | Intermediate |
| Basalt | Fine | Dark grey to black | Feldspar, augite, sometimes olivine | Low (basic / mafic) |
| Peridotite | Coarse | Dark green to black | Olivine, augite | Very low (ultrabasic / ultramafic) |

So the classification uses two axes: crystal size (coarse = intrusive, cooled slowly underground; fine = extrusive or small intrusions, cooled quickly) and composition (pale silica-rich to dark iron-magnesium rich).

#### Common mistakes

- Calling any dark rock basalt: check crystal size (peridotite is dark but coarse).
- Describing granite crystals as aligned.

### 1.2c Crystal size and cooling rate

#### What you need to know

- Crystal size in igneous rocks is related to the cooling rate of the magma.

#### Explained

As magma cools, crystals start to grow from many small centres. If it cools slowly (deep underground, insulated by surrounding rock, over thousands to millions of years), there is time for atoms to move to a few growing crystals, so they become large: a coarse-grained rock such as granite. If it cools quickly (lava at the surface, in air or water, in days to years), many crystals start but have little time to grow: a fine-grained rock such as basalt. Extremely rapid cooling forms glass.

Within one body, the edges cool fastest (against cold country rock), so they are finer-grained (a chilled margin) than the centre.

#### Common mistakes

- Linking crystal size to depth without explaining cooling rate.

### 1.2d Magma viscosity, eruptions and volcano shape

#### What you need to know

- Magma viscosity affects the type of volcanic activity and the shape of volcanoes: relatively passive fissure eruptions and violent central vent eruptions.
- Recognise volcano shapes from diagrams and photographs.
- Investigate factors affecting the length of lava flows using a jelly lava flow simulation, including viscosity (related to temperature) and slope angle.

#### Explained

Viscosity is resistance to flow ("thickness"). Silica-rich magma is more viscous because silica forms linked chains; hotter magma is less viscous.

- Basaltic magma (low silica, about 1100 to 1200 °C): low viscosity, runny. Gas escapes easily, so eruptions are relatively gentle (effusive), with lava fountains and long lava flows. It often erupts from long cracks (fissure eruptions), forming wide lava plateaus, or builds broad, gently sloping shield volcanoes (Hawaii, Iceland).
- Andesitic and rhyolitic magma (higher silica, cooler, about 800 to 1000 °C): high viscosity, sticky. Gas is trapped until pressure builds up, giving violent explosive eruptions from a central vent, with ash, pumice and pyroclastic flows. Lava does not travel far, so steep-sided cone-shaped composite (strato-) volcanoes form from layers of ash and lava (for example Mount St Helens, volcanoes of the Andes).

Jelly lava flow investigation: pour a fixed volume of jelly (or syrup) down a sloping board and measure the length of the flow after a fixed time (or the time to travel a fixed distance).

- Independent variable: temperature of the jelly (affecting viscosity) or slope angle (measured with a protractor or set square).
- Dependent variable: flow length (ruler) or time (stopwatch).
- Control variables: volume of jelly, surface, pouring height, time allowed.
- Repeat and average results; plot a graph of flow length against temperature or angle.
- Expected results: warmer (less viscous) jelly and steeper slopes give longer flows. This models why runny basaltic lava travels far and builds gentle slopes, and viscous lava forms short, thick flows near the vent.

#### Worked example

Flow lengths (cm) at 30° slope: jelly at 40 °C: 32, 35, 33; at 20 °C: 14, 12, 13. Means: 33.3 cm and 13.0 cm. The warmer, less viscous jelly flowed about 2.6 times as far.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying viscous magma produces long lava flows.
- Saying the jelly experiment's temperature affects slope rather than viscosity.

### 1.2e Igneous bodies, structures and field relationships

#### What you need to know

- Igneous bodies are distinguished by structure (columnar jointing, pillow lava), form (lava flows, sills, dykes, plutons) and field relationships.
- Identify them in specimens, exposures, diagrams and photographs; analyse simplified geological maps and cross-sections to interpret their modes of formation.

#### Explained

Forms:

| Body | Description | Crystal size |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lava flow | Extrusive: erupted at the surface and flowed over the land or sea floor; its top is often rubbly or full of gas bubbles (vesicles), and weathered or eroded before the next layer covered it | Fine |
| Sill | Intrusive: a sheet of magma injected along (parallel to) the bedding of the surrounding rocks; concordant | Medium to fine (thicker sills coarser in the centre) |
| Dyke | Intrusive: a vertical or steep sheet cutting across the bedding; discordant; often in swarms | Medium to fine |
| Pluton (batholith) | Large intrusive body, kilometres across, cooled deep down; usually granite | Coarse |

Structures:

- Columnar jointing: as a thick lava flow or sill cools, it contracts and cracks into regular columns, usually six-sided, at right angles to the cooling surfaces (Giant's Causeway, Fingal's Cave).
- Pillow lava: lava erupted under water forms rounded, pillow-shaped blobs with a glassy chilled skin, stacked on each other. Shows eruption under water, typically at mid-ocean ridges.

Field relationships distinguish a sill from a buried lava flow, which can look similar on a map:

| Feature | Sill (intrusive) | Lava flow (extrusive) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Baking (contact metamorphism) | Rocks above and below are baked | Only the rocks below are baked |
| Chilled margins | Top and bottom are finer-grained | Only the base is chilled; top cooled in air |
| Top surface | Smooth contact | Vesicular, weathered or rubbly top |
| Fragments | Pieces of the rocks above may be included (xenoliths) | Pieces of the lava may be eroded into the beds above |
| Cross-cutting | May step across from one layer to another (transgressive) | Follows the land surface |

Plutons are surrounded by a metamorphic aureole (1.4) and their contacts cut across the surrounding rocks. On a map, a dyke appears as a narrow straight band cutting across other outcrops; a pluton as a large rounded outcrop; a sill as a band following the bedding.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling a sill a dyke (or the reverse): a sill follows bedding; a dyke cuts across it.
- Saying a lava flow bakes rocks above it. It bakes only the ground it flowed over.

## 1.3 Sedimentary rocks and their fossil content

Sedimentary rocks record the surface environments of the past: rivers, deserts, beaches, reefs, deep seas and ice. This topic covers how sediment is made and moved, what grain size, shape and sorting reveal, porosity and permeability, the named sedimentary rocks and how to identify them, depositional environments and graphic logs, sedimentary structures, and fossils as environmental clues.

### 1.3a Weathering, erosion, transport and deposition

#### What you need to know

- Rock is broken down by weathering and erosion into particles of various sizes and dissolved materials, which are transported and deposited to form new sediments.
- Distinguish weathering from erosion and evaluate their significance in the characteristics and history of sedimentary rock.

#### Explained

- Weathering is the breakdown of rock in place, without movement:
  - Physical (mechanical): freeze-thaw (water expands by about 9% as it freezes in cracks, prising rock apart), thermal expansion and contraction, plant roots. Produces angular fragments with unchanged minerals.
  - Chemical: reactions with water, oxygen and acids. Rainwater with dissolved carbon dioxide (weak carbonic acid) dissolves calcite in limestone; feldspar decomposes to clay minerals; iron minerals oxidise (rust). Quartz is very resistant, so it survives to form sand grains.
  - Biological: plant roots, burrowing animals and organic acids.
- Erosion is the removal of material by a moving agent: rivers, the sea, wind, ice and gravity. Moving particles also wear each other down (abrasion and attrition).
- Transport: particles are carried by rolling, bouncing, in suspension, or dissolved in solution.
- Deposition: when the transporting agent loses energy (a river slows entering a lake or the sea; wind drops), the particles settle out, largest first. Dissolved material is deposited by evaporation (evaporites) or by organisms (shells forming limestone).
- Lithification: buried sediment is compacted (squeezed, water expelled) and cemented (minerals crystallise between grains, 1.1) into rock.

Significance: rocks dominated by angular fragments (breccia) suggest little transport after physical weathering; clean, rounded quartz sand suggests long transport and strong chemical weathering that removed less stable minerals.

#### Common mistakes

- Using "weathering" and "erosion" interchangeably. Weathering does not involve movement.

### 1.3b Grain size, shape, sorting and energy

#### What you need to know

- The grain size, shape and sorting of sediment depend on the energy of the transporting medium and the depositional environment (screes, rivers, shallow and deep seas, wind-formed dunes).
- Interpret distance of transport from shape and sorting, and energy of the environment from grain size.

#### Explained

- Grain size reflects energy: fast-flowing water or strong wind can move large particles; only fine particles are carried by slow water and settle in calm water. So coarse sediments (gravel) show high energy (fast rivers, storm beaches); fine mud shows very low energy (deep sea, lakes, lagoons).
- Grain shape (roundness and sphericity): angular grains have travelled little (scree at the foot of a cliff); grains become more rounded the further and longer they are transported, as corners are knocked off. Wind-blown sand grains are very well rounded and often "frosted" by impacts.
- Sorting: well sorted means grains are all of a similar size (the transporting agent selected one size range, such as wind-blown dune sand or beach sand); poorly sorted means a mixture of sizes (dumped quickly: scree, glacial till, flash flood deposits).

| Environment | Energy | Typical sediment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scree | Gravity; no transport | Very angular, poorly sorted, coarse |
| River channel | Moderate to high | Rounded pebbles and sand, moderately sorted |
| Shallow sea / beach | Moderate (waves) | Well sorted, rounded sand; shells |
| Deep sea | Very low | Fine mud and clay |
| Desert dunes (wind) | Low to moderate, selective | Very well sorted, very well rounded, frosted sand |

#### Common mistakes

- Saying rounded grains have travelled a short distance.
- Mixing up sorting (range of sizes) with roundness (shape).

### 1.3c Porosity and permeability

#### What you need to know

- The porosity and permeability of sedimentary rock depend on the characteristics of the original sediment and the degree of compaction and cementation.
- Distinguish permeable from impermeable rocks by observing water dropped on specimens and/or immersing them.

#### Explained

- Porosity: the percentage of a rock's volume that is pore space. Porosity (%) = volume of pores ÷ total volume × 100.
- Permeability: how easily a fluid (water, oil, gas) can flow through the rock, which needs connected pores (or fractures).

Factors:

- Sorting: well-sorted sediment has high porosity; in poorly sorted sediment, small grains fill the gaps between large ones.
- Grain shape and packing: rounded grains pack with more space than angular grains that interlock.
- Grain size: clay and mud can have high porosity but very low permeability, because the pores are tiny and water clings to the grains.
- Compaction and cementation: burial squeezes grains closer together and cement fills pores, reducing both porosity and permeability. An uncemented sandstone is porous and permeable; a well-cemented one much less so.

Tests: drop water onto a dry specimen: on a permeable rock (sandstone, chalk) it soaks in quickly; on an impermeable rock (shale, granite) it beads up or runs off. Immersion: weigh a dry rock, immerse it in water (bubbles show air escaping from pores), then weigh it again; the gain in mass in grams equals the volume of water absorbed in cm³, from which porosity can be calculated.

#### Worked example

A dry sandstone cube of volume 50 cm³ has mass 110 g. After soaking, its mass is 118 g. Water absorbed = 8 g = 8 cm³. Porosity = 8 ÷ 50 × 100 = 16%.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a porous rock must be permeable (clay is porous but impermeable).

### 1.3d Identifying sedimentary rocks

#### What you need to know

- Breccia, conglomerate, sandstone, shale, evaporites and limestone have diagnostic properties: colour, texture, reaction with acid, mineralogy and other features.
- Identify them using sediment comparators (grain size, shape and sphericity); coarse > 2 mm, fine < 1/16 mm; reaction with 0.5 mol dm⁻³ HCl.
- Construct and apply a classification system or key.

#### Explained

Sedimentary rocks are made of grains (clasts) or crystals; clastic rocks are often fragmental, with grains held by cement; layered; may contain fossils.

| Rock | Grain size | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Breccia | Coarse (clasts over 2 mm) | Angular fragments, poorly sorted |
| Conglomerate | Coarse (over 2 mm) | Rounded pebbles in a finer matrix |
| Sandstone | Medium (1/16 to 2 mm) | Sand grains (mostly quartz), feels gritty; red if iron-oxide cement; does not fizz unless calcite-cemented |
| Shale | Fine (under 1/16 mm) | Clay and silt; splits into thin layers (fissile); grey or black; soft |
| Limestone | Varies | Mainly calcite; fizzes strongly in dilute HCl; often contains fossils or shell fragments; chalk is a fine white limestone |
| Evaporites (halite, gypsum) | Crystalline | Formed by evaporation; halite tastes salty and dissolves; gypsum is soft (scratched by a fingernail) |

A sediment comparator is a card with grains of known sizes and shapes; a sample is compared with it using a hand lens to estimate grain size, roundness and sphericity.

Simple key:

1. Does it fizz in dilute acid? Yes → limestone. No → go to 2.
2. Is it made of interlocking crystals that are soft or salty? Yes → evaporite. No → go to 3.
3. Are grains over 2 mm? Yes → go to 4. No → go to 5.
4. Are the clasts angular? Yes → breccia. No (rounded) → conglomerate.
5. Can individual grains be seen or felt (gritty)? Yes → sandstone. No (smooth, fine, splits into layers) → shale.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling a coarse rock with rounded clasts a breccia.
- Forgetting that a sandstone with calcite cement can also fizz slightly.

### 1.3e Environments of deposition and graphic logs

#### What you need to know

- Rock type depends on environment: shallow marine (limestone, sandstone, conglomerate); deep marine (turbidites, black shale); terrestrial: rivers and deltas (shale, sandstone, conglomerate, coal); deserts by wind and water (breccia, desert sandstone); evaporating saline water (halite, gypsum); ice (glacial till/tillite).
- Use textures, structures, mineralogy and fossils to interpret environments; analyse sedimentary rocks on maps, cross-sections and graphic logs; construct a simple graphic log from bed thickness and grain size data.

#### Explained

| Environment | Rocks | Clues |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shallow marine | Limestone (warm, clear water, reefs), sandstone (beach and offshore), conglomerate (beach) | Marine fossils (corals, shells), ripple marks, cross bedding, well-sorted sand |
| Deep marine | Turbidites (repeated graded beds of sandstone and shale from underwater avalanches), black shale (fine mud in still, oxygen-poor water) | Graded bedding, fine lamination, graptolites in black shale, no shallow-water fossils |
| Rivers and deltas | Conglomerate (channels), sandstone (channels and bars), shale (floodplains), coal (swamp plants on deltas) | Cross bedding, channel shapes, plant fossils, desiccation cracks on floodplains |
| Desert | Breccia (scree, flash floods), desert sandstone (dunes) | Red colour (iron oxide), large-scale cross bedding, frosted well-rounded grains, desiccation cracks, no fossils |
| Evaporating saline water | Halite, gypsum | Shallow seas or salt lakes in hot dry climates |
| Ice | Glacial till / tillite | Very poorly sorted mixture of clay to boulders, angular clasts, sometimes scratched (striated) |

A graphic log is a vertical diagram of a sequence of sedimentary beds, drawn from the bottom (oldest) upwards. The vertical axis is thickness (to scale); the width of each bed shows its grain size (wider = coarser: clay, silt, sand, gravel); symbols show the rock type, structures and fossils. Logs show how environments changed over time, for example a coarsening-upward sequence as a delta built out into the sea.

#### Worked example

Field data (bottom to top): shale 2.0 m (clay); sandstone 1.5 m (medium sand) with cross bedding; conglomerate 0.5 m (pebbles); coal 0.3 m. Draw columns to scale (for example 1 cm = 0.5 m), with widths increasing from shale to sandstone to conglomerate, a narrow column for coal, and symbols. Interpretation: deposits of an advancing river or delta, ending in a swamp.

#### Common mistakes

- Drawing a graphic log from the top down.
- Ignoring fossils and structures when deciding the environment.

### 1.3f Sedimentary structures

#### What you need to know

- Lamination and bedding, cross bedding, graded bedding, ripple marks and desiccation cracks are characteristic of their environments.

#### Explained

| Structure | Description | Environment and meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bedding and lamination | Layers (beds over 1 cm; laminae under 1 cm) separated by bedding planes | Each layer is a change in deposition; fine lamination shows calm water |
| Cross bedding | Inclined layers within a bed, cut off at the top | Migrating ripples or dunes in rivers, shallow seas or deserts (large-scale in desert dunes); shows current direction (the layers slope down in the direction of flow) and which way up the rock is |
| Graded bedding | Grains coarsest at the base of a bed, getting finer upwards | A sudden flow that slowed down, such as a turbidity current in deep water; shows the way up |
| Ripple marks | Small ridges on a bedding surface | Symmetrical ripples: waves moving back and forth (shallow water); asymmetrical: one-way current (river, wind), with the steep side facing downstream |
| Desiccation (mud) cracks | Polygonal cracks in mudstone, often filled by sediment above | Wet mud dried out in the open air: floodplain, lake margin, desert; shows the way up (cracks taper downwards) |

#### Common mistakes

- Saying cross bedding is caused by folding. It forms during deposition.

### 1.3g Fossils as environmental indicators

#### What you need to know

- Reef-building corals (marine, shallow, warm); trilobites and ammonites (marine); plants (terrestrial, indicating past climate); trace fossils (tracks indicating terrestrial, burrows indicating shallow water).
- Identify trilobites, ammonites, corals, plants and trace fossils from their morphology.

#### Explained

| Fossil | Morphology | Environment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reef-building coral | Colonies of many small cups (corallites) with radiating partitions (septa), or solitary horn shapes | Shallow (light needed for algae living with them), warm, clear, marine water |
| Trilobite | Extinct arthropod; body in three lobes lengthwise (axial lobe and two side lobes) and three parts from head to tail (head shield / cephalon, segmented thorax, tail / pygidium); compound eyes | Marine (Palaeozoic) |
| Ammonite | Extinct cephalopod; coiled, chambered shell with complex suture lines where internal walls meet the shell (2.3) | Marine, swimming (Mesozoic) |
| Plants | Leaves, stems, bark impressions, often carbonised | Land (terrestrial); leaf types indicate the climate (large ferns of coal swamps suggest warm, humid tropics) |
| Trace fossils | Evidence of activity rather than the body: footprints, tracks, burrows | Footprints of land animals: terrestrial (or very shallow water); burrows: usually shallow water sea floor |

The fossils present are clues; the best interpretations combine fossils with rock type and structures.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying ammonites lived on land.
- Treating any fossil as proof of deep water: corals indicate shallow water.

## 1.4 Metamorphic rocks and processes

Metamorphic rocks are existing rocks changed by heat and/or pressure without melting. This topic covers what metamorphism does, the textures and minerals of slate, schist, marble and metaquartzite, and how contact and regional metamorphism differ, including metamorphic aureoles.

### 1.4a Metamorphism and recrystallisation

#### What you need to know

- Metamorphic rocks result from increased temperature and/or pressure acting on pre-existing rocks, causing recrystallisation to form new minerals and textures.
- Identify the named rocks in hand specimen from crystal size (coarse, fine), crystal orientation (aligned, random) and reaction with dilute hydrochloric acid.

#### Explained

When rocks are buried deeply, squeezed in mountain building, or heated by nearby magma, their minerals are no longer stable. In the solid state, atoms move and rearrange:

- Existing minerals grow into larger, interlocking crystals (recrystallisation), for example calcite in limestone becomes coarser in marble.
- New minerals grow that are stable at higher temperature and pressure, for example clay minerals in mudstone change to mica, then garnet.
- Under directed pressure, platy minerals (mica, clay) grow lined up at right angles to the pressure, giving an aligned texture.
- Fossils, sedimentary structures and original grains are usually destroyed.

The rock does not melt; if it did, it would form igneous rock. The original rock is called the parent rock (protolith). All metamorphic rocks are crystalline, with interlocking crystals.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying metamorphic rocks form by melting.
- Saying metamorphism happens at the surface. It needs heat or pressure beyond surface conditions.

### 1.4b and 1.4c Metamorphic textures and mineralogy

#### What you need to know

- Slate, schist, marble and metaquartzite have diagnostic textures (crystal size and orientation): non-foliated texture and foliated texture (slaty cleavage and schistosity).
- Schist, marble and metaquartzite have diagnostic mineralogy.
- Identify the features of a metamorphic aureole on diagrams, maps and cross-sections; use characteristics to interpret contact and regional metamorphism.

#### Explained

Foliated textures (minerals aligned in parallel planes, from directed pressure):

- Slaty cleavage: very fine-grained clay and mica crystals aligned so the rock splits into thin, flat, hard sheets, at an angle to the original bedding (often seen as faint bedding stripes crossing the cleavage). Forms at low temperature and pressure.
- Schistosity: coarser, visible, aligned flakes of mica give a shiny, wavy surface; the rock splits along the mica layers. Forms at higher temperature and pressure.

Non-foliated texture: crystals are interlocking and randomly oriented, with no alignment. Typical of rocks made of mainly one mineral that does not form flat crystals (calcite, quartz), or of rocks heated without directed pressure.

| Rock | Parent rock | Texture | Crystal size | Minerals and tests |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Slate | Shale / mudstone | Foliated: slaty cleavage | Very fine (crystals invisible) | Clay minerals and fine mica; dark grey; rings when tapped; no fizz |
| Schist | Shale / mudstone (at higher grade) | Foliated: schistosity | Medium to coarse | Mica (shiny), quartz, often garnet crystals (porphyroblasts); no fizz |
| Marble | Limestone | Non-foliated, interlocking crystals | Medium to coarse, sugary | Calcite; fizzes in dilute HCl; scratched by steel; often white, fossils destroyed |
| Metaquartzite | Sandstone (quartz-rich) | Non-foliated, interlocking crystals | Medium | Quartz; very hard (scratches steel); no fizz; breaks through grains, not round them |

Metaquartzite and sandstone: both mostly quartz, but in sandstone the rock breaks around the grains (gritty surface), while in metaquartzite the grains are fused, so fractures pass straight through them (smooth, glassy surface).

Contact metamorphism:

- Cause: heat from an igneous intrusion (pluton, dyke or sill) affecting surrounding rocks. Pressure is low.
- Extent: a zone around the intrusion called a metamorphic aureole, from centimetres (dykes) to kilometres (large plutons) wide. The degree of change is greatest next to the contact and decreases outwards.
- Textures: non-foliated, because there is no directed pressure. Shale becomes hard, splintery "baked" rock (hornfels) with spotted texture further out; limestone becomes marble; sandstone becomes metaquartzite.
- On a map: a ring or band (often shown by a special ornament) surrounding the outcrop of a pluton, wider where the contact dips gently and narrower where it is steep.

Regional metamorphism:

- Cause: high pressure (including directed pressure) and high temperature over very large areas, during mountain building at convergent plate boundaries (2.2).
- Extent: hundreds to thousands of square kilometres.
- Textures: foliated. With increasing grade (temperature and pressure), mudstone changes progressively: slate → (phyllite) → schist → (gneiss). Limestone and sandstone become marble and metaquartzite but may show some alignment.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying marble is foliated.
- Confusing slaty cleavage (a metamorphic structure) with mineral cleavage (a property of a crystal) or with bedding.
- Saying contact metamorphism produces schist.

## 1.5 Deformational structures

Sedimentary rocks are deposited in nearly flat layers. When they are found tilted, bent, broken or eroded, the rocks record tectonic forces. This topic covers measuring dip and strike, folds, faults and unconformities, and how to recognise them in the field, in photographs, and on maps and cross-sections.

### 1.5a Evidence of tectonic activity; field safety; dip and strike

#### What you need to know

- The rock record provides evidence of tectonic activity.
- Describe safety precautions for visiting field exposures.
- Measure strike and dip; analyse strike and dip measurements to describe and interpret rock structures in 3D.

#### Explained

Tectonic activity is recorded by tilted beds, folds, faults, unconformities, cleavage and metamorphism (1.4).

Field safety (in line with the Geological Code of Conduct): wear a hard hat near cliffs and quarry faces, and stout boots; high-visibility clothing in quarries; goggles when hammering (and only hammer where permitted; collect from loose material where possible); check tide times on coasts and do not get cut off; keep away from unstable cliff edges and the base of overhangs; check weather; work in groups and tell someone your plans; carry a first-aid kit and phone; get landowner permission; follow the Countryside Code and do not damage sites.

Dip and strike describe the orientation of a planar surface (a bedding plane, fault or cleavage):

- Dip: the angle of greatest slope, measured downwards from the horizontal (0° flat to 90° vertical), together with the direction in which the surface slopes down (dip direction, a compass bearing).
- Strike: the compass direction of a horizontal line on the surface. It is at right angles to the dip direction.
- Measured with a compass clinometer: the clinometer (a pendulum or bubble against a protractor) measures the dip angle; the compass measures the strike or dip direction. Written as, for example, 25°/140° (dip 25° towards bearing 140°, south-east).
- Map symbol: a T-shaped (or arrow) symbol; the long line shows strike, a short tick or arrow points in the dip direction, with the dip angle written beside it.

Analysing dip data: beds all dipping the same way show a tilted sequence; dips facing away from each other on either side of a line show an anticline (antiform); dips facing towards each other show a syncline (synform); a sudden change in dip or strike across a line may show a fault or unconformity.

#### Worked example

At outcrop A the bedding dips 30° towards 090° (east); at outcrop B, 200 m east, it dips 30° towards 270° (west). The beds dip towards each other, so there is a synform between A and B, with its axis running north-south.

#### Common mistakes

- Measuring the dip in the direction of strike, which gives 0°.
- Giving dip without the direction.

### 1.5b Folding

#### What you need to know

- Folding is caused by compressional tectonic stress.
- Interpret horizontal beds, dipping beds and folded beds (antiform, synform, axial plane trace, limb) in exposures, diagrams, photographs, maps and cross-sections.

#### Explained

When rocks are squeezed (compression), especially when warm and deep or under slow, steady stress, they bend (ductile deformation) rather than break, forming folds.

- Antiform: an arch-shaped fold, with limbs dipping away from the centre (hinge). If the oldest rocks are in the core it is an anticline.
- Synform: a trough-shaped fold, with limbs dipping towards the centre. If the youngest rocks are in the core it is a syncline.
- Limbs: the two sides of the fold.
- Hinge: the line of greatest curvature.
- Axial plane: the imaginary surface that divides the fold into two halves as symmetrically as possible. The axial plane trace is the line where it meets the ground surface (shown on maps by a line with outward arrows for an antiform, inward for a synform).
- Fold types: symmetrical (limbs dip at equal angles, vertical axial plane), asymmetrical (one limb steeper), overturned (one limb tilted past vertical), recumbent (lying on its side). The steeper limb and inclined axial plane show the direction the compression came from.

On a geological map of eroded folds, beds outcrop as repeated bands symmetrical about the fold axis: in an eroded antiform the oldest beds are in the middle; in an eroded synform the youngest beds are in the middle.

Horizontal beds outcrop following the contours on a map; vertical beds outcrop in straight lines regardless of topography; dipping beds cross contours at an angle (they "V" in valleys).

#### Common mistakes

- Swapping antiform (arch) and synform (trough).
- Saying folds are formed by tension. They form by compression.

### 1.5c Faulting

#### What you need to know

- Faulting is caused by tectonic stress: compressional, tensional and shear.
- Interpret normal faults, reverse/thrust faults, strike-slip faults and fault displacement in exposures, diagrams, photographs, maps and cross-sections.

#### Explained

A fault is a fracture along which the rocks on either side have moved relative to each other. Rocks fault (brittle deformation) when stress is applied quickly or near the surface where they are cold and brittle. Faults often show crushed rock (fault breccia), polished, scratched surfaces (slickensides) and dragged beds.

On a dipping fault, the block above the fault plane is the hanging wall; the block below is the footwall.

| Fault | Stress | Movement | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | Tension (pulling apart) | Hanging wall moves down relative to the footwall | Crust is extended (lengthened); typical at divergent boundaries and rift valleys |
| Reverse | Compression | Hanging wall moves up relative to the footwall; fault plane steep | Crust is shortened; beds repeated |
| Thrust | Compression | A low-angle reverse fault (dip under 45°); older rocks pushed over younger | Large shortening in mountain belts |
| Strike-slip (transform) | Shear (sideways) | Blocks slide horizontally past each other; fault plane near vertical | Offsets features sideways (for example the San Andreas Fault) |

Displacement (throw) is the amount of movement: vertical throw is the vertical offset of a bed across the fault; in a strike-slip fault the horizontal offset is measured. On maps, faults are drawn as heavy lines; outcrops of beds are offset across them; a downthrow side may be marked with a tick. A rift valley is a block dropped down between two normal faults (a graben); a raised block is a horst.

#### Worked example

On a cross-section, the top of a sandstone bed is at 120 m below the surface on one side of a normal fault and 165 m below on the other. Throw = 165 − 120 = 45 m, downthrown on the hanging-wall side.

#### Common mistakes

- Mixing up normal and reverse: in a normal fault the hanging wall goes down (extension).
- Calling a reverse fault with a gentle dip a normal fault because the hanging wall "looks lower".

### 1.5d Unconformities

#### What you need to know

- Unconformities are gaps in the rock record. Angular unconformities form by a sequence: deformation, uplift, erosion, then later deposition.
- Identify them in the field, diagrams, photographs, maps and cross-sections; use them to interpret geological history.

#### Explained

An unconformity is a surface of erosion or non-deposition separating older rocks below from younger rocks above. It represents a time gap, which may be millions of years, during which rocks were eroded or not deposited.

Angular unconformity: the older beds below are tilted or folded at a different angle from the younger beds above. Sequence of events:

1. Deposition of the lower sediments (horizontal).
2. Lithification.
3. Deformation: folding or tilting (and perhaps faulting, metamorphism, intrusion).
4. Uplift above sea level.
5. Erosion, producing an irregular surface that cuts across the tilted beds.
6. Subsidence (or sea level rise) and deposition of new horizontal sediments on the eroded surface.

Features: the lower beds are cut off at the unconformity; the base of the upper sequence often has a basal conglomerate containing pebbles of the older rocks; faults and dykes in the lower sequence may stop at the unconformity (showing they are older than the upper beds). Hutton's unconformity at Siccar Point, Scotland, convinced him of the vast length of geological time (2.3).

On a geological map, the younger beds cross the outcrops of several older beds at an angle; their base line "cuts across" older boundaries and faults.

#### Common mistakes

- Missing out uplift or deformation in the sequence of events.
- Saying an unconformity is a fault. There is no movement along it; it is a buried erosion surface.

## 2.1 The rock cycle

The three rock groups (igneous, sedimentary, metamorphic) are not fixed: over geological time, rocks are continually changed from one type to another. This topic covers the rock cycle as a system powered by energy, and the very different rates at which its processes operate.

### 2.1a The rock cycle and energy transfer

#### What you need to know

- Sedimentary, metamorphic and igneous processes and rocks are linked by the rock cycle, powered by energy transfer, over geological time.
- Interpret rock cycle diagrams.

#### Explained

Processes and the paths between rock types:

- Igneous rocks form by crystallisation of magma or lava.
- At the surface, any rock is weathered and eroded; sediment is transported and deposited, then buried, compacted and cemented (lithified) into sedimentary rock.
- Buried rocks heated and/or put under pressure undergo metamorphism to form metamorphic rocks.
- At very high temperatures rocks melt to form magma, which can rise and crystallise as new igneous rock.
- Uplift (during mountain building) brings buried igneous, metamorphic and sedimentary rocks back to the surface, where they are weathered and eroded again.

There are many shortcuts: an igneous rock can be metamorphosed without ever being weathered; a sedimentary rock can be uplifted and eroded straight into new sediment; a metamorphic rock can be weathered at the surface.

Energy sources:

- External: energy from the Sun drives the water cycle and the weather, which power weathering, erosion and transport (rain, rivers, wind, waves, ice). Gravity moves sediment downhill and makes it settle.
- Internal: heat from inside the Earth (from radioactive decay and heat left from the Earth's formation) drives mantle convection and plate movements, melting, metamorphism and uplift.

So the rock cycle links to plate tectonics (2.2): subduction carries rocks down to be metamorphosed and melted; collisions uplift mountains that are then eroded.

#### Common mistakes

- Drawing the rock cycle as a single circle in one order. There are many possible pathways.
- Saying the Sun's energy causes melting.

### 2.1b Rates of rock cycle processes

#### What you need to know

- Rock cycle processes take place at different rates, from seconds to millions of years (catastrophism versus gradualism, for example meteorite impact versus river erosion).
- Distinguish between processes in the rock record that happened at different rates.

#### Explained

- Very fast (seconds to days): meteorite impacts, volcanic eruptions, pyroclastic flows, earthquakes and fault movements, landslides, turbidity currents, flash floods.
- Moderate (years to thousands of years): river deposition building floodplains and deltas, dune migration, cooling of lava flows, growth of reefs.
- Very slow (millions of years): deposition of thick sequences of fine mud in the deep sea (perhaps a few millimetres per thousand years), cooling of large plutons, mountain building and regional metamorphism, erosion of mountain ranges, plate movements (a few centimetres per year).

Gradualism (linked to Hutton and Lyell's uniformitarianism, 2.3) says the Earth has been shaped mainly by slow, steady processes acting over vast time. Catastrophism says sudden, violent events have shaped the Earth. Modern geology accepts both: most of the rock record built up slowly, but it is punctuated by rare catastrophic events (such as the K/Pg impact, 2.5 and 3.1).

Evidence in the rock record: a single graded bed or a turbidite was deposited in minutes or hours; a thick sequence of laminated black shale represents a very long time; an impact crater or a layer of ash forms almost instantly; coarse crystals in granite show slow cooling.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying all geological processes are slow.

## 2.2 Plate tectonics

Plate tectonics is the unifying theory of geology: it explains earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain belts and ocean basins. This topic covers the Earth's layers, the lithosphere and asthenosphere, how the theory grew from continental drift, the evidence, what happens at each type of plate boundary, and how the theory is still being tested. Background on the Earth's layers is also in [Astronomy 01 Planet Earth](../astronomy-edexcel/01-planet-earth.md).

### 2.2a, 2.2b and 2.2c The Earth's structure, lithosphere and plates

#### What you need to know

- The Earth has a concentric structure based on chemical properties (crust, mantle, core) and on mechanical behaviour (lithosphere, asthenosphere).
- The lithosphere is a cold, rigid outer shell of crust and uppermost mantle, underlain by the weaker asthenosphere in the upper mantle.
- The lithosphere is divided into rigid tectonic plates that move relative to each other, by mechanisms not yet completely understood.

#### Explained

Chemical layers:

- Crust: oceanic crust (basalt, thin: about 7 km, denser, young) and continental crust (granitic on average, thick: 30 to 70 km, less dense, can be very old).
- Mantle: silicate rock rich in iron and magnesium (peridotite), to about 2900 km.
- Core: iron and nickel; liquid outer core, solid inner core.

Mechanical layers (by how the rock behaves):

- Lithosphere: the crust plus the uppermost mantle, about 100 km thick (thinner under oceans, thicker under continents). Cold and rigid; it breaks into plates.
- Asthenosphere: below the lithosphere, in the upper mantle, to about 350 km. Hot enough to be weak and to flow very slowly (a tiny amount of it may be molten), so the lithosphere plates can move over it.

There are about seven major plates and many smaller ones, moving a few centimetres per year. Proposed driving mechanisms include convection currents in the mantle, ridge push (elevated, hot ridges sliding down and away) and slab pull (cold, dense subducting plates pulling the rest of the plate behind them, thought to be the largest force). The exact balance is still not fully understood.

#### Common mistakes

- Equating lithosphere with crust. The lithosphere includes the uppermost mantle.
- Saying the asthenosphere is liquid magma.

### 2.2d and 2.2e From continental drift to plate tectonics; the evidence

#### What you need to know

- Plate tectonic theory developed from continental drift as new evidence appeared: Wegener (1915) proposed continental drift; evidence for sea-floor spreading came from Hess (1960), Vine and Matthews (1963) and J. Tuzo Wilson (1965).
- Evidence for plate tectonics and for the direction and rate of movement: jigsaw fit, fossil distributions, heat flow, magnetic stripes, age of the ocean floor, GPS data.
- Use maps of earthquakes, volcanoes and mountain belts to interpret processes at plate boundaries.

#### Explained

Alfred Wegener (1915) proposed that the continents were once joined in a supercontinent (Pangaea) and had drifted apart. His evidence: the jigsaw fit of coastlines (especially South America and Africa, even better at the edge of the continental shelf); matching fossils (such as the land reptile Mesosaurus and the plant Glossopteris) on continents now separated by oceans; matching rock types and mountain belts across oceans; evidence of past climates in the wrong places (glacial deposits in tropical areas, coal in Antarctica). But he could not explain a mechanism, and most geologists rejected drift.

New evidence from the ocean floor after the Second World War:

- Harry Hess (1960) proposed sea-floor spreading: new ocean floor forms at mid-ocean ridges from rising mantle material and spreads outwards, and is destroyed in ocean trenches. He used maps of the sea floor (ridges, trenches) and high heat flow at ridges.
- Vine and Matthews (1963) explained the magnetic stripes on the sea floor: as basalt lava cools at a ridge, iron minerals record the direction of the Earth's magnetic field. The field reverses every few hundred thousand years, so the sea floor has stripes of normal and reversed magnetism, symmetrical on either side of the ridge, like a tape recorder. This confirmed sea-floor spreading.
- J. Tuzo Wilson (1965) identified transform faults, which offset ridges, and described the plates as rigid units with three kinds of boundary; he also explained hotspot chains (such as Hawaii) as plates moving over a fixed hotspot. These completed the theory of plate tectonics.

Summary of evidence:

| Evidence | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| Jigsaw fit of continents | Continents were once joined |
| Fossil distributions | Land animals and plants could not have crossed oceans, so the continents were joined |
| Heat flow | High at ridges (hot rising mantle), low at trenches (cold sinking slabs) |
| Magnetic stripes | Symmetrical pattern of reversals either side of ridges: new crust forms at ridges and spreads |
| Age of ocean floor | Youngest at ridges, older away from them; nowhere older than about 180 million years, so ocean floor is destroyed; thin sediment at ridges |
| GPS data | Satellites measure present plate movement directly: centimetres per year, in the directions predicted |
| Global distributions | Earthquakes, volcanoes and young mountain belts are concentrated in narrow belts that outline the plates |

Rate calculation: rate = distance ÷ time.

#### Worked example

Basalt 150 km from a ridge axis is 6 million years old. Rate of spreading on that side = 150 km ÷ 6 Ma = 25 km per million years = 2.5 cm per year (because 25 km = 2 500 000 cm, divided by 1 000 000 years). The total opening rate (both sides) is 5 cm per year.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying Wegener proposed plate tectonics. He proposed continental drift without a workable mechanism.
- Saying magnetic stripes show the continents' positions. They record reversals of the field in new crust.

### 2.2f Plate boundaries

#### What you need to know

- Relative plate movements produce different magma types, structures and topography at different boundaries:
  - Divergent: basalt extrusion, sea-floor spreading, basaltic magma by partial melting of the upper mantle, ocean ridges, high heat flow, rift valleys, abyssal plain (example: Mid-Atlantic Ridge).
  - Conservative: earthquakes, transform faults (example: San Andreas fault zone).
  - Convergent: oceanic-oceanic (island arc and trench, examples: Java-Sumatra, Caribbean); oceanic-continental (active continental margin, subduction zone, Benioff zone, partial melting giving andesitic and granitic magma, example: the Andes); continental-continental (mountain building, folding, thrust faulting, crustal melting giving granite, regional metamorphism, example: the Himalaya).
- Interpret plate movements and boundary types from magmatic, seismic and topographic data on maps and diagrams.

#### Explained

Divergent (constructive) boundaries: plates move apart. As the lithosphere thins and pressure on the hot mantle drops, the upper mantle partially melts (only a small fraction melts), producing basaltic magma that rises and erupts as basalt (often pillow lava) or intrudes as dykes, forming new oceanic crust. Features: mid-ocean ridges (an underwater mountain chain with a central rift valley), high heat flow, shallow earthquakes, fissure eruptions; away from the ridge, the cooled crust subsides to form the flat, deep abyssal plain covered with fine sediment. On land, continental rifting forms rift valleys bounded by normal faults (East African Rift). Example: Mid-Atlantic Ridge (Iceland is a part above sea level).

Conservative boundaries: plates slide past each other along transform faults. No crust is made or destroyed; no volcanoes. Stress builds up and is released as frequent, often strong, shallow earthquakes. Example: San Andreas fault zone (Pacific and North American plates).

Convergent (destructive) boundaries: plates move towards each other.

1. Oceanic-oceanic: one oceanic plate (usually the older, colder, denser one) subducts beneath the other into the mantle, forming a deep ocean trench. Water released from the sinking slab lowers the melting point of the mantle above, causing partial melting; magma rises to build a curved chain of volcanic islands (an island arc). Examples: Java-Sumatra (Indonesia), Caribbean (Lesser Antilles).
2. Oceanic-continental: dense oceanic lithosphere subducts beneath less dense continental lithosphere, forming a trench offshore and an active continental margin. Earthquakes occur along the sinking slab, getting deeper inland (the inclined zone of earthquakes is the Benioff zone, down to about 700 km). Partial melting produces andesitic magma (explosive composite volcanoes) and granitic magma (large plutons); compression folds and thrusts the crust into fold mountains. Example: the Andes (Nazca plate under the South American plate).
3. Continental-continental: when an ocean between two continents has closed, neither continent subducts (both are too buoyant). The crust is crumpled and thickened into huge fold mountains with folding and thrust faulting. Thickened crust partly melts to form granite; deep rocks undergo regional metamorphism. Few or no volcanoes; shallow to intermediate earthquakes over a wide zone. Example: the Himalaya (India colliding with Asia).

Interpreting data: a linear belt of shallow earthquakes along a ridge with basalt and high heat flow → divergent. Earthquakes getting deeper in one direction, with a trench and andesite volcanoes → subduction (the slab dips towards the deeper earthquakes). Shallow earthquakes, no volcanoes, offset features → conservative. Wide belt of shallow earthquakes, high mountains, no volcanoes → continental collision.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying continental plates subduct beneath oceanic plates. Denser oceanic lithosphere subducts.
- Saying subduction melts the sinking plate completely to make magma. Melting is mainly in the mantle wedge above, helped by water from the slab.
- Putting volcanoes at conservative boundaries.

### 2.2g Continuing re-evaluation of plate theory

#### What you need to know

- Plate theory is re-evaluated in the light of new evidence, for example seismic tomography and ocean drilling (RRS James Cook; JOIDES Resolution Expedition 360, 2016).

#### Explained

- Seismic tomography uses earthquake waves recorded at stations around the world to build 3D images of the mantle, like a medical CT scan. Seismic waves travel faster through cold, dense rock and slower through hot rock. Images show cold subducted slabs sinking deep into the mantle (some to the core-mantle boundary) and hot plumes rising, helping test ideas about mantle convection and driving forces.
- Ocean drilling: scientific drilling ships recover cores of sediment and rock from the ocean floor. They confirmed the ages of ocean crust increase away from ridges and allow study of the lower crust and mantle. JOIDES Resolution Expedition 360 (2016) drilled into lower oceanic crust at Atlantis Bank in the south-west Indian Ocean to understand how slow-spreading ridges form crust and how the mantle is exposed. The research ship RRS James Cook has carried out related seabed surveys and sampling.

The theory is still developing: questions remain about what exactly drives plates, how subduction starts, and the role of mantle plumes. This shows science changes as new evidence is collected.

#### Common mistakes

- Treating plate tectonics as finished science with nothing left to discover.

## 2.3 Geochronological principles

Geochronology is working out the order and age of geological events. This topic covers the principles of relative dating, zone fossils (cephalopods and graptolites), absolute dating with radioactive decay, and how ideas about the age of the Earth developed.

### 2.3a Stratigraphic principles and relative dating

#### What you need to know

- Uniformitarianism: the present is the key to the past.
- Original horizontality, lateral continuity and superposition of strata.
- Relative dating using included fragments and cross-cutting relationships.
- Apply these principles to exposures, diagrams, photographs, maps and cross-sections to work out the sequence of events.

#### Explained

- Uniformitarianism (James Hutton, popularised by Charles Lyell): the processes we see today (rivers depositing sand, volcanoes erupting, waves making ripples) also operated in the past, so we can interpret ancient rocks by comparing them with modern environments. For example, ancient ripple marks in sandstone formed like ripples on a modern beach.
- Original horizontality: sediments are deposited in nearly horizontal layers. If beds are tilted or folded, they were deformed after deposition.
- Lateral continuity: a bed extends sideways in all directions until it thins out or meets the edge of its basin. Matching beds on either side of a valley were once continuous; the valley was eroded later.
- Superposition: in an undisturbed sequence, younger beds lie on top of older ones. (Check the way up using structures such as graded bedding, cross bedding or desiccation cracks, in case the sequence has been overturned.)
- Cross-cutting relationships: any feature that cuts across rocks (a fault, dyke, intrusion, unconformity, erosion surface) is younger than the rocks it cuts.
- Included fragments: fragments of one rock inside another must be older than the rock that contains them. Pebbles of granite in a conglomerate show the granite is older; xenoliths of country rock inside an intrusion show the country rock is older.
- Also: baking (contact metamorphism) shows the intrusion is younger than the baked rock.

Working out a geological history: list events from oldest to youngest: deposition of each bed (using superposition), then any folding or tilting, intrusions (with baking), faults (which cut beds and possibly intrusions), uplift and erosion (unconformity), later deposition, and the present erosion.

#### Worked example

A cross-section shows: beds A (bottom), B, C tilted at 30°; a dyke cutting A, B and C; a fault cutting the dyke and the beds; an erosion surface on top; horizontal bed D above it, not cut by the fault.
Sequence: deposition of A, B, C → tilting → intrusion of dyke → faulting → uplift and erosion (unconformity) → deposition of D.

#### Common mistakes

- Placing a dyke before the beds it cuts.
- Forgetting uplift and erosion before the unconformity.

### 2.3b and 2.3c Zone fossils

#### What you need to know

- Rocks can be dated and correlated using the evolutionary change of zone fossils over time.
- Cephalopods (goniatites, ceratites, ammonites: suture lines) and graptolites (stipes, thecae) show changes in shape over time used for dating and correlation.

#### Explained

A zone fossil (index fossil) is a fossil species that is useful for dating rocks because it:

- evolved rapidly, so each species lived for only a short time (a narrow time range);
- was widespread geographically (often swimming or floating in the sea);
- was abundant and easily preserved;
- is easy to identify.

Rocks in different places containing the same zone fossil are the same age (correlation). A zone is the span of rock containing a particular species.

Cephalopods (marine animals with a coiled, chambered shell): where the internal walls (septa) meet the outer shell, they form a suture line, seen on fossils with the shell removed. The suture became more complex over time, so it shows age:

- Goniatites (Devonian to Permian, Upper Palaeozoic): simple zig-zag suture with smooth, rounded saddles and pointed lobes.
- Ceratites (Triassic): saddles smooth and rounded, but lobes frilled (finely crinkled).
- Ammonites (Jurassic and Cretaceous): very complex suture, both lobes and saddles intricately frilled, like fern leaves.

Graptolites (Lower Palaeozoic: Ordovician and Silurian): colonial floating animals, preserved as thin carbon films in black shale (deep water). Each colony has one or more branches (stipes) carrying cups (thecae) that housed individual animals. Trends over time: the number of stipes decreased (many-stiped early forms to two-stiped "tuning fork" types to single-stiped forms); stipes changed from hanging down (pendent) to pointing upward (scandent); thecae changed from simple to more complex shapes (hooked, isolated). These changes allow precise dating of Lower Palaeozoic shales.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying any common fossil is a good zone fossil. Long-lived species are poor zone fossils.
- Mixing up the suture patterns of goniatites and ammonites.

### 2.3d Radiometric (absolute) dating

#### What you need to know

- The decay of radioactive materials provides absolute dates for some rocks and minerals.
- Carry out a simple analysis of a radioactive mineral's age using half-life (parent-daughter ratio, unstable parent, stable daughter).

#### Explained

Some minerals contain radioactive (unstable) isotopes, the parent, which decay at a known, constant rate into stable daughter isotopes (for example uranium-238 decays to lead-206; potassium-40 decays to argon-40). The half-life is the time for half the parent atoms to decay. It is unaffected by temperature or pressure.

When a mineral crystallises (in an igneous rock), it contains parent but (ideally) no daughter. As time passes, the parent decreases and the daughter increases. Measuring the ratio of parent to daughter gives the time since crystallisation:

| Half-lives passed | Parent remaining | Parent : daughter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 100% | 1 : 0 |
| 1 | 50% | 1 : 1 |
| 2 | 25% | 1 : 3 |
| 3 | 12.5% | 1 : 7 |
| 4 | 6.25% | 1 : 15 |

Age = number of half-lives × half-life.

Limits: it works best on igneous rocks (dating crystallisation). Sedimentary rocks contain grains of older rocks, so they give the age of the source, not of deposition; they are dated indirectly by igneous rocks above and below them (bracketing), or by cross-cutting intrusions. Metamorphism can reset the clock.

#### Worked example

A mineral contains a parent : daughter ratio of 1 : 3. The half-life is 700 million years. 25% parent remains → 2 half-lives → age = 2 × 700 = 1400 million years (1.4 Ga).

Bracketing: a sandstone lies on top of a lava flow dated at 62 Ma and is itself cut by a dyke dated at 50 Ma. The sandstone must be younger than the lava below it (superposition) and older than the dyke that cuts it (cross-cutting), so it was deposited between 62 and 50 Ma.

#### Common mistakes

- Thinking all parent atoms decay after two half-lives.
- Giving a 1 : 1 ratio as two half-lives.
- Using radiometric dates of detrital grains as the age of a sandstone.

### 2.3e Deep Time

#### What you need to know

- The concept of Deep Time developed through Ussher (the Bible), Hutton, Kelvin, Joly and Holmes, extending the age of the Earth to about 4.6 billion years.

#### Explained

- James Ussher (1650): an archbishop who added up the generations and events in the Bible and concluded that the Earth was created in 4004 BCE, about 6000 years ago. Widely accepted at the time.
- James Hutton (1780s): observed slow processes (erosion, deposition) and unconformities such as Siccar Point, where vertical older rocks are overlain by younger horizontal beds. He reasoned that huge amounts of time were needed for repeated deposition, deformation, uplift and erosion: "no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end". He did not give a number, but showed the Earth was vastly older than thousands of years.
- Lord Kelvin (1862): assumed the Earth started molten and has been cooling by conduction; from the rate heat flows out today, calculated an age of 20 to 400 million years (later narrowed to about 20 to 40 Ma). He did not know about radioactive heating inside the Earth, which keeps it hot, so his age was far too young.
- John Joly (1899): estimated the age of the oceans from the rate rivers deliver salt to the sea and the total salt in the sea: about 90 to 100 million years. Too young, because salt is also removed (into evaporites) and recycled.
- Arthur Holmes (from 1911, and his 1913 book): used radioactive decay of uranium to lead to date rocks, finding ages of over a billion years, and argued for a much older Earth; he also supported continental drift through mantle convection.
- Today: radiometric dating of meteorites and the oldest Earth and Moon rocks gives an age of about 4.6 billion years (4600 Ma).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying Hutton calculated the age of the Earth. He showed it was immensely old without giving a figure.
- Saying Kelvin's method was wrong because of bad maths; the error was the missing heat source.

## 2.4 Global climate and sea level change

Rocks hold a record of the Earth's climate going back hundreds of millions of years. This topic covers evidence for icehouse and greenhouse periods, how Britain's climate changed as it drifted across latitudes, evidence for sea level change, sources of carbon dioxide, past carbon dioxide levels, feedback in the climate system, ice sheets and sea level, and carbon capture and storage.

### 2.4a Icehouse and greenhouse conditions

#### What you need to know

- Evidence for global climate change through geological time, from icehouse to greenhouse conditions: glacial deposits near the equator (Carboniferous tillites), limestone outside the tropics (Cretaceous limestones and chalk).

#### Explained

- Icehouse periods: large ice sheets exist at the poles (as now). Global temperatures are lower and sea level lower.
- Greenhouse periods: no permanent polar ice; warm climates extend to high latitudes; sea level is high.

Evidence: tillites (lithified glacial till: poorly sorted mixtures of clay to boulders, with scratched clasts) and scratched rock surfaces from the Late Carboniferous and Permian are found in South America, Africa, India and Australia, regions which were then close together near the South Pole in Gondwana. Some glacial deposits, such as those from the late Precambrian, are even found in rocks that formed near the equator, suggesting very cold global conditions. Thick Cretaceous chalk and limestone (which form in warm, shallow seas) occur well outside the tropics across northern Europe, including the white cliffs of southern England, showing warm seas reached high latitudes and sea level was very high: a greenhouse world.

Care is needed: plate movements change a region's latitude (2.4b), so evidence must be compared with where the continent was at the time.

#### Common mistakes

- Assuming tillite near today's equator means the whole Earth froze, without considering plate movement.

### 2.4b Britain's changing latitude and climate

#### What you need to know

- Evidence for changes in the climate of the British area caused by changes in its latitude.
- Interpret evidence from rocks, fossils, maps and diagrams for changes in latitude from the Lower Palaeozoic to the Cenozoic; use the data sheet.

#### Explained

Plate movements carried the British area northwards from the southern hemisphere to its present position at about 50 to 60° N.

| Time | Approximate latitude | Rocks and fossils | Climate and environment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lower Palaeozoic (Cambrian to Silurian) | About 30 to 60° S (southern Britain on a separate microcontinent from Scotland, across the Iapetus Ocean) | Deep water shales with graptolites, turbidites, volcanic rocks | Temperate to cool oceans |
| Devonian | About 10 to 20° S | Old Red Sandstone: red river and desert sandstones, fish fossils | Hot, semi-arid continental interior |
| Carboniferous | Equator | Reef limestones with corals; coal seams from tropical swamp forests | Tropical, warm and humid |
| Permian and Triassic | About 10 to 30° N | New Red Sandstone: desert dune sandstones, breccias, evaporites (halite, gypsum) | Hot desert, like today's Sahara |
| Jurassic | About 30 to 40° N | Shallow marine limestones, shales with ammonites | Warm shallow seas |
| Cretaceous | About 40 to 45° N | Chalk | Warm, very high sea level |
| Cenozoic | About 50 to 55° N | Clays and sands; later glacial deposits (Quaternary) | Cooling; ice ages in the last 2.6 million years |

Rocks such as coal (tropical swamps), evaporites and desert sandstones (latitudes of hot deserts around 15 to 30°) and reef limestones (tropical seas) give evidence of past latitude. Palaeomagnetism also records latitude.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the British climate changed only because the global climate changed. Changing latitude was a major cause.

### 2.4c Sea level change

#### What you need to know

- Evidence for changes in sea level, for example drowned forests.

#### Explained

- Drowned (submerged) forests: tree stumps rooted in peat are exposed at low tide on some British beaches (for example in Cardigan Bay and Cornwall). Trees grew on dry land; they are now below high tide, showing sea level has risen (after the last ice age, as ice sheets melted) or the land has sunk.
- Raised beaches: old wave-cut platforms and beach deposits above present sea level (as in western Scotland) show relative sea level has fallen, because the land rose after the weight of the ice sheets was removed (isostatic rebound).
- Rock evidence: marine sediments overlying land sediments (a transgression) show a sea level rise; the reverse (a regression) a fall. Chalk deposited across much of Europe shows very high Cretaceous sea level.
- Drowned river valleys (rias) and submerged landscapes under the North Sea (Doggerland) are other examples.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a raised beach shows sea level rise.

### 2.4d, 2.4e and 2.4f Carbon dioxide, past levels and feedback

#### What you need to know

- The major sources of atmospheric carbon dioxide are volcanic emissions and the burning of fossil fuels; evaluate their relative roles in current climate change.
- Evidence for past changes in atmospheric carbon dioxide from ice cores and sedimentary rocks.
- Positive feedback (reduction of ice-cap albedo accelerating warming) and negative feedback (carbon dioxide dissolving in sea water and being taken up by organisms to form limestone) on atmospheric carbon dioxide, with roles of subduction, volcanic emissions, chemical weathering and marine storage.

#### Explained

Sources: volcanoes release carbon dioxide from the Earth's interior, but at present the total is small: on average roughly 0.3 to 0.4 billion tonnes per year. Burning fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) releases about 35 billion tonnes per year, around a hundred times more. So human activity is by far the main cause of the current rapid rise in atmospheric carbon dioxide (from about 280 ppm before industrialisation to over 420 ppm). In the distant past, huge volcanic outpourings (flood basalts) released enough carbon dioxide to cause major warming.

Evidence of past carbon dioxide:

- Ice cores: snow falling on ice sheets traps tiny bubbles of air as it compacts into ice. Cores drilled from Antarctica and Greenland contain air going back about 800 000 years. The carbon dioxide in the bubbles is measured directly; it rises and falls with temperature through the ice ages (about 180 ppm in glacial periods, 280 ppm in warm interglacials).
- Sedimentary rocks: evidence over millions of years comes from rock types (widespread limestone, coal, evaporites), the chemistry of carbonates, fossil plant leaves (the density of breathing pores, stomata, falls when carbon dioxide is high), and fossil soils.

Feedback:

- Positive feedback amplifies change. Warming melts ice and snow, which are white and reflect much sunlight (high albedo). The exposed darker ocean or land absorbs more sunlight, causing more warming and more melting.
- Negative feedback reduces change. Carbon dioxide dissolves in sea water (more dissolves as levels rise); marine organisms use dissolved carbon to build calcium carbonate shells and skeletons, which become limestone, storing carbon for millions of years. Chemical weathering of rocks (faster in warm, wet climates) uses up atmospheric carbon dioxide and its products are carried to the sea and stored in carbonates. This slowly lowers carbon dioxide.

The long-term carbon cycle: carbon is stored in limestone and organic-rich sediments (marine storage); when plates subduct, some of this carbon is carried into the mantle and later returned to the atmosphere by volcanic emissions. This cycle, over millions of years, has kept the climate within limits suitable for life.

#### Worked example

If fossil fuels release 35 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide per year and volcanoes 0.35 billion tonnes, the ratio is 35 ÷ 0.35 = 100: human emissions are about 100 times volcanic emissions.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying volcanoes produce more carbon dioxide than human activity.
- Mixing up positive feedback (amplifies) with "good" feedback.

### 2.4g and 2.4h Ice sheets, sea level and carbon capture

#### What you need to know

- Global warming and cooling affect the size of continental ice sheets and global sea level; investigate past and current changes using the internet, maps and aerial images.
- Carbon sequestration (capture and storage) is a geological strategy for reducing atmospheric carbon dioxide.

#### Explained

Ice on land (Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets, glaciers) holds water taken from the oceans. When climate cools, ice sheets grow and sea level falls: at the peak of the last glaciation, about 20 000 years ago, ice covered much of Britain and sea level was about 120 m lower than today (Britain was joined to Europe). When climate warms, ice on land melts and sea level rises. Warming water also expands (thermal expansion). Melting floating sea ice does not raise sea level much, because it already displaces its own weight of water. Satellite images, aerial photographs and maps show glaciers retreating and ice sheets thinning; tide gauges and satellites measure present sea level rise (a few millimetres per year).

Carbon capture and storage (CCS): carbon dioxide is captured from power stations or industry (or directly from air), compressed and pumped deep underground into suitable rock:

- Porous, permeable reservoir rocks, such as depleted oil and gas fields or deep saline aquifers (sandstones), with an impermeable cap rock (shale, salt) above to stop it escaping, the same geology that trapped oil and gas (4.2).
- Basalts, where the carbon dioxide reacts with the rock to form solid carbonate minerals, locking it away permanently.

Challenges: cost, energy needed, risk of leaks, need for monitoring, and suitable sites.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying melting Arctic sea ice is the main cause of sea level rise.

## 2.5 The origin and development of life on Earth

The fossil record shows how life has changed over about 3.5 billion years. This topic covers where life probably began, the broad pattern of diversification, mass extinctions (especially the K/Pg event), and famous fossil finds that show exceptional preservation, evolutionary links, the challenges of reconstructing skeletons, and early human relatives, along with the limits of the fossil record.

### 2.5a The origin of life

#### What you need to know

- Life probably originated in the oceans or in hydrothermal pools about 3500 Ma (black smokers).

#### Explained

The oldest widely accepted evidence of life is about 3500 million years old (3.5 Ga): microscopic fossils of single cells, and stromatolites, layered mounds built by mats of bacteria trapping sediment in shallow water. Chemical signatures in even older rocks may point to life earlier.

Hydrothermal vents (black smokers) on the deep sea floor at mid-ocean ridges are a leading candidate for where life began. Hot, mineral-rich water (heated by magma) gushes out and precipitates black metal sulfides. They provide energy (chemical energy from reactions between the vent fluids and sea water), a supply of elements, and protection from harmful ultraviolet radiation at the surface. Today they support communities of bacteria and animals that do not rely on sunlight. An alternative is warm, shallow hydrothermal pools on land, where repeated wetting and drying could concentrate the chemicals needed.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the first life was on land.

### 2.5b The diversification of life

#### What you need to know

- The fossil record shows increasing diversity: single cells, multicellular organisms, animals with hard parts, fish, amphibians, reptiles, mammals, birds and humans.
- Use simple evolutionary tree diagrams (cladograms) to show trends; use the data sheet.

#### Explained

Approximate order (Ma = millions of years ago):

| First appearance | Approximate time |
| --- | --- |
| Single-celled life (bacteria) | About 3500 Ma |
| Complex single cells with a nucleus | About 2000 Ma |
| Multicellular organisms (soft-bodied, e.g. Ediacaran fauna) | About 600 Ma |
| Animals with hard parts (shells, skeletons): "Cambrian explosion" | About 540 Ma |
| Fish | About 500 Ma (Ordovician) |
| Land plants | About 470 to 430 Ma |
| Amphibians | About 370 Ma (Devonian) |
| Reptiles | About 310 Ma (Carboniferous) |
| Mammals | About 200 Ma (Triassic–Jurassic) |
| Birds | About 150 Ma (Jurassic) |
| Humans (Homo sapiens) | About 0.3 Ma |

Hard parts (shells, bones, teeth) are far more easily fossilised than soft tissue, so the fossil record becomes much richer from the Cambrian onwards. Life moved from sea to land as plants, then invertebrates, then vertebrates (amphibians) colonised it.

A cladogram is a branching diagram showing how groups are related: groups that share a more recent common ancestor are joined by a more recent branch point. For example, birds branch from within the reptile line (closest to dinosaurs), and mammals branch from an earlier reptile-like ancestor. Reading up the tree shows the order in which features (such as backbone, four limbs, eggs with a shell, feathers) appeared.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying humans lived with the dinosaurs.
- Reading a cladogram as a ladder, with modern groups "evolving into" each other.

### 2.5c Mass extinctions

#### What you need to know

- Life's development was interrupted by major extinction events, for example the Cretaceous/Palaeogene (K/Pg) mass extinction.

#### Explained

A mass extinction is when a large proportion of species dies out in a geologically short time. There have been five major mass extinctions; the largest was at the end of the Permian (about 252 Ma), probably linked to huge volcanic eruptions in Siberia.

The K/Pg (Cretaceous/Palaeogene) extinction, 66 Ma, wiped out about three-quarters of species, including the dinosaurs (except birds), ammonites and many marine reptiles. Evidence for a meteorite impact as the main cause:

- A thin clay layer at the K/Pg boundary worldwide, enriched in iridium, an element rare in the Earth's crust but common in meteorites.
- Shocked quartz (with special deformation features formed only at extreme pressures) and tiny glass spherules in the boundary layer.
- The Chicxulub crater, about 180 km across, buried under the Yucatán Peninsula, Mexico, dated to 66 Ma.
- Tsunami deposits around the Gulf of Mexico.

The impact threw dust and sulfur aerosols into the atmosphere, blocking sunlight, collapsing food chains and causing a sudden cold period, followed by warming. Massive volcanic eruptions in India (the Deccan Traps) at about the same time may also have contributed.

After a mass extinction, surviving groups diversify into empty niches: mammals diversified rapidly after the K/Pg extinction.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying only the dinosaurs died out at the K/Pg boundary.

### 2.5d Major fossil finds and the incomplete fossil record

#### What you need to know

- Major finds show: rare and exceptional preservation (Burgess Shale fauna); links between groups seen in morphology of modern reptiles and birds (Archaeopteryx); the need to interpret complex skeletons from incomplete, disarticulated remains (dinosaurs); features of early hominids ("Lucy").
- Evaluate the significance of the incompleteness of the fossil record.

#### Explained

- Burgess Shale (Canada, about 508 Ma, Middle Cambrian): fine mud slumped rapidly onto animals and buried them in oxygen-poor conditions, preserving soft bodies (legs, guts, gills) as thin films. It shows the great variety of strange animals that appeared in the Cambrian, many with no close living relatives. This exceptional preservation is very rare and shows how much is normally lost.
- Archaeopteryx (Germany, about 150 Ma, Late Jurassic, in fine Solnhofen limestone): has feathers and wings like a bird, but also reptile (dinosaur) features: teeth in its jaws, claws on its wings, and a long bony tail. It is a transitional fossil linking reptiles (theropod dinosaurs) and birds.
- Dinosaurs: skeletons are usually found incomplete and the bones scattered (disarticulated) by scavengers, currents or decay. Palaeontologists reconstruct them by comparing bones with other specimens and with living animals (birds and reptiles), using uniformitarianism. Reconstructions have changed over time (for example, Iguanodon's thumb spike was first placed on its nose), showing interpretation can be revised as more complete finds appear.
- "Lucy" (Australopithecus afarensis, Ethiopia, about 3.2 Ma): about 40% of a skeleton of an early hominin. Her pelvis and leg bones show she walked upright on two legs, but her brain was small, about the size of a chimpanzee's, and her arms long. This showed upright walking evolved before large brains.

The incomplete fossil record: only a tiny fraction of organisms become fossils. Fossilisation needs rapid burial, hard parts, and the right conditions; most remains are eaten, decay or are destroyed. Many fossils are then lost by erosion, metamorphism or subduction, or are not exposed or found. Soft-bodied organisms and land animals in eroding areas are especially under-represented. So gaps in the record (missing transitional forms) are expected, the first and last appearances of species are approximate, and diversity in the past is underestimated. Exceptional sites (Lagerstätten) such as the Burgess Shale give rare, detailed windows.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling Archaeopteryx a modern bird.
- Saying gaps in the fossil record prove evolution did not happen. Gaps are expected from how fossils form.

## 3.1 Planetary geology

Comparing the Earth with the Moon, Mars and other bodies helps explain both. This topic covers similarities and differences between the Earth and its neighbours, what meteorites tell us about the Earth's interior, using Earth landforms to interpret landforms elsewhere (uniformitarianism), how other bodies reveal processes hidden on Earth, and the effect of impacts on Earth's history. For astronomy background, see [Astronomy 09 Exploring the Moon](../astronomy-edexcel/09-exploring-the-moon.md) and [Astronomy 11 Exploring the Solar System](../astronomy-edexcel/11-exploring-the-solar-system.md).

### 3.1a Earth and its planetary neighbours

#### What you need to know

- Similarities and differences between the Earth and its planetary neighbours: rocks, landscapes, atmosphere, temperature, pressure and gravity.

#### Explained

| | Earth | Moon | Mars | Venus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rocks | Igneous, sedimentary, metamorphic; granite continents, basalt ocean floor | Basalt (maria), anorthosite (highlands), impact breccias; no sedimentary rocks formed by water | Basalt crust; sedimentary rocks deposited by water (sandstones, mudstones, evaporites) | Mostly basalt |
| Landscape | Mountains, ocean basins, rivers, few craters (erased by erosion and plate tectonics) | Heavily cratered highlands, smooth maria | Giant volcanoes (Olympus Mons, about 22 km high), huge canyon (Valles Marineris), dry river channels and deltas, craters, polar ice caps, dunes | Volcanic plains, volcanoes, few craters (resurfaced fairly recently) |
| Atmosphere | Nitrogen and oxygen, moderate pressure (1 bar) | Virtually none | Thin carbon dioxide, about 1% of Earth's pressure | Thick carbon dioxide, about 90 times Earth's pressure, sulfuric acid clouds |
| Temperature | Mean about 15 °C; liquid water | −170 °C to +120 °C (no air to even out) | Mean about −60 °C | About 460 °C (greenhouse effect) |
| Gravity | 1 g | About 0.17 g | About 0.38 g | About 0.9 g |
| Plate tectonics | Active | None | None now (single-plate "stagnant lid") | No Earth-style plates |

The Earth is unique in the Solar System in having plate tectonics and liquid water oceans at the surface today, which constantly renew its surface. Smaller bodies (Moon, Mars) lost their internal heat faster, so their geological activity largely stopped, preserving ancient surfaces. Lower gravity on Mars allows taller volcanoes.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying Mars has never had liquid water. Its surface shows clear evidence of past water.

### 3.1b Meteorites and the Earth's composition

#### What you need to know

- Meteorites provide evidence for the composition of the Earth.

#### Explained

We cannot sample the Earth's core or deep mantle directly. Most meteorites come from asteroids, which formed at the same time and from the same material as the Earth (about 4.6 billion years ago):

- Chondrites (stony meteorites from undifferentiated asteroids) have a composition thought to be like the average starting material of the Earth (apart from volatile elements). Comparing the Earth's crust with chondrites suggests heavy elements such as iron and nickel sank into the core.
- Iron meteorites (iron-nickel alloy) are thought to be fragments of the cores of asteroids that melted and separated into core and mantle and were later broken up by collisions. They are good models for the Earth's core.
- Stony-iron meteorites resemble the core-mantle boundary; achondrites (some stony meteorites) resemble mantle or crust.

This supports the idea that the Earth has an iron-nickel core and a silicate mantle. Meteorites also give the age of the Solar System, and so of the Earth: about 4.6 billion years (2.3).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying meteorites come from the Earth's core.

### 3.1c and 3.1d Using landforms to interpret other worlds and the Earth

#### What you need to know

- The relationship between landforms and processes on Earth is an analogue for interpreting landforms on other planetary bodies (uniformitarianism).
- Planetary landforms provide evidence for unseen Earth processes, for example impact craters on the Moon.
- Use space imagery and planetary exploration data (maps, diagrams, photographs) to interpret landforms and processes, for example on the Moon and Mars.

#### Explained

Earth as a key to other planets: if a landform on Mars looks like a landform on Earth whose origin we understand, it probably formed by a similar process.

| Landform on Mars or the Moon | Earth analogue | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Branching channels and fan-shaped deltas on Mars | River valleys and deltas | Water once flowed across Mars |
| Layered sedimentary rocks with cross bedding (photographed by rovers such as Curiosity) | Sedimentary sequences | Deposition in rivers and lakes or by wind |
| Desiccation cracks and evaporite minerals on Mars | Drying lake beds | Lakes that evaporated |
| Olympus Mons, a broad gently sloping volcano | Hawaiian shield volcanoes | Low-viscosity basaltic lava, over a hotspot that stayed in one place (no plate movement) |
| Sand dunes on Mars | Desert dunes | Wind transport |
| Sinuous channels (rilles) on the Moon | Lava channels and collapsed lava tubes in Hawaii | Flowing lava |
| Lunar maria | Flood basalts on Earth | Large outpourings of runny basaltic lava |

Other bodies as a key to Earth: some processes that shaped the early Earth have left little trace here, because erosion, weathering, sedimentation and plate tectonics have destroyed or buried the evidence. The Moon's heavily cratered surface preserves a record of the intense bombardment of the inner Solar System about 4 billion years ago: the Earth must have been struck at least as often, but almost all those early craters on Earth have been erased. Only about 200 impact craters are recognised on Earth, mostly young. So the Moon shows how important impacts were in the Earth's early history.

#### Common mistakes

- Using an analogue without explaining the matching process.

### 3.1e Impacts and the evolution of the Earth and its biosphere

#### What you need to know

- Impacts from meteorites and comets may have had a significant effect on the evolution of the Earth and its biosphere.

#### Explained

- Formation of the Moon: a giant impact of a Mars-sized body with the early Earth probably formed the Moon (see Astronomy 09), and the impact energy melted the Earth's outer layers.
- Early bombardment: frequent large impacts in the first few hundred million years heated and repeatedly disrupted the surface, which may have delayed the start of life or sterilised early habitats.
- Delivery of water and organic molecules: some of the Earth's water and carbon compounds may have come from impacting asteroids and comets.
- Mass extinctions: the Chicxulub impact at 66 Ma caused the K/Pg mass extinction (2.5), ending the age of dinosaurs and allowing mammals to diversify, so it changed the course of evolution.
- Future hazard: near-Earth objects are tracked, and missions such as DART (2022) have tested whether an asteroid's path could be changed.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying impacts have had no effect since the early Solar System.

## 4.1 Earth hazards and their mitigation

Geological events become hazards when they threaten people and property. This topic covers earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, landslides and tsunamis, why the same event causes very different losses in different places, why prediction is limited, and how risk can be reduced.

### 4.1a Geological hazards

#### What you need to know

- Earthquakes (shaking, triggering landslides), volcanic eruptions (lava, ash, pyroclastic flows, mudflows), landslides (and related subsidence) and tsunamis can be hazardous.
- Investigate and interpret data on the distribution, measurement and causes of these events.

#### Explained

Earthquakes: sudden movement on a fault releases stored strain energy as seismic waves, which radiate from the focus (the point underground where the rupture starts); the epicentre is the point on the surface directly above. Most occur at plate boundaries (2.2). Hazards: ground shaking (collapsing buildings cause most deaths), ground rupture, liquefaction (shaken, waterlogged sediment behaves like a liquid and buildings sink or topple), fires from broken gas pipes, landslides triggered on slopes, and tsunamis.

Measurement: seismometers record ground motion. Magnitude measures the energy released (moment magnitude scale; each whole number step is about 32 times more energy and 10 times larger ground motion on a seismogram). Intensity (Modified Mercalli scale, I to XII) describes the effects at a place, which depend on distance, geology and building quality. The epicentre is located using the difference in arrival times of P-waves (faster) and S-waves at three or more stations.

Volcanic eruptions:

- Lava flows: slow-moving at most volcanoes, so people can usually escape, but they destroy property.
- Ash (tephra) falls: collapse roofs under its weight, ruin crops, contaminate water, cause breathing problems, and ground aircraft.
- Pyroclastic flows: avalanches of hot gas, ash and rock moving down the slopes at over 100 km/h and several hundred °C: the deadliest volcanic hazard (Mount Pelée 1902; Vesuvius AD 79).
- Mudflows (lahars): volcanic ash mixed with water (from rain, melted snow or a crater lake) flows down valleys like wet concrete, burying towns far from the volcano (Nevado del Ruiz 1985).
- Also volcanic gases (carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide).

Explosive (andesitic) eruptions at subduction zones are generally far more dangerous than effusive basaltic ones.

Landslides: masses of rock or soil move downslope under gravity when slopes become unstable: steep slopes; rocks dipping out of the slope; weak rocks such as clay; joints and faults; heavy rain raising water pressure in pores; removal of support at the base (river or wave erosion, road cuttings); earthquake shaking; removing vegetation. Subsidence (ground sinking) can result from removal of material below, for example dissolution of limestone or salt, collapse of old mines, or compaction of drained peat and clay.

Tsunamis: series of very long-wavelength sea waves caused by sudden displacement of a large volume of water, usually by vertical movement of the sea floor in a large subduction-zone earthquake (also by submarine landslides, volcanic collapse or impacts). In deep ocean they travel at hundreds of km/h with small height; near the coast they slow and grow tall, flooding low-lying land. The sea may first retreat. Examples: Indian Ocean 2004 (Sumatra), Japan 2011.

#### Common mistakes

- Confusing focus and epicentre.
- Confusing pyroclastic flows with lava flows.
- Saying tsunamis are caused by wind.

### 4.1b The level of risk

#### What you need to know

- The level of risk to life and property depends on population density, technology (buildings) and development (economic situation, education, communication).
- Contrast risks from natural hazards in areas of contrasting development (LEDC and MEDC).

#### Explained

Risk depends on the hazard (its size and how often it happens) and on vulnerability (how many people and how much property are exposed and how well they can cope).

- Population density: more people in the affected area means more potential casualties. Rapidly growing cities in hazard zones increase risk.
- Technology: earthquake-resistant buildings, monitoring networks and warning systems reduce deaths.
- Development: wealthier countries (MEDCs, more economically developed countries) can afford building regulations enforced by inspection, monitoring, emergency services, insurance and rapid rescue; education and drills mean people know what to do; good communications spread warnings. Poorer countries (LEDCs, less economically developed countries) often have poorly built, crowded housing, little monitoring, weaker emergency services and slower recovery.

Typical pattern: MEDCs suffer greater financial losses (more expensive property), while LEDCs suffer much greater loss of life. For example, earthquakes of similar magnitude have killed hundreds of thousands in poorly built cities (Haiti 2010, magnitude 7.0) but far fewer where buildings are designed to resist shaking (Chile 2010, magnitude 8.8, a much larger event, with about 500 deaths).

#### Common mistakes

- Comparing events without considering magnitude, depth and distance from cities.

### 4.1c and 4.1d Prediction and reducing risk

#### What you need to know

- The accuracy of hazard prediction is limited.
- Risk can be reduced by building design and regulation; prediction using hazard interval patterns (seismic gaps), ground deformation (tiltmeters), groundwater changes and gas emissions; warning schemes and evacuation.

#### Explained

Prediction means stating where, when and how big an event will be. Geologists can say where hazards are likely (plate boundaries, active volcanoes, unstable slopes) and estimate probabilities over decades (forecasting), but cannot reliably predict the exact time of an earthquake. Volcanic eruptions are easier to forecast because magma rising gives warning signs, but timing and size are still uncertain, and false alarms reduce public trust.

Methods:

- Hazard interval patterns: studying the dates of past events gives an average recurrence interval. Seismic gaps are sections of an active fault that have not ruptured for a long time while neighbouring sections have; stress is building up there, so a large earthquake is more likely.
- Ground deformation: rising magma swells a volcano; tiltmeters measure tiny changes in slope, and GPS and satellite radar measure uplift. Strain gauges and GPS also measure strain build-up across faults.
- Groundwater changes: changes in well water levels, temperature or chemistry (such as radon gas) may occur before earthquakes or eruptions as rocks crack or are heated.
- Gas emissions: increasing sulfur dioxide or carbon dioxide from vents can show magma rising.
- Also: swarms of small earthquakes beneath volcanoes as magma forces its way up.

Reducing risk:

- Building design and regulation: earthquake-resistant buildings with deep foundations, flexible steel frames, cross-bracing, base isolators (rubber pads) and dampers; avoid heavy roofs; strict building codes enforced by inspection; land-use planning keeping housing off fault lines, liquefaction-prone ground, lahar valleys and unstable slopes; slope stabilisation (drainage, rock bolts, retaining walls, nets); sea walls and tsunami refuges.
- Warning schemes: tsunami warning systems (seismometers and ocean-floor pressure sensors linked to alerts); volcano alert levels; earthquake early warning (detects the fast P-waves and gives seconds of warning before strong shaking arrives to stop trains and close gas valves); public education and drills.
- Evacuation: planned routes and shelters; moving people away from volcanoes before eruptions and to high ground before tsunamis.

#### Common mistakes

- Claiming earthquakes can be predicted precisely.
- Saying building design prevents earthquakes. It reduces their effects.

7. Explain what a seismic gap is and why it is used in hazard assessment. [2 marks]
8. Describe two methods used to forecast a volcanic eruption. [4 marks]

### Mark scheme

## 4.2 Earth resources and engineering

Geology underpins the materials, energy and water we use and the safety of what we build. This topic covers resources and reserves, three named mineral resources, prospecting methods, oil and gas traps and extraction, groundwater and aquifers, waste disposal, and geological factors in siting engineering projects.

### 4.2a Resources and reserves

#### What you need to know

- Resources are naturally occurring useful substances; reserves are the calculated amount of a resource that is economic to extract.

#### Explained

A resource includes all of a substance that exists, known or estimated, whether or not it can be extracted profitably now. A reserve is the part of a resource that has been located and measured and can be extracted at a profit with current technology and prices. Reserves change: if the price rises or new technology lowers costs, more of the resource becomes reserves; if the price falls, some reserves become uneconomic. Economic factors include the grade (concentration) of the ore, depth, size of deposit, accessibility and transport, extraction cost, market price and environmental restrictions.

#### Common mistakes

- Using "resource" and "reserve" as the same thing.

### 4.2b Mineral resources: limestone, haematite, uranium

#### What you need to know

- Mineral resources are important in construction, manufacturing and energy generation; investigate uses of limestone (aggregate in construction), haematite (steel industry) and uranium (energy generation).

#### Explained

- Limestone: quarried in large amounts and crushed for aggregate (crushed rock used in concrete, road foundations and railway ballast); also used to make cement (heated with clay), lime for agriculture and steel-making flux, and as building stone. Quarries are large and close to markets because aggregate is heavy and cheap, so transport costs matter.
- Haematite (iron oxide, Fe₂O₃): the main ore of iron. In a blast furnace, it is reduced by carbon to iron, which is made into steel for construction, vehicles and machinery. Forms in banded iron formations, hydrothermal veins and sedimentary deposits.
- Uranium: found as minerals such as uraninite (pitchblende), in granites, hydrothermal veins and some sandstones. Mined, concentrated and enriched to fuel nuclear power stations, where fission releases large amounts of energy with very low carbon dioxide emissions, but produces long-lived radioactive waste (4.2g).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying aggregate is a mineral. It is crushed rock used in construction.

### 4.2c Prospecting

#### What you need to know

- Prospecting techniques: geological mapping; borehole correlation (using microfossils); geophysical methods (seismic, magnetic, ground-penetrating radar); geochemical methods (soil and river sediment analysis).
- Interpret prospecting data to identify possible valuable mineral resources.

#### Explained

- Geological mapping: recording rock types, structures and mineralised veins at the surface; maps show where favourable rocks and structures (such as faults that may carry hydrothermal veins, or granite contacts) occur.
- Borehole correlation: drilling boreholes and matching layers between them by rock type and microfossils (tiny fossils that survive in drill cuttings, used as zone fossils, 2.3) to map the depth and thickness of layers underground, such as a coal seam or reservoir rock.
- Geophysical methods (measuring physical properties from the surface, aircraft or ships):
  - Seismic surveys: sound waves from small explosions or vibrating trucks reflect off rock boundaries; the echoes reveal underground structures such as anticlines and faults (vital for oil and gas).
  - Magnetic surveys: magnetometers detect anomalies (unusually strong readings) from magnetic minerals such as magnetite, or iron-rich igneous rocks.
  - Ground-penetrating radar: radar pulses reflect from shallow features (buried cavities, water table, sediment layers), useful for shallow engineering and archaeology.
  - (Gravity surveys detect dense ore bodies.)
- Geochemical methods: samples of soil or river (stream) sediment are analysed for metal concentrations. Unusually high values (an anomaly) suggest a deposit nearby. In streams, follow the anomaly upstream until values drop; the source lies between the last high and first low sample.

#### Worked example

Stream sediment copper values (ppm) going upstream: 40, 45, 300, 320, 310, 50, 40. Background is about 40 to 50 ppm. High values start at the third sample and stop after the fifth, so the source of the copper is probably between the fifth and sixth sample sites (or in a tributary entering there).

#### Common mistakes

- Looking for the source downstream of the anomaly. Sediment travels downstream, so the source is upstream.

### 4.2d and 4.2e Oil and gas

#### What you need to know

- Structures and rock properties linked to the migration and accumulation of oil and gas onshore and offshore: source rock; contrasting porosity and permeability of reservoir and cap rocks; main traps (anticline, fault, unconformity, salt dome).
- Interpret maps, cross-sections and seismic surveys to identify possible oil and gas fields.
- Technological difficulties and environmental issues in exploring for and extracting oil and gas, including fracking.

#### Explained

Formation: organic-rich mud (from plankton) accumulates in oxygen-poor water and becomes a source rock (black shale). Buried to depths with suitable temperatures (about 60 to 150 °C, the "oil window"), the organic matter is slowly converted to oil and then gas. Being less dense than water, oil and gas migrate upwards through permeable rocks.

Requirements for an oil field:

1. Source rock: organic-rich shale.
2. Reservoir rock: porous (to store oil and gas) and permeable (so they can flow to a well), e.g. sandstone or fractured limestone.
3. Cap (seal) rock: impermeable rock above the reservoir that stops further upward movement, e.g. shale, clay or salt.
4. Trap: a structure that collects oil and gas in one place.

Traps:

- Anticline trap: reservoir and cap rock folded into an arch; oil and gas collect at the crest. Gas on top, then oil, then water (by density).
- Fault trap: a fault moves the reservoir rock against impermeable rock, sealing it.
- Unconformity trap: tilted reservoir beds are cut off by an unconformity, and impermeable beds above seal them.
- Salt dome trap: a column of salt rises through the overlying rocks (salt is low density and flows), bending them upward; reservoir beds are sealed against the impermeable salt.

On seismic sections and cross-sections, look for a porous layer beneath an impermeable layer, arched or sealed by a fault, salt or unconformity, above a source rock.

Difficulties and issues: offshore drilling in deep water and stormy seas (North Sea) needs expensive platforms; drilling to great depths at high temperature and pressure; risk of blowouts, spills (damaging marine life and coasts) and gas flaring; pipelines and tankers can leak; burning oil and gas releases carbon dioxide (2.4). Fracking (hydraulic fracturing): water, sand and chemicals are pumped at high pressure into low-permeability shale to crack it and release gas. Concerns include triggering small earthquakes (as near Blackpool, UK, in 2011 and 2019), possible contamination of groundwater by fluids or methane, large water use, disposal of waste water, traffic and noise; supporters cite energy security.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying oil forms in the reservoir rock. It forms in the source rock and migrates.
- Saying the cap rock must be porous. It must be impermeable.

### 4.2f Groundwater and aquifers

#### What you need to know

- Factors affecting extraction of groundwater from aquifers: height of the water table, porosity and permeability of the aquifer, presence of natural springs, distribution of wells.
- Analyse rock types for their suitability as an aquifer.

#### Explained

An aquifer is a body of porous and permeable rock that stores and transmits water (for example chalk, sandstone and some limestones in Britain). Rocks such as clay, shale and unfractured granite are aquicludes (impermeable). Groundwater fills the pores below the water table; above it, pores contain air and water (unsaturated zone).

- Water table height: wells must reach below the water table, preferably well below the lowest level it falls to in dry seasons. The water table rises in wet periods (recharge) and falls with drought and pumping.
- Porosity and permeability: high porosity stores lots of water; high permeability lets it flow quickly to wells.
- Springs: where the water table meets the surface, or where water flowing through an aquifer meets an impermeable layer below, water flows out naturally, a useful supply.
- Distribution of wells: pumping lowers the water table around a well in a cone of depression. Wells too close together interfere; over-pumping lowers the water table, dries up springs and wells, and in coastal areas can draw salt water into the aquifer.
- Confined aquifers sandwiched between impermeable layers may be under pressure (artesian), so water rises up the well on its own.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying an aquifer is an underground lake. Water fills pores and cracks in rock.

### 4.2g Waste disposal and aquifers

#### What you need to know

- The impact of domestic and hazardous waste disposal on vulnerable aquifers depends on geological factors (permeability), engineering factors (geomembranes), monitoring of potentially polluted water, and restoration of contaminated ground.
- Investigate the suitability of sites for landfill of domestic waste and for long-term storage of hazardous waste.

#### Explained

Landfill waste produces leachate (polluted liquid from rainwater percolating through waste) and landfill gas (methane). If leachate reaches an aquifer, drinking water supplies can be contaminated.

- Geological factors: an ideal landfill site is on impermeable rock or clay (to contain leachate), away from aquifers, faults and joints (pathways for pollutants), with a deep water table, stable ground and not on a floodplain.
- Engineering factors: sites are lined with compacted clay and synthetic geomembranes (plastic liners); leachate is collected by drains and treated; gas is collected and burned or used for energy; the site is capped when full.
- Monitoring: boreholes around the site are sampled regularly to check groundwater quality and detect leaks early.
- Restoration: contaminated land and groundwater can be cleaned by removing polluted soil, pumping and treating water, or using natural processes; closed landfills are capped and landscaped.

Hazardous waste, including high-level radioactive waste, needs storage for many thousands of years. A geological disposal facility should be deep underground (hundreds of metres) in stable, low-permeability rock (clay, salt, or unfractured granite), in a tectonically stable area away from faults, earthquakes and volcanoes, with slow groundwater flow and no valuable resources that people might drill for in future, plus engineered barriers (canisters, clay buffer).

#### Common mistakes

- Choosing permeable sandstone or chalk as a landfill site.

### 4.2h Siting engineering projects

#### What you need to know

- Geological factors affect the siting of reservoirs, dams, tunnels and cuttings: permeability, stability of bedrock, dip of strata, presence of faults and joints.
- Use descriptions, diagrams, photographs, maps and cross-sections to investigate geological factors.

#### Explained

| Project | Good geology | Problems to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reservoir | Impermeable rocks under the reservoir floor and sides (so water does not leak away); stable valley sides | Permeable or soluble rock (limestone with caves, sandstone); faults that could leak; landslide-prone slopes that could slide into the water and cause a wave |
| Dam | Strong, impermeable, unweathered bedrock for foundations; narrow valley | Faults (could move and leak); weak or weathered rock; beds dipping downstream (could slide); joints that let water pass under the dam |
| Tunnel | Strong, uniform, impermeable rock that needs little support; few faults | Faults and shattered rock (collapse); water-bearing permeable layers (flooding); changes in rock type (cutting difficulties); swelling clays |
| Cutting (road or rail) | Beds dipping into the slope or horizontal; strong rock | Beds or joints dipping towards the road (rock slides); clay that slumps when wet; faults |

Site investigation uses geological mapping, boreholes, geophysical surveys, and data from the British Geological Survey and Environment Agency (often in a GIS, 14).

#### Common mistakes

- Siting a reservoir on cavernous limestone.
- Ignoring the dip direction relative to a cutting face.

### Check questions

1. Explain the difference between a resource and a reserve, and how a reserve can increase without new discoveries. [3 marks]
2. Give one use of each of limestone, haematite and uranium. [3 marks]
3. Explain how stream sediment sampling can locate a metal ore deposit. [3 marks]
4. Name two geophysical methods and state what each detects. [4 marks]
5. Describe the conditions needed for an oil and gas field to form. [4 marks]
6. Draw and label an anticline trap. [3 marks]
7. Give two environmental concerns about fracking. [2 marks]
8. Explain why chalk is a good aquifer but clay is not. [3 marks]
9. Explain why too many closely spaced wells can cause problems for groundwater supply. [2 marks]
10. Describe the ideal geology for a landfill site for domestic waste and one engineering measure that protects groundwater. [4 marks]
11. Explain two geological factors that would make a site unsuitable for a dam. [4 marks]

### Mark scheme

1. Resource: total naturally occurring amount of a useful substance (1). Reserve: the amount economic to extract with current technology / prices (1). A price rise / better technology makes more of the resource economic (1).
2. Limestone: aggregate / cement / building stone (1). Haematite: iron ore for steel (1). Uranium: nuclear fuel for electricity generation (1).

## Fieldwork, geological maps and practical skills

Every Eduqas GCSE Geology student must spend at least two days in the field, including at least one directed investigation to answer a geological problem. Both exams test practical and field skills (at least 15% of the marks) and maths (at least 10%), and Component 2 is built entirely around a simplified geological map. This file covers the fieldwork requirement, locating and measuring in the field, recording observations, geological maps and cross-sections, geological histories, laboratory tests and data handling, and the main maths skills.

### Fieldwork and directed investigations

#### What you need to know

- At least two days of fieldwork, with at least one directed investigation; follow the Geological Code of Conduct and safety rules.
- Example investigations: determining the environments of deposition of a sedimentary sequence; determining the effects of tectonic stress at a location; determining the geological history of an area from field observations.

#### Explained

A directed investigation follows a scientific method: a geological question (for example "In what environment did these beds form?"); a plan of what to observe and measure and where; collection of data (logs, measurements, sketches, photographs); analysis; and a conclusion supported by evidence, with an evaluation of its reliability (how many exposures, how representative, sources of error).

Safety and conduct (see also 1.5a): hard hats near faces, goggles for hammering, appropriate clothing and footwear, awareness of tides and weather, group working and communication, landowner permission, minimal hammering and collecting, leaving gates and sites as found.

#### Common mistakes

- Drawing conclusions from one exposure without considering whether it is representative.

### Locating and measuring in the field

#### What you need to know

- Locate geological features on a base map using navigation, basic survey skills and GPS; give six-figure grid references.
- Identify folds, faults and sedimentary structures in the field.
- Measure dip and strike (dip angle, dip direction, strike direction) of bedding planes or faults with a compass clinometer.
- Use sampling along a transect for data collection, for example to build a graphic log.

#### Explained

Six-figure grid references: on an Ordnance Survey map, read the easting first (along the bottom: the vertical grid line to the left of the point, plus tenths across the square), then the northing (up the side: the horizontal line below the point, plus tenths up). For example 345 672 is 5 tenths east of line 34 and 2 tenths north of line 67, locating a point to within 100 m. GPS receivers give grid references directly but may be inaccurate under cliffs or in valleys.

Dip and strike measurement is described in 1.5a. Take several readings on each surface and average them, because bedding surfaces are uneven.

Sampling along a transect: measure the thickness of each bed in a vertical sequence (using a tape and, for tilted beds, correcting for dip, or measuring perpendicular to bedding), and record grain size, structures and fossils at set intervals or in each bed, to construct a graphic log (1.3e).

#### Common mistakes

- Giving the northing before the easting.

### Recording observations

#### What you need to know

- Record observations of rocks, minerals, fossils and structures in the field, in hand specimens, in photomicrographs and from images (including images from Mars).
- Produce full rock descriptions of macro and micro features from hand specimens and unfamiliar field exposures.
- Produce annotated field sketches and annotated drawings of fossils and igneous rocks.
- Apply classification systems and keys.

#### Explained

A full rock description, in a logical order:

1. Colour (fresh and weathered surfaces).
2. Texture: crystalline (interlocking) or fragmental (grains and cement); grain or crystal size (use a grain size scale or comparator; coarse, medium, fine); shape (angular to rounded) and sorting; alignment (foliation) or random orientation.
3. Mineral composition (identified with a hand lens and simple tests: hardness, acid, cleavage, lustre).
4. Structures (bedding, cross bedding, graded bedding, vesicles, foliation) and fossils.
5. Reaction with dilute hydrochloric acid.
6. Name and rock group, with reasons.

Annotated field sketch: a simple line drawing (not artistic) of the exposure showing beds, folds, faults, intrusions and unconformities; include a title, location (grid reference), orientation (compass direction faced), scale (approximate height or a hammer for scale), and labels with notes (annotations) explaining features and interpretations. Annotated drawings of fossils or rocks similarly include scale and labelled diagnostic features.

Photomicrographs are photos of thin slices of rock seen through a microscope; they show crystal shapes, sizes, textures and grain relationships, for example interlocking crystals in granite, rounded grains with cement in sandstone, aligned mica in schist.

#### Common mistakes

- Forgetting scale and orientation on a sketch.
- Describing colour only, without texture and minerals.

### Geological maps, cross-sections and histories

#### What you need to know

- Analyse simplified geological maps and cross-sections (Component 2 is based on one).
- Complete a partly constructed geological map from a written description.
- Construct a geological history of a small area in the field and of an area shown on a map.

#### Explained

A geological map shows the rock outcrops at the surface (where each rock unit would be seen if soil were removed), coloured or patterned by rock type and age, with a key (usually oldest at the bottom), plus symbols: dip and strike, fault lines, fold axes, and boundaries (geological contacts). It is drawn on a topographic base with contours.

Reading the map:

- Horizontal beds: boundaries follow contour lines.
- Vertical beds and dykes: straight outcrops, unaffected by topography.
- Dipping beds: boundaries cross contours; in a valley, the outcrop "V" points in the direction of dip (unless the beds dip less steeply than the valley floor).
- Width of outcrop depends on bed thickness, dip and slope: thicker beds and gentler dips give wider outcrops.
- Folds: outcrops repeated symmetrically; oldest in the centre of an eroded anticline, youngest in a syncline (1.5b).
- Faults: outcrops end abruptly or are offset across a line (1.5c).
- Unconformity: younger beds cut across older boundaries and structures (1.5d).
- Intrusions: dykes as narrow bands crossing other outcrops; plutons as large outcrops surrounded by metamorphic aureoles (1.2e, 1.4c).

Cross-sections show the vertical slice along a line on the map: draw the topographic profile from the contours, mark where boundaries cross the line, and draw beds going down at the dip shown (on a simplified section, using the dip symbols).

Geological history: list events from oldest to youngest using the principles in 2.3: deposition of each unit, folding or tilting, intrusions and metamorphism, faulting, uplift and erosion, deposition above unconformities, and present-day erosion. Interpret each sedimentary unit's environment from its rock type and fossils (1.3).

#### Worked example

A map key lists: shale (oldest), sandstone, limestone, conglomerate (youngest), and a dolerite dyke. The shale, sandstone and limestone dip 40° west; the conglomerate is horizontal and lies across the eroded edges of the other beds; the dyke cuts the shale, sandstone and limestone but not the conglomerate; a fault offsets the dyke.

History: shale deposited (deep, calm water), then sandstone (shallower, higher energy), then limestone (warm, shallow sea) → tilting 40° west → dyke intruded → faulting (fault offsets the dyke, so younger) → uplift and erosion (unconformity) → conglomerate deposited (high-energy, perhaps a beach or river as the sea returned). The fault must be older than the conglomerate if it does not cut it.

#### Common mistakes

- Putting the dyke before the tilting without evidence (the dyke here could cut tilted or untilted beds; check whether it is itself tilted).
- Forgetting the erosion stage at an unconformity.

### Laboratory tests, measurements and data

#### What you need to know

- Use physical and chemical tests to identify minerals: density (hefting) and Mohs' hardness (fingernail, copper coin, steel pin) (1.1).
- Use apparatus to measure mass, time, volume, temperature and length (for example the jelly lava flow experiment, 1.2d; porosity by immersion, 1.3c), with attention to accuracy (repeat timings, set square, plumb line).
- Compile and analyse geological data sets, including with a geographic information system (GIS), for example assessing a site for civil engineering using British Geological Survey and Environment Agency data.
- Use ICT (computer modelling, data loggers, software) to collect and process data.

#### Explained

Accuracy and reliability: repeat measurements and take a mean; use a set square or plumb line to measure angles and vertical heights accurately; use a data logger or video to time fast events; identify anomalous results; control variables.

GIS (geographic information system) software combines layers of mapped data (geology, faults, boreholes, groundwater, flood risk, landslide records, topography) so a site can be assessed for a project such as a landfill, reservoir or tunnel (4.2g, 4.2h).

### Maths skills (Appendix A)

#### What you need to know

- Arithmetic: decimals, standard form, ratios, fractions and percentages, squares and square roots.
- Handling data: significant figures, means, frequency tables, bar charts and histograms, scatter graphs; sampling.
- Algebra and graphs: substitute into and rearrange simple equations; plot and interpret graphs, including gradients.
- Geometry: angles, scale, area and volume, 3D interpretation of maps and sections.

#### Explained

Examples of geological calculations: porosity percentage (1.3c); spreading rate = distance ÷ time (2.2); half-life ages (2.3d); ratio of ore to gangue; percentage of calcium carbonate in a sand (mass lost after acid treatment ÷ original mass × 100); map scale (1 : 25 000 means 4 cm = 1 km); mean clast size; vertical exaggeration on cross-sections; throw on a fault; areas of outcrops or craters on photographs.

#### Worked example

1. A 20 g sand sample loses 3 g after dissolving its shell fragments in acid. Percentage calcium carbonate = 3 ÷ 20 × 100 = 15%.
2. On a 1 : 50 000 map, two points are 7.4 cm apart. Real distance = 7.4 × 50 000 = 370 000 cm = 3.7 km.
3. An ore sample contains 45 g of galena and 180 g of quartz and calcite. Ore : gangue = 45 : 180 = 1 : 4.
4. Five clasts measure 32, 45, 28, 51 and 39 mm. Mean = 195 ÷ 5 = 39 mm.

#### Common mistakes

- Mis-converting map scales (cm to km: divide by 100 000).
- Giving too many significant figures.

### Check questions

1. Give the six-figure grid reference for a point 3 tenths east of easting line 52 and 8 tenths north of northing line 19. [1 mark]
2. List four things that should be included on an annotated field sketch. [4 marks]
3. Write a full rock description of a medium-grained, well-sorted, red rock with rounded quartz grains and large-scale cross bedding that does not react with acid. Name the rock and suggest its environment. [5 marks]
4. On a geological map, a rock boundary follows the contour lines exactly. What does this show? [1 mark]
5. Explain how a fold can be recognised on a geological map. [3 marks]

8. Explain how a GIS can be used to assess a potential reservoir site. [2 marks]
9. A cross-section shows: limestone, overlain by shale, both folded into a syncline; a granite intrusion with an aureole cutting both; an erosion surface; horizontal sandstone above. Write the geological history. [5 marks]

### Mark scheme

2. Any four: title (1); location / grid reference (1); direction faced / orientation (1); scale (1); labels / annotations of features and interpretations (1).

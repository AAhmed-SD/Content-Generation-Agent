### ATP and why respiration never stops

*Refs: OCR A J247/J250 B1.3a; OCR B J257/J260 B4.1.2.*

#### What you need to know

- Respiration is a chemical process that happens all the time in every living cell.
- The energy released by respiration is used to make a molecule called **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate).
- ATP is the cell's immediate energy supply. When it is broken down, it releases energy for processes the cell needs.
- Cells cannot store much ATP, so respiration has to continue all the time to keep making it.
- Aerobic respiration makes much more ATP from each glucose molecule than anaerobic respiration.
- Respiration is exothermic: some of the energy is transferred to the surroundings as heat.

#### Explained

Glucose holds a lot of energy, but in one large package. A cell needs energy in small, ready-to-use amounts, many times a second. Respiration releases the energy from glucose step by step and uses it to join a phosphate group onto ADP (adenosine diphosphate), making ATP.

ADP + phosphate + energy from respiration → ATP

When a process in the cell needs energy, ATP is broken back into ADP and phosphate, releasing the energy where it is needed. The ADP is then recycled and turned back into ATP by respiration.

Processes that use ATP include:

- muscle contraction, so animals can move
- active transport, such as root hair cells taking up mineral ions, or the gut absorbing glucose against a concentration gradient
- building large molecules, such as proteins from amino acids, and starch, glycogen and lipids
- nerve impulses and cell division.

Because a cell holds only a few seconds' supply of ATP, it must respire continuously. If respiration stops (for example, cyanide blocks it), ATP runs out within moments and the cell dies.

Aerobic respiration breaks glucose down completely to carbon dioxide and water, so it releases much more energy and makes far more ATP per glucose (roughly 30 or more) than anaerobic respiration (2). That is why muscles that are respiring anaerobically tire quickly, and why yeast fermenting without oxygen grows slowly.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying respiration "makes energy". Energy cannot be made. Respiration releases energy stored in glucose and transfers it to ATP.
- Confusing respiration with breathing. Breathing moves air in and out of the lungs; respiration is a reaction inside cells, and plants respire day and night.
- Thinking only animal cells respire, or that plants respire only at night. Plant cells respire all the time; in daylight photosynthesis is usually faster, so the plant takes in carbon dioxide overall.

### Field sampling methods

*Refs: OCR A J247/J250 B6.1a; OCR B J257/J260 B3.4.2.*

#### What you need to know

- **Distribution** is where organisms are found; **abundance** is how many there are.
- Plants and slow-moving animals are sampled with quadrats. Random quadrats estimate population size; a transect shows how distribution changes across a habitat. (These are in Biology 4.7.2.1.)
- Mobile animals are estimated by **capture–mark–release–recapture**.
- Animals are collected with pooters, sweep nets, pond nets and pitfall traps.
- **Identification keys** are used to name the organisms found.
- **Indicator species** show the level of pollution.
- Abiotic factors are measured with instruments such as light meters, thermometers and pH probes.
- Results from a sample are **scaled up** to estimate the population of the whole area.

#### Explained

**Capture–mark–release–recapture.** Animals that move about cannot be counted in a quadrat, so a sample is caught, marked harmlessly (for example a small dot of non-toxic paint on a beetle's shell) and released. Later a second sample is caught. Some of these will be marked. If the marked animals have mixed evenly back into the population, the fraction of marked animals in the second sample equals the fraction of the whole population that was marked:

estimated population = (number in first sample × number in second sample) ÷ number marked in second sample

The method only works if:

- no animals are born, die, arrive or leave between the two samples
- the marks do not wash or rub off
- the marks do not make the animals easier for predators to see
- the marked animals have time to mix back into the population.

**Collecting equipment.**

- Pooter: a jar with two tubes. Sucking on one tube draws small insects up the other into the jar.
- Sweep net: swept through long grass to catch flying and crawling insects.
- Pond net: swept through water to catch aquatic animals.
- Pitfall trap: a container sunk into the ground with its rim level with the soil, often with a cover raised above it to keep out rain. Animals walking across the ground fall in. Traps are checked often so the animals can be released.

**Identification keys.** A dichotomous key asks a series of questions, each with two answers (for example "Does it have six legs?"), until only one name is left.

**Indicator species.** Some species survive only in clean conditions; others are found only where there is pollution. For example, many lichens cannot grow where the air contains a lot of sulfur dioxide, and mayfly larvae need water with plenty of dissolved oxygen, while bloodworms and sludge worms tolerate polluted, low-oxygen water.

**Scaling up.** If a sample covers a known area, multiply the mean number per sample by the number of sample areas that fit into the whole habitat.

#### Worked example

A student catches 40 woodlice under logs in a garden, marks each with a dot of paint and releases them. Two days later she catches 50 woodlice, of which 8 are marked. Estimate the population.

estimated population = (40 × 50) ÷ 8 = 2000 ÷ 8 = 250 woodlice.

Ten quadrats of 0.25 m² in a 120 m² field contain a total of 30 daisies. Mean per quadrat = 3. Number of quadrats that fit in the field = 120 ÷ 0.25 = 480. Estimated population = 3 × 480 = 1440 daisies.

#### Common mistakes

- Dividing by the wrong number in capture–recapture. The number of marked animals recaptured always goes on the bottom.
- Leaving too long between samples, so births and deaths change the population, or too short, so the marked animals have not mixed back in.
- Forgetting to convert quadrat area and field area to the same units before scaling up.
- Placing quadrats where it looks interesting instead of using random coordinates, which biases the estimate.

### OCR's named diseases

*Refs: OCR A J247/J250 B6.3f; OCR B J257/J260 B2.1.4, B2.1.5.*

#### What you need to know

OCR A asks for at least one human infection, one plant disease and sexually transmitted infections including HIV/AIDS; its plant examples are tobacco mosaic virus, barley powdery mildew and crown gall. OCR B names influenza, Salmonella, athlete's foot, malaria and HIV/AIDS in humans, and tobacco mosaic virus, ash dieback and crown gall in plants. HIV, Salmonella, malaria and tobacco mosaic virus are in Biology 4.3.1.2 to 4.3.1.5. For each of the others, know the pathogen type, how it spreads, its effects and how it is controlled.

| Disease | Pathogen | Spread | Effects | Control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Influenza (flu) | Virus | Droplets from coughs and sneezes; touching contaminated surfaces | Fever, aches, tiredness, cough; can be fatal in elderly or ill people | Yearly vaccine, hand washing, staying at home when ill; antiviral drugs in some cases |
| Athlete's foot | Fungus | Direct contact; damp floors, towels and shoes | Itchy, flaking, cracked skin, usually between the toes | Antifungal creams or powders; dry feet well; do not share towels; wear sandals in communal showers |
| Barley powdery mildew | Fungus | Spores carried by wind | White powdery patches on leaves; less photosynthesis, lower grain yield | Fungicides, resistant varieties, crop rotation, removing infected stubble |
| Crown gall | Bacterium (*Agrobacterium tumefaciens*) | Soil bacteria enter through wounds in roots or stem base; contaminated tools | Swollen, tumour-like galls; weakened, stunted plant | No cure: remove and destroy infected plants, avoid wounding, clean tools, do not replant in infected soil |
| Ash dieback | Fungus | Spores from infected fallen leaves blown by wind; movement of infected young trees | Leaves wilt and fall early, dark patches on bark, crown of tree dies back; many trees die | No cure: restrict movement of ash plants, remove infected young trees, breed and plant tolerant trees |

#### Explained

**Influenza** viruses change their surface proteins slightly every year, so antibodies made after last year's infection or vaccine may not recognise this year's virus. That is why flu vaccines are offered every autumn, especially to older people and others at high risk.

**Athlete's foot** fungi feed on dead skin and grow best where it is warm and damp. Keeping feet dry removes the conditions they need.

**Barley powdery mildew** grows on the leaf surface and sends feeding structures into the leaf cells. Covered leaf area cannot absorb light, so less photosynthesis happens and less glucose is available for grain.

**Crown gall** is caused by a bacterium that transfers some of its own DNA into the plant's cells. The plant cells then divide out of control and form a gall. Scientists use this same ability of *Agrobacterium* to carry useful genes into crop plants in genetic engineering.

**Ash dieback** was first confirmed in Britain in 2012 and has since spread through most of the country. It is expected to kill a large majority of ash trees, which affects the many insects, lichens and birds that depend on ash. A small number of trees show tolerance, and these are being used to breed more resistant trees.

**STIs.** HIV (a virus) is in Biology 4.3.1.2 and gonorrhoea (a bacterium) in 4.3.1.3. Both are spread by sexual contact and their spread is reduced by using condoms, testing and treatment. Any correct STI can be given where OCR asks for "sexually transmitted infections".

#### Common mistakes

- Treating a viral disease with antibiotics. Influenza is a virus; antibiotics have no effect on it.
- Saying fungicides "cure" ash dieback. There is no practical cure for trees in woodland; control depends on stopping spread and breeding tolerant trees.
- Calling crown gall a fungal disease because it causes swellings. It is bacterial.

### Hydroponics and biological control

*Refs: OCR A J247 B6.2b; OCR B J257 B6.4.6.*

#### What you need to know

- **Hydroponics** is growing plants without soil, with their roots in a solution of mineral ions.
- **Biological control** uses a living organism (a predator, parasite or pathogen) to reduce the population of a pest.
- Know the advantages and disadvantages of each, compared with using soil, fertilisers and chemical pesticides. (Fertilisers, pesticides and genetic modification are in Biology 4.6.2.4 and 4.7.5.)

#### Explained

**Hydroponics.** The nutrient solution can be adjusted to give each crop exactly the mineral ions it needs, and it is pumped past the roots so they also get oxygen. Crops are usually grown in glasshouses, where temperature, light and carbon dioxide can also be controlled.

Advantages:

- high yields in a small space, all year round
- no soil, so no soil pests, diseases or weeds
- mineral ions are supplied exactly, with less waste
- crops can be grown where the soil is poor or there is little land.

Disadvantages:

- expensive to set up and to run (pumps, heating and lighting use energy)
- needs skilled monitoring of the solution
- a disease in the shared solution can spread quickly to every plant
- plants may need artificial support without soil.

**Biological control.** A natural enemy of the pest is released. Examples: ladybirds eat aphids; a tiny parasitic wasp lays its eggs in glasshouse whitefly larvae, killing them.

Advantages:

- no chemical residues on food or in the environment
- usually affects only the target pest, not other species
- the control organism can breed, so it may not need to be reapplied
- pests do not become resistant in the way they can to pesticides.

Disadvantages:

- slower than a chemical pesticide
- reduces the pest but does not wipe it out
- the introduced species may eat other species, or itself become a pest. For example, cane toads introduced to Australia to control beetles in sugar cane fields spread widely and harmed native wildlife.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying hydroponics needs no fertiliser. The nutrient solution is itself a fertiliser solution.
- Saying biological control "kills all the pests". The aim is to keep the pest population low; if all the pests died, the control organism would die out too.

### Check questions

- OCR B (Twenty First Century) J258 and J260: C3.4.6 and C3.4.7 (empirical formula, with ratio arithmetic), C5.1.6 (chromatographic methods, including aqueous and non-aqueous solvents and locating agents).

### Empirical formulae

*Refs: OCR A J248/J250 C2.1d; OCR B J258/J260 C3.4.6, C3.4.7.*

#### What you need to know

- The **empirical formula** is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.
- The **molecular formula** is the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule.
- Deduce an empirical formula from the numbers of atoms in a model, a diagram or a molecular formula, and the other way round (what models fit a given empirical formula).
- Calculate an empirical formula from the masses (or percentages by mass) of each element, using relative atomic masses.

#### Explained

To get the empirical formula from a molecular formula, divide all the numbers by their highest common factor.

| Compound | Molecular formula | Empirical formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ethene | C₂H₄ | CH₂ |
| Glucose | C₆H₁₂O₆ | CH₂O |
| Hydrogen peroxide | H₂O₂ | HO |
| Water | H₂O | H₂O |

Ionic compounds and giant covalent structures (such as sodium chloride and silicon dioxide) do not form molecules, so their formula is always the empirical formula: NaCl, SiO₂.

From masses, the method is:

1. Write the mass (or percentage) of each element.
2. Divide each by its relative atomic mass. This gives the relative number of moles of atoms.
3. Divide every answer by the smallest one.
4. If the results are not close to whole numbers, multiply them all by the same small number (2, 3 or 4) to make them whole.
5. Write the formula with the whole numbers as subscripts.

If you know the relative formula mass of the compound, you can go on to find the molecular formula: divide Mr by the mass of one empirical formula unit, and multiply the empirical formula by the answer.

#### Worked example

**From a diagram.** A ball-and-stick model shows a molecule with 4 carbon atoms and 10 hydrogen atoms. Molecular formula C₄H₁₀. Divide by 2: empirical formula C₂H₅.

**From masses.** A 4.0 g sample of an oxide of copper contains 3.2 g of copper. Find its empirical formula. (Ar: Cu = 63.5, O = 16)

| | Cu | O |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mass (g) | 3.2 | 4.0 − 3.2 = 0.8 |
| ÷ Ar | 3.2 ÷ 63.5 = 0.0504 | 0.8 ÷ 16 = 0.0500 |
| ÷ smallest | 1.01 | 1 |

Ratio 1 : 1, so the empirical formula is CuO.

**Needing a multiplier.** A compound contains 52.9% aluminium and 47.1% oxygen by mass. (Ar: Al = 27, O = 16)

- Al: 52.9 ÷ 27 = 1.96; O: 47.1 ÷ 16 = 2.94
- Divide by 1.96: Al 1, O 1.5
- Multiply by 2: Al 2, O 3. Empirical formula Al₂O₃.

**Molecular formula.** A hydrocarbon has empirical formula CH₂ and Mr = 56. Mass of CH₂ = 12 + 2 = 14. 56 ÷ 14 = 4, so the molecular formula is C₄H₈.

#### Common mistakes

- Dividing the masses by the atomic number, or by the Mr of the element's molecule (O₂ = 32), instead of the relative atomic mass.
- Rounding too early. A ratio of 1 : 1.5 must be doubled to 2 : 3, not rounded to 1 : 2.
- Forgetting to work out the mass of oxygen by subtraction when only the metal and the oxide are weighed.
- Simplifying a molecular formula that cannot be simplified, or writing an empirical formula with fractions.

### Thin layer chromatography and choosing a solvent

*Refs: OCR A J248/J250 C2.1g; OCR B J258/J260 C5.1.6.*

#### What you need to know

- Describe paper chromatography and thin layer chromatography (TLC).
- In TLC the stationary phase is a thin layer of a solid such as silica gel or alumina, spread on a glass, plastic or aluminium sheet. The mobile phase is the solvent.
- Substances separate because they spend different amounts of time dissolved in the mobile phase and stuck to the stationary phase.
- The solvent can be water (aqueous) or another liquid such as ethanol or propanone (non-aqueous). Substances that do not dissolve in water need a non-aqueous solvent.
- Colourless spots are made visible with a **locating agent** or, on some TLC plates, ultraviolet light.
- Rf values (in Chemistry 4.8.1.3) are calculated the same way for TLC as for paper.

#### Explained

**Running a TLC plate.**

1. Draw a pencil line about 1 cm from the bottom of the plate. Pencil does not dissolve and move with the solvent; ink would.
2. Put a small, concentrated spot of each sample on the line, using a capillary tube.
3. Stand the plate in a beaker or tank with solvent below the pencil line, so the spots do not dissolve into the solvent pool.
4. Cover the container so the air inside is saturated with solvent vapour; this stops the solvent evaporating from the plate and gives even movement.
5. Remove the plate when the solvent is near the top, and mark the solvent front in pencil straight away, before it evaporates.
6. Locate the spots and measure the distances.

**Why TLC is often preferred to paper.** The solid layer is more even than paper fibres, so spots are sharper and better separated. TLC runs in a few minutes, needs only tiny samples, and the plate can be used with a wider range of solvents, including ones that would damage paper.

**Choosing the solvent.** A substance only moves if it dissolves in the mobile phase. Food colourings dissolve in water, so water works for them. The ink in a permanent marker does not dissolve in water, so it stays on the baseline in water but separates in ethanol or propanone. The same substance has a different Rf in different solvents, so Rf values are only compared when the solvent (and stationary phase) are the same. A pure substance gives a single spot in every solvent tried; if a sample gives one spot in one solvent, trying a second solvent is a stronger test of purity.

**Locating agents.** Many substances (such as amino acids and sugars) are colourless. After the plate dries, it is sprayed with or exposed to a chemical that reacts with them to give coloured spots. Examples:

- ninhydrin spray, which gives purple spots with amino acids
- iodine vapour, which makes many organic compounds show up as brown spots.

Some TLC plates contain a substance that glows under ultraviolet light; compounds on the plate block the glow and show as dark spots, which are circled in pencil.

#### Worked example

On a TLC plate run in ethanol, the solvent front is 6.0 cm from the baseline. An unknown dye gives spots at 1.5 cm and 4.2 cm. Reference dye X gives one spot at 4.2 cm.

Rf of the upper spot = 4.2 ÷ 6.0 = 0.70. Rf of X = 0.70. The unknown contains two substances; one may be X. To be more confident, run both samples again in a different solvent and check the spots still match.

#### Common mistakes

- Putting the baseline below the solvent level, so the samples wash off into the solvent.
- Measuring to the edge of a spot instead of its centre.
- Calling the solvent the stationary phase. The solvent moves: it is the mobile phase.
- Concluding two substances are the same from one solvent alone. Different substances can share an Rf value in one solvent.

### Electrolysis with non-inert electrodes

*Refs: OCR A J248/J250 C3.4e.*

#### What you need to know

- **Inert electrodes** (graphite or platinum) do not react; they only carry electrons. The products come from the ions in the electrolyte (Chemistry 4.4.3.4).
- **Non-inert (active) electrodes** take part in the reaction. In copper sulfate solution with copper electrodes, the copper anode dissolves and copper is deposited on the cathode.
- This is how copper is purified and how objects are electroplated.
- Compare the results of electrolysing copper sulfate solution with inert and with copper electrodes.

#### Explained

**Copper sulfate with graphite (inert) electrodes.**

- Cathode (−): Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. A pink-brown coating of copper forms.
- Anode (+): oxygen gas is given off (from water or hydroxide ions; sulfate ions are not discharged).
- The blue colour fades as Cu²⁺ ions are used up, and the solution becomes acidic because H⁺ ions and sulfate ions are left behind.

**Copper sulfate with copper (non-inert) electrodes.**

- Anode (+): Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻. Copper atoms lose electrons and go into solution, so the anode gets lighter. No gas is given off.
- Cathode (−): Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. The cathode gets heavier.
- Copper ions are added to the solution at the anode as fast as they are removed at the cathode, so the concentration and the blue colour stay the same.
- In a perfect cell the mass lost by the anode equals the mass gained by the cathode.

**Purifying copper.** The anode is a block of impure copper; the cathode is a thin sheet of pure copper. Copper from the anode dissolves and is deposited as pure copper on the cathode. Impurities that do not dissolve fall below the anode as a sludge, which can contain valuable metals such as silver and gold.

**Electroplating.** The object to be coated is made the cathode; the anode is the plating metal; the electrolyte contains ions of the plating metal. For example, to silver-plate a spoon, the spoon is the cathode, a silver bar is the anode and the solution contains silver ions. Plating is used to prevent corrosion or to improve appearance.

#### Worked example

A student electrolyses copper sulfate solution with copper electrodes for 20 minutes.

| | Start mass (g) | End mass (g) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anode | 5.42 | 5.15 |
| Cathode | 5.38 | 5.64 |

Anode change = 5.15 − 5.42 = −0.27 g. Cathode change = 5.64 − 5.38 = +0.26 g. The cathode gains almost exactly what the anode loses, as expected. The small difference can come from copper flaking off the anode or the cathode not being dried fully before weighing.

#### Practical

This is part of OCR's electrolysis practical group (PAG C2 in J248, PAG C1 in J250; see [practical-activity-groups.md](practical-activity-groups.md)). A typical investigation varies the current or the time and measures the change in mass of each copper electrode. Clean the electrodes with emery paper first; rinse the cathode with water and then propanone, and dry it before weighing so the mass of liquid is not included. Copper sulfate solution is harmful and an irritant, so wear eye protection.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying oxygen is given off at a copper anode. With a copper anode, the copper itself is oxidised instead.
- Writing the anode half equation with electrons on the wrong side. Oxidation is loss of electrons: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻.
- Saying the solution changes colour when copper electrodes are used. The concentration of Cu²⁺ stays the same.
- Making the object to be plated the anode. It must be the cathode, where metal ions gain electrons and form metal.

### Check questions

1. Give the empirical formula of: (a) C₃H₆ (b) C₂H₆O (c) N₂O₄. [3 marks]
2. 2.4 g of magnesium reacts completely with oxygen to form 4.0 g of magnesium oxide. Find the empirical formula of magnesium oxide. (Ar: Mg = 24, O = 16) [3 marks]
3. A compound is 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen and 53.3% oxygen by mass. Its Mr is 60. Find its empirical and molecular formulae. (Ar: C = 12, H = 1, O = 16) [4 marks]
4. Give two advantages of thin layer chromatography over paper chromatography. [2 marks]
5. A student runs a sample of permanent marker ink in water and sees no movement. Explain why, and suggest how to improve the experiment. [2 marks]
6. Explain why a locating agent is needed to see amino acids on a chromatogram. [2 marks]
7. Copper sulfate solution is electrolysed with copper electrodes. Write the half equation at each electrode. [2 marks]
8. Explain why the blue colour of the solution fades with graphite electrodes but not with copper electrodes. [3 marks]
9. Describe how electrolysis is used to purify copper. [4 marks]

### Mark scheme

1. (a) CH₂ (1) (b) C₂H₆O (it cannot be simplified) (1) (c) NO₂ (1).
2. Mass of O = 4.0 − 2.4 = 1.6 g (1). Mg: 2.4 ÷ 24 = 0.1; O: 1.6 ÷ 16 = 0.1 (1). Ratio 1 : 1, MgO (1).
3. C 40.0 ÷ 12 = 3.33; H 6.7 ÷ 1 = 6.7; O 53.3 ÷ 16 = 3.33 (1). Ratio 1 : 2 : 1, empirical formula CH₂O (1). Mass of CH₂O = 30; 60 ÷ 30 = 2 (1). Molecular formula C₂H₄O₂ (1).
4. Any two: faster; better / sharper separation; needs smaller samples; more solvents can be used; more sensitive.
5. The ink is insoluble in water, so it does not move with the mobile phase (1). Use a non-aqueous solvent such as ethanol or propanone (1).
6. Amino acids are colourless, so the spots cannot be seen (1). The locating agent (for example ninhydrin) reacts with them to form coloured spots (1).
7. Anode: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ (1). Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (1).
8. Blue colour comes from Cu²⁺ ions (1). With graphite, Cu²⁺ ions are removed at the cathode and not replaced, so the colour fades (1). With copper, the anode releases Cu²⁺ ions as fast as they are removed, so the concentration stays the same (1).
9. Impure copper is the anode (1); pure copper is the cathode (1); electrolyte is copper sulfate solution (1); copper dissolves from the anode and pure copper is deposited on the cathode, while impurities fall as sludge (1).

#### Explained

**PAG 6: Respiration in yeast with different substrates (OCR B B4.1.5).** Yeast respires sugars and releases carbon dioxide. The rate of carbon dioxide release shows the rate of respiration.

1. Make up a yeast suspension and equal-concentration solutions of different sugars (for example glucose, sucrose, fructose, lactose).
2. Mix equal volumes of yeast suspension and one sugar solution in a boiling tube. Stand it in a water bath at a fixed temperature (about 35 °C) for a few minutes so it reaches that temperature.
3. Fit a bung and delivery tube. Either collect the gas in an upturned measuring cylinder or gas syringe, or count bubbles through water in a set time.
4. Measure the volume of gas in a fixed time (for example 5 minutes). Repeat for each sugar, and do a control with water instead of sugar.

Control: the same mass of yeast, volume and concentration of sugar, temperature and time. Yeast usually respires glucose and fructose fastest, sucrose more slowly (it must first be broken down) and lactose hardly at all (yeast lacks the enzyme to digest it).

**PAG 6: Respirometers.** A respirometer measures oxygen uptake by small organisms such as woodlice or germinating seeds. The organisms sit on a gauze above a chemical that absorbs carbon dioxide (such as soda lime or potassium hydroxide). As they use oxygen, the gas volume in the tube falls, and a drop of coloured liquid in a capillary tube moves towards them. Distance moved per minute (or volume, from the tube's cross-section) gives the rate. A second tube with glass beads instead of organisms is the control, to correct for temperature and pressure changes.

**PAG 6: Exercise and pulse rate.** Measure resting pulse (beats in 15 seconds × 4), exercise for a fixed time (such as 2 minutes of step-ups), then measure pulse every minute until it returns to resting. The recovery time is a measure of fitness.

**Ethics with living organisms.** Use the smallest number of animals needed, keep them in their habitat conditions, handle them gently, return them to where they were found, and never use vertebrates for experiments that harm them. Students' own data (pulse, reaction time) are used only with their consent.

#### Common mistakes

- Not letting the tubes reach the water-bath temperature before starting timing.
- Forgetting the control tube in a respirometer, so temperature changes are mistaken for oxygen uptake.
- In yeast experiments, changing the mass of sugar instead of keeping the concentration the same.
- Counting bubbles when bubbles are different sizes; a gas syringe gives more accurate volumes.

#### Explained

**PAG 1: Reactivity trend.** Two common ways to put metals in order of reactivity:

- *Temperature rise with acid.* Add the same mass of each metal powder to the same volume and concentration of dilute hydrochloric acid in an insulated cup, and record the maximum temperature rise. A more reactive metal gives a larger rise. Use powders of similar particle size, and only metals that react safely (magnesium, zinc, iron, copper; never sodium or potassium with acid).
- *Displacement.* Put a piece of each metal into solutions of the other metals' salts. If the metal is coated or the solution changes colour (or the temperature rises), the added metal is more reactive and has displaced the other. Record results in a grid and count the reactions for each metal.

Halogen displacement (chlorine, bromine and iodine solutions with halide solutions) is another reactivity trend: a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from its salt, shown by a colour change.

**PAG 4: Distillation.**

- *Simple distillation* separates a liquid from a dissolved solid (water from salty water). Heat the flask; the water boils, the vapour is cooled in a Liebig condenser (cold water in at the bottom, out at the top, so the jacket stays full), and the pure liquid drips into a beaker. The salt stays in the flask. The thermometer bulb sits at the side arm, to read the temperature of the vapour that is distilling.
- *Fractional distillation* separates liquids with close boiling points, such as ethanol (78 °C) and water (100 °C). A fractionating column packed with glass beads sits on the flask. Vapour condenses and re-boils many times up the column, so the vapour at the top is mostly the lower-boiling liquid. Collect the liquid while the thermometer reads near 78 °C; change the beaker when the temperature rises.
- Safety: use anti-bumping granules for smooth boiling, never heat a sealed apparatus, and use an electric heater or water bath for flammable liquids like ethanol.

**PAG 5: Identification of species.** Use clean nichrome wire (dipped in acid and heated until there is no colour) for flame tests; add sodium hydroxide solution drop by drop for metal hydroxide precipitates; acidify with dilute nitric acid before adding silver nitrate (halides) or with hydrochloric acid before adding barium chloride (sulfate), so carbonate ions do not give a false precipitate. **PAG 6: Titration.** Rinse the burette with the solution it will hold, fill it with the funnel removed, and read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level. Add the alkali from a pipette (using a pipette filler) to a conical flask on a white tile, add a few drops of indicator, then add acid quickly for a rough titre, and then slowly, drop by drop near the end point, for accurate titres. Repeat until two titres agree within 0.10 cm³ (concordant) and take their mean.

#### Common mistakes

- Placing the thermometer bulb in the boiling liquid during distillation instead of at the side arm.
- Running condenser water in at the top, which leaves air in the jacket and poor cooling.

- Including the rough titre in the mean.

## 4.2 Bonding, structure, and the properties of matter

How atoms are held together decides how a substance behaves. This topic covers ionic, covalent and metallic bonding, the structures they make, why those structures have the properties they do, the forms of carbon, and nanoparticles (Chemistry only).

### 4.2.1.1 Chemical bonds

#### What you need to know

There are three types of strong chemical bonds: ionic, covalent and metallic.

- For ionic bonding the particles are oppositely charged ions.
- For covalent bonding the particles are atoms that share pairs of electrons.
- For metallic bonding the particles are atoms that share delocalised electrons.

Ionic bonding occurs in compounds formed from metals combined with non-metals. Covalent bonding occurs in most non-metallic elements and in compounds of non-metals. Metallic bonding occurs in metallic elements and alloys.

You should be able to explain chemical bonding in terms of electrostatic forces and the transfer or sharing of electrons.

#### Explained

| Bond | Between | What happens to electrons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ionic | A metal and a non-metal | Transferred from the metal to the non-metal |
| Covalent | Non-metals | Shared in pairs |
| Metallic | Metal atoms | Outer electrons delocalised (free to move) through the whole structure |

### 4.2.1.2 Ionic bonding

#### What you need to know

- When a metal atom reacts with a non-metal atom, electrons in the outer shell of the metal atom are transferred. Metal atoms lose electrons to become positively charged ions. Non-metal atoms gain electrons to become negatively charged ions.
- The ions produced by metals in Groups 1 and 2 and by non-metals in Groups 6 and 7 have the electronic structure of a noble gas (Group 0).
- The electron transfer during the formation of an ionic compound can be represented by a dot and cross diagram.
- The charge on the ions produced by metals in Groups 1 and 2 and by non-metals in Groups 6 and 7 relates to the group number of the element in the periodic table.
- You should be able to draw dot and cross diagrams for ionic compounds formed by metals in Groups 1 and 2 with non-metals in Groups 6 and 7, and work out the charge on the ions from the group number.

#### Explained

| Group | Electrons lost or gained | Ion charge | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Lose 1 | +1 | Na⁺ |
| 2 | Lose 2 | +2 | Mg²⁺ |
| 6 | Gain 2 | −2 | O²⁻ |
| 7 | Gain 1 | −1 | Cl⁻ |

**Sodium chloride.** Sodium (2,8,1) loses its outer electron to chlorine (2,8,7). Sodium becomes Na⁺ (2,8) and chlorine becomes Cl⁻ (2,8,8). Both now have the electronic structure of a noble gas.

**Magnesium oxide.** Magnesium (2,8,2) gives two electrons to oxygen (2,6). Mg²⁺ (2,8) and O²⁻ (2,8).

In a dot and cross diagram, one atom's electrons are drawn as dots, the other's as crosses. Show the ions in square brackets with the charge outside, top right.

#### Common mistakes

- Drawing a shared pair in an ionic compound. Electrons are transferred, not shared.
- Writing the charge inside the brackets, or forgetting the brackets.

### 4.2.1.3 Ionic compounds

#### What you need to know

- An ionic compound is a giant structure of ions. Ionic compounds are held together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions. These forces act in all directions in the lattice, and this is called ionic bonding.
- The structure of sodium chloride can be represented in different forms.
- You should be able to deduce that a compound is ionic from a diagram of its structure, describe the limitations of using dot and cross, ball and stick, two- and three-dimensional diagrams to represent a giant ionic structure, and work out the empirical formula of an ionic compound from a given model or diagram that shows the ions in the structure.

#### Explained

In sodium chloride, each Na⁺ is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions and each Cl⁻ by six Na⁺. The pattern repeats in all directions: a giant lattice.

| Diagram | Limitation |
| --- | --- |
| Dot and cross | Shows electron transfer but not the arrangement of ions in the lattice |
| Ball and stick | The sticks suggest bonds are physical links; the gaps between ions are much bigger than they really are; shows only a tiny part of the lattice |
| Two-dimensional | Does not show the 3D arrangement |
| Three-dimensional (space-filling) | Shows only the outer layer and a small part of the structure |

**Working out a formula.** The charges must balance. Calcium (Ca²⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻): two Cl⁻ balance one Ca²⁺, so CaCl₂. Aluminium (Al³⁺) and oxide (O²⁻): 2 × (+3) = +6 and 3 × (−2) = −6, so Al₂O₃.

### 4.2.1.4 Covalent bonding

#### What you need to know

- When atoms share pairs of electrons, they form covalent bonds. These bonds between atoms are strong.
- Covalently bonded substances may consist of small molecules. Some covalently bonded substances have very large molecules, such as polymers. Some covalently bonded substances have giant covalent structures, such as diamond and silicon dioxide.
- The covalent bonds in molecules and giant structures can be represented in a number of ways.
- You should be able to recognise common substances that consist of small molecules from their chemical formula; draw dot and cross diagrams for the molecules of hydrogen, chlorine, oxygen, nitrogen, hydrogen chloride, water, ammonia and methane; represent the covalent bonds in small molecules, in the repeating units of polymers and in part of giant covalent structures using a line to represent a single bond; describe the limitations of using these representations; and deduce the molecular formula of a substance from a given model or diagram.

#### Explained

| Molecule | Formula | Bonds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrogen | H₂ | One single bond (H–H) |
| Chlorine | Cl₂ | One single bond |
| Oxygen | O₂ | One double bond (O=O): two shared pairs |
| Nitrogen | N₂ | One triple bond (N≡N): three shared pairs |
| Hydrogen chloride | HCl | One single bond |
| Water | H₂O | Two single O–H bonds; oxygen also has two non-bonding pairs |
| Ammonia | NH₃ | Three single N–H bonds; nitrogen also has one non-bonding pair |
| Methane | CH₄ | Four single C–H bonds |

Each atom shares enough electrons to fill its outer shell: hydrogen needs 2; carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and chlorine need 8.

Limitations of representations: dot and cross diagrams do not show the shape of the molecule; displayed formulas (with lines) show which atoms are bonded but not the 3D shape or which electrons came from which atom; ball and stick models show shape but not the electrons.

#### Common mistakes

- Drawing oxygen with a single bond. O₂ has a double bond.
- Forgetting the non-bonding pairs on oxygen in water.

### 4.2.1.5 Metallic bonding

#### What you need to know

- Metals consist of giant structures of atoms arranged in a regular pattern.
- The electrons in the outer shell of metal atoms are delocalised and so are free to move through the whole structure. The sharing of delocalised electrons gives rise to strong metallic bonds.

#### Explained

Picture a regular grid of positive metal ions in a "sea" of delocalised electrons. The strong electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons is the metallic bond.

### 4.2.2.1 The three states of matter

#### What you need to know

- The three states of matter are solid, liquid and gas. Melting and freezing take place at the melting point; boiling and condensing take place at the boiling point.
- The three states of matter can be represented by a simple model, in which the particles are represented by small solid spheres. Particle theory can help to explain melting, boiling, freezing and condensing.
- The amount of energy needed to change state from solid to liquid and from liquid to gas depends on the strength of the forces between the particles of the substance. The nature of the particles involved depends on the type of bonding and the structure of the substance. The stronger the forces between the particles, the higher the melting point and boiling point of the substance.
- (HT only) Limitations of the simple model include that in the model there are no forces, that all particles are represented as spheres and that the spheres are solid.
- You should be able to predict the states of substances at different temperatures, given appropriate data, and explain the different temperatures at which changes of state occur in terms of energy transfers and types of bonding.

#### Explained

| State | Arrangement | Movement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solid | Close together in a regular pattern | Vibrate in fixed positions |
| Liquid | Close together, random | Move around each other |
| Gas | Far apart, random | Move quickly in all directions |

Predicting a state: if room temperature (about 20 °C) is below the melting point, the substance is solid; between the melting and boiling points, liquid; above the boiling point, gas.

(HT only) The model is limited because real particles are not solid spheres, they have forces between them, and the model does not show how far apart the particles really are.

### 4.2.2.2 State symbols

#### What you need to know

In chemical equations, the three states of matter are shown as (s), (l) and (g), with (aq) for aqueous solutions. You should be able to include appropriate state symbols in chemical equations for the reactions in this specification.

#### Explained

Aqueous means dissolved in water. Example: Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g).

### 4.2.2.3 Properties of ionic compounds

#### What you need to know

- Ionic compounds have regular structures (giant ionic lattices) in which there are strong electrostatic forces of attraction in all directions between oppositely charged ions.
- These compounds have high melting points and high boiling points because of the large amounts of energy needed to break the many strong bonds.
- When melted or dissolved in water, ionic compounds conduct electricity because the ions are free to move and so charge can flow.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying solid ionic compounds conduct. The ions are fixed in the solid.
- Saying electrons carry the current in an ionic liquid. The ions move.

### 4.2.2.4 Properties of small molecules

#### What you need to know

- Substances that consist of small molecules are usually gases or liquids that have relatively low melting points and boiling points.
- These substances have only weak forces between the molecules (intermolecular forces). It is these intermolecular forces that are overcome, not the covalent bonds, when the substance melts or boils.
- The intermolecular forces increase with the size of the molecules, so larger molecules have higher melting and boiling points.
- These substances do not conduct electricity because the molecules do not have an overall electric charge.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying covalent bonds break when water boils. The weak intermolecular forces are overcome; the O–H bonds stay intact.

### 4.2.2.5 Polymers

#### What you need to know

- Polymers have very large molecules. The atoms in the polymer molecules are linked to other atoms by strong covalent bonds.
- The intermolecular forces between polymer molecules are relatively strong, and so these substances are solids at room temperature.
- You should be able to recognise polymers from diagrams showing their bonding.

#### Explained

A polymer is shown by its repeating unit in brackets with an "n" outside, meaning the unit repeats many times. Poly(ethene) is –(CH₂–CH₂)ₙ–.

### 4.2.2.6 Giant covalent structures

#### What you need to know

- Substances that consist of giant covalent structures are solids with very high melting points. All of the atoms in these structures are linked to other atoms by strong covalent bonds. These bonds must be overcome to melt or boil these substances.
- Diamond and graphite (forms of carbon) and silicon dioxide (silica) are examples of giant covalent structures.
- You should be able to recognise giant covalent structures from diagrams showing their bonding and structure.

### 4.2.2.7 Properties of metals and alloys

#### What you need to know

- Metals have giant structures of atoms with strong metallic bonding. This means that most metals have high melting and boiling points.
- In pure metals, atoms are arranged in layers, which allows metals to be bent and shaped.
- Pure metals are too soft for many uses and so are mixed with other metals to make alloys, which are harder.
- You should be able to explain why alloys are harder than pure metals in terms of the layers of atoms.

#### Explained

In a pure metal, all the atoms are the same size, so the layers slide over each other easily. In an alloy, atoms of a different size distort the layers, so they cannot slide as easily. The alloy is harder.

### 4.2.2.8 Metals as conductors

#### What you need to know

- Metals are good conductors of electricity because the delocalised electrons in the metal carry electrical charge through the metal.
- Metals are good conductors of thermal energy because energy is transferred by the delocalised electrons.

### 4.2.3.1 Diamond

#### What you need to know

- In diamond, each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds with other carbon atoms in a giant covalent structure, so diamond is very hard, has a very high melting point and does not conduct electricity.
- You should be able to explain the properties of diamond in terms of its structure and bonding.

#### Explained

All four outer electrons of each carbon are used in bonding, so there are no free electrons to carry charge. Breaking the many strong covalent bonds takes a lot of energy, giving the very high melting point. Uses: cutting tools and drill tips.

### 4.2.3.2 Graphite

#### What you need to know

- In graphite, each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds with three other carbon atoms, forming layers of hexagonal rings which have no covalent bonds between the layers.
- In graphite, one electron from each carbon atom is delocalised.
- You should be able to explain the properties of graphite in terms of its structure and bonding, and know that graphite is similar to metals in that it has delocalised electrons.

#### Explained

| Property | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Soft and slippery | Only weak forces between the layers, so the layers slide over each other |
| High melting point | Many strong covalent bonds must be broken |
| Conducts electricity and thermal energy | One delocalised electron per carbon atom can move along the layers |

Uses: pencil leads, lubricants, electrodes.

### 4.2.3.3 Graphene and fullerenes

#### What you need to know

- Graphene is a single layer of graphite and has properties that make it useful in electronics and composites.
- Fullerenes are molecules of carbon atoms with hollow shapes. The structure of fullerenes is based on hexagonal rings of carbon atoms, but they may also contain rings with five or seven carbon atoms. The first fullerene to be discovered was Buckminsterfullerene (C₆₀), which has a spherical shape.
- Carbon nanotubes are cylindrical fullerenes with very high length to diameter ratios. Their properties make them useful for nanotechnology, electronics and materials.
- You should be able to explain the properties of graphene in terms of its structure and bonding, recognise graphene and fullerenes from diagrams, and give examples of the uses of fullerenes, including carbon nanotubes.

#### Explained

Graphene is one atom thick. It is very strong (many strong covalent bonds), very light, and conducts electricity (delocalised electrons).

Uses of fullerenes:

- delivering drugs into the body (molecules can be carried inside the hollow cage)
- lubricants (spherical molecules roll)
- catalysts (large surface area)
- reinforcing materials: nanotubes add strength without much weight, for example in tennis rackets.

### 4.2.4.1 Sizes of particles and their properties (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Nanoscience refers to structures that are 1–100 nm in size, of the order of a few hundred atoms.
- Nanoparticles are smaller than fine particles (PM2.5), which have diameters between 100 and 2500 nm (1 × 10⁻⁷ m and 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m).
- Coarse particles (PM10) have diameters between 1 × 10⁻⁵ m and 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m. Coarse particles are often referred to as dust.
- As the side of a cube decreases by a factor of 10, the surface area to volume ratio increases by a factor of 10.
- Nanoparticles may have properties different from those for the same materials in bulk because of their high surface area to volume ratio. It may also mean that smaller quantities are needed to be effective than for materials with normal particle sizes.
- You should be able to compare "nano" dimensions to typical dimensions of atoms and molecules.

#### Explained

| Particle | Diameter |
| --- | --- |
| Atom | About 0.1 nm |
| Nanoparticle | 1 to 100 nm |
| Fine particle (PM2.5) | 100 to 2500 nm |
| Coarse particle (PM10, dust) | 2500 to 10 000 nm |

#### Worked example

A cube has sides of 10 nm. Another has sides of 1 nm. Compare their surface area to volume ratios.

- 10 nm cube: surface area = 6 × 10² = 600 nm²; volume = 10³ = 1000 nm³; ratio = 0.6.
- 1 nm cube: surface area = 6 nm²; volume = 1 nm³; ratio = 6.

The side fell by a factor of 10 and the ratio rose by a factor of 10.

### 4.2.4.2 Uses of nanoparticles (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Nanoparticles have many applications in medicine, in electronics, in cosmetics and sun creams, as deodorants, and as catalysts.
- New applications for nanoparticulate materials are an important area of research.
- You should consider advantages and disadvantages of the applications of these nanoparticulate materials, but do not need to know specific examples or properties other than those specified.

#### Explained

## 4.1 Atomic structure and the periodic table

Everything is made of atoms. This topic covers what atoms are made of, how the model of the atom developed, isotopes, electron arrangement, and how the periodic table groups elements with similar properties.

### 4.1.1.1 Atoms, elements and compounds

#### What you need to know

- All substances are made of atoms. An atom is the smallest part of an element that can exist.
- Atoms of each element are represented by a chemical symbol, for example O for an atom of oxygen and Na for an atom of sodium.
- There are about 100 different elements. Elements are shown in the periodic table.
- Compounds are formed from elements by chemical reactions. Chemical reactions always involve the formation of one or more new substances, and often involve a detectable energy change.
- Compounds contain two or more elements chemically combined in fixed proportions and can be represented by formulae using the symbols of the atoms from which they were formed.
- Compounds can only be separated into elements by chemical reactions.
- You should be able to use the names and symbols of the first 20 elements, Groups 1 and 7, and other elements in this specification; name compounds of these elements from given formulae or symbol equations; and write word equations and formulae for the reactions in this specification.
- (HT only) You should be able to write balanced half equations and ionic equations where appropriate.

#### Explained

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Element | A substance made of only one type of atom | Oxygen, O₂; iron, Fe |
| Compound | Two or more elements chemically combined in fixed proportions | Water, H₂O; sodium chloride, NaCl |
| Mixture | Two or more substances not chemically combined | Air; sea water |

**Naming compounds.** A compound of a metal and one non-metal ends in "-ide": sodium and chlorine make sodium chloride. If oxygen is also present, it often ends in "-ate": copper, sulfur and oxygen make copper sulfate (CuSO₄).

**Reading a formula.** In H₂SO₄ there are 2 hydrogen atoms, 1 sulfur atom and 4 oxygen atoms. In Ca(OH)₂ the bracket is doubled: 1 calcium, 2 oxygen and 2 hydrogen atoms.

**Balancing an equation.** The number of each type of atom must be the same on both sides. Change only the big numbers in front of formulae, never the small numbers in a formula.

#### Worked example

Balance: Mg + O₂ → MgO.

1. Left: 1 Mg, 2 O. Right: 1 Mg, 1 O.
2. Put 2 in front of MgO: Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Now right has 2 Mg, 2 O.
3. Put 2 in front of Mg: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO.

Balanced: 2 Mg and 2 O on each side.

#### Common mistakes

- Changing a formula to balance an equation (writing MgO₂).
- Calling a mixture a compound. In a compound the elements are chemically joined.

### 4.1.1.2 Mixtures

#### What you need to know

- A mixture consists of two or more elements or compounds not chemically combined together. The chemical properties of each substance in the mixture are unchanged.
- Mixtures can be separated by physical processes such as filtration, crystallisation, simple distillation, fractional distillation and chromatography. These physical processes do not involve chemical reactions and no new substances are made.
- You should be able to describe, explain and give examples of the specified processes of separation, and suggest suitable separation and purification techniques for mixtures when given appropriate information.

#### Explained

| Method | Separates | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Filtration | An insoluble solid from a liquid (sand from water) | Pour through filter paper; the solid stays behind as residue, the liquid passes through as filtrate |
| Crystallisation | A soluble solid from its solution (salt from salt water) | Heat to evaporate some of the water, leave to cool so crystals form, then filter and dry |
| Simple distillation | A liquid from a solution (pure water from salt water) | Heat so the liquid boils; the vapour is cooled in a condenser and collected; the solid stays behind |
| Fractional distillation | Liquids with different boiling points (ethanol from water; crude oil) | Heat the mixture; a fractionating column lets each liquid condense at a different height or temperature |
| Chromatography | Dissolved substances with different solubilities (dyes in ink) | Substances move different distances up paper with a solvent |

Choosing a method: is the substance you want a solid or a liquid? Is it dissolved? Do the liquids have different boiling points?

#### Common mistakes

- Using filtration to get salt out of salt water. Salt is dissolved, so it passes through the filter paper.

### 4.1.1.3 The development of the model of the atom

#### What you need to know

- New experimental evidence may lead to a scientific model being changed or replaced.
- Before the discovery of the electron, atoms were thought to be tiny spheres that could not be divided.
- The discovery of the electron led to the plum pudding model of the atom. The plum pudding model suggested that the atom is a ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it.
- The results from the alpha particle scattering experiment led to the conclusion that the mass of an atom was concentrated at the centre (nucleus) and that the nucleus was charged. This nuclear model replaced the plum pudding model.
- Niels Bohr adapted the nuclear model by suggesting that electrons orbit the nucleus at specific distances. The theoretical calculations of Bohr agreed with experimental observations.
- Later experiments led to the idea that the positive charge of any nucleus could be subdivided into a whole number of smaller particles, each particle having the same amount of positive charge. The name proton was given to these particles.
- The experimental work of James Chadwick provided the evidence to show the existence of neutrons within the nucleus. This was about 20 years after the nucleus became an accepted scientific idea.
- You should be able to describe why the new evidence from the scattering experiment led to a change in the atomic model, and the difference between the plum pudding model and the nuclear model.

#### Explained

**The alpha scattering experiment.** Positive alpha particles were fired at a very thin sheet of gold foil.

| Observation | Conclusion |
| --- | --- |
| Most alpha particles passed straight through | Most of the atom is empty space |
| A few were deflected | The centre of the atom is positively charged, repelling the positive alpha particles |
| A very small number bounced straight back | The mass and positive charge are concentrated in a tiny nucleus |

The plum pudding model could not explain particles bouncing back: its positive charge was spread out, so nothing was dense enough to reflect them.

| Model | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Solid sphere (early 1800s, Dalton) | Atoms are tiny spheres that cannot be divided |
| Plum pudding (Thomson, after discovering the electron) | A ball of positive charge with electrons embedded in it |
| Nuclear (Rutherford, after alpha scattering) | A small, dense, positive nucleus with electrons around it; mostly empty space |
| Bohr | Electrons orbit the nucleus in shells at fixed distances |
| Neutrons added (Chadwick) | The nucleus contains protons and neutrons |

### 4.1.1.4 Relative electrical charges of subatomic particles

#### What you need to know

| Particle | Relative charge |
| --- | --- |
| Proton | +1 |
| Neutron | 0 |
| Electron | −1 |

- In an atom, the number of electrons is equal to the number of protons in the nucleus. Atoms have no overall electrical charge.
- The number of protons in an atom of an element is its atomic number. All atoms of a particular element have the same number of protons. Atoms of different elements have different numbers of protons.

### 4.1.1.5 Size and mass of atoms

#### What you need to know

- Atoms are very small, having a radius of about 0.1 nm (1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m).
- The radius of a nucleus is less than 1/10 000 of that of the atom (about 1 × 10⁻¹⁴ m).
- Almost all of the mass of an atom is in the nucleus.

| Particle | Relative mass |
| --- | --- |
| Proton | 1 |
| Neutron | 1 |
| Electron | Very small |

- The sum of the protons and neutrons in an atom is its mass number.
- Atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons. These atoms are called isotopes of that element.
- Atoms can be represented with the mass number at the top and the atomic number at the bottom, for example ²³₁₁Na.
- You should be able to calculate the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in an atom or ion, given its atomic number and mass number, and relate size and scale of atoms to objects in the physical world.

#### Explained

For ²³₁₁Na (sodium):

- Protons = atomic number = 11.
- Electrons = 11 (same as protons in an atom).
- Neutrons = mass number − atomic number = 23 − 11 = 12.

For an ion, adjust the electrons: Na⁺ has lost one electron, so it has 10 electrons. Cl⁻ (atomic number 17) has gained one, so it has 18.

Isotopes of chlorine: chlorine-35 (17 protons, 18 neutrons) and chlorine-37 (17 protons, 20 neutrons). They react the same way because they have the same number of electrons.

#### Common mistakes

- Using the mass number as the number of neutrons.
- Saying isotopes have different numbers of protons.

### 4.1.1.6 Relative atomic mass

#### What you need to know

- The relative atomic mass of an element is an average value that takes account of the abundance of the isotopes of the element.
- You should be able to calculate the relative atomic mass of an element given the percentage abundance of its isotopes.

```latex
A_r = \frac{(\text{mass}_1 \times \%_1) + (\text{mass}_2 \times \%_2)}{100}
```

#### Worked example

Chlorine is 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37. Calculate its relative atomic mass.

1. (35 × 75) + (37 × 25) = 2625 + 925 = 3550.
2. 3550 ÷ 100 = 35.5.

Answer: Ar = 35.5.

### 4.1.1.7 Electronic structure

#### What you need to know

- The electrons in an atom occupy the lowest available energy levels (innermost available shells).
- The electronic structure of an atom can be represented by numbers or by a diagram. For example, sodium is 2,8,1.
- You should be able to represent the electronic structures of the first twenty elements of the periodic table in both forms.

#### Explained

Shell capacities for the first 20 elements: first shell 2, second shell 8, third shell 8 (then the fourth shell starts).

| Element | Atomic number | Electronic structure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrogen | 1 | 1 |
| Carbon | 6 | 2,4 |
| Oxygen | 8 | 2,6 |
| Neon | 10 | 2,8 |
| Sodium | 11 | 2,8,1 |
| Chlorine | 17 | 2,8,7 |
| Argon | 18 | 2,8,8 |
| Potassium | 19 | 2,8,8,1 |
| Calcium | 20 | 2,8,8,2 |

The number of shells is the period (row); the number of outer electrons is the group (for Groups 1 to 7). Chlorine, 2,8,7: three shells, period 3; seven outer electrons, Group 7.

### 4.1.2.1 The periodic table

#### What you need to know

- The elements in the periodic table are arranged in order of atomic (proton) number, so that elements with similar properties are in columns, known as groups.
- The table is called a periodic table because similar properties occur at regular intervals.
- Elements in the same group have the same number of electrons in their outer shell (outer electrons), and this gives them similar chemical properties.
- You should be able to explain how the electronic structure of atoms follows a pattern, and predict possible reactions and probable reactivity of elements from their positions in the periodic table.

### 4.1.2.2 Development of the periodic table

#### What you need to know

- Before the discovery of protons, neutrons and electrons, scientists attempted to classify the elements by arranging them in order of their atomic weights.
- The early periodic tables were incomplete, and some elements were placed in inappropriate groups if the strict order of atomic weights was followed.
- Mendeleev overcame some of the problems by leaving gaps for elements that he thought had not been discovered, and in some places changed the order based on atomic weights.
- Elements with properties predicted by Mendeleev were discovered and filled the gaps.
- Knowledge of isotopes made it possible to explain why the order based on atomic weights was not always correct.

#### Explained

Example: tellurium has a higher atomic weight than iodine, but iodine behaves like the other halogens. Mendeleev put iodine after tellurium to keep it in the right group. We now know tellurium has isotopes with more neutrons, which raise its atomic weight, but iodine has more protons, so ordering by atomic number puts them the right way round.

Mendeleev predicted the properties of germanium (which he called eka-silicon) before it was discovered. When it was found and matched his predictions, scientists accepted his table.

### 4.1.2.3 Metals and non-metals

#### What you need to know

- Elements that react to form positive ions are metals.
- Elements that do not form positive ions are non-metals.
- The majority of elements are metals. Metals are found to the left and towards the bottom of the periodic table. Non-metals are found towards the right and top.
- You should be able to explain the differences between metals and non-metals on the basis of their characteristic physical and chemical properties, and link these to their atomic structures and positions.

#### Explained

| | Metals | Non-metals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ions | Lose outer electrons to form positive ions | Gain electrons to form negative ions, or share electrons |
| Conduct electricity | Yes | No (except graphite) |
| Melting point | Usually high | Usually low |
| Appearance | Shiny | Dull |
| Physical | Malleable and ductile | Brittle when solid |
| Oxides | Basic | Acidic |

Metals have few outer electrons (1, 2 or 3), so it takes less energy to lose them than to gain enough to fill the shell.

### 4.1.2.4 Group 0

#### What you need to know

- The elements in Group 0 of the periodic table are called the noble gases. They are unreactive and do not easily form molecules because their atoms have stable arrangements of electrons.
- The noble gases have eight electrons in their outer shell, except for helium, which has only two electrons.
- The boiling points of the noble gases increase with increasing relative atomic mass (going down the group).

#### Explained

A full outer shell is stable, so noble gas atoms do not need to gain, lose or share electrons. They exist as single atoms. Uses come from their unreactivity: argon in light bulbs, helium in balloons, neon in signs.

### 4.1.2.5 Group 1

#### What you need to know

- The elements in Group 1 of the periodic table are known as the alkali metals and have characteristic properties because of the single electron in their outer shell.
- You should be able to describe the reactions of the first three alkali metals with oxygen, chlorine and water.
- In Group 1, the reactivity of the elements increases going down the group.
- You should be able to explain how properties of the elements in Group 1 depend on the outer shell of electrons of the atoms, and predict properties from given trends down the group.

#### Explained

| Reaction | Products | Equation (sodium) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| With water | Metal hydroxide (alkaline solution) + hydrogen | 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ |
| With oxygen | Metal oxide | 4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O |
| With chlorine | Metal chloride (a white solid) | 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl |

What you see with water:

| Metal | Observation |
| --- | --- |
| Lithium | Floats, fizzes steadily, moves slowly |
| Sodium | Floats, fizzes fast, melts into a ball, moves quickly |
| Potassium | Floats, melts, fizzes violently, burns with a lilac flame |

**Why reactivity increases down the group.** Each atom further down has more shells. The outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded by inner electrons, so it is less strongly attracted. It is lost more easily, so the metal reacts more readily.

Other trends down Group 1: melting and boiling points decrease; density generally increases. Alkali metals are soft, have low densities, and are stored under oil because they react with air and water.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the outer electron is "lost faster". Say it is lost more easily, because it is less strongly attracted to the nucleus.

### 4.1.2.6 Group 7

#### What you need to know

- The elements in Group 7 of the periodic table are known as the halogens and have similar reactions because they all have seven electrons in their outer shell. The halogens are non-metals and consist of molecules made of pairs of atoms.
- You should be able to describe the nature of the compounds formed when chlorine, bromine and iodine react with metals and non-metals.
- In Group 7, the further down the group an element is, the higher its relative molecular mass, melting point and boiling point.
- In Group 7, the reactivity of the elements decreases going down the group.
- A more reactive halogen can displace a less reactive halogen from an aqueous solution of its salt.
- You should be able to explain how properties of the elements in Group 7 depend on the outer shell of electrons of the atoms, and predict properties from given trends down the group.

#### Explained

| Halogen | Formula | State at room temperature | Colour |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fluorine | F₂ | Gas | Pale yellow |
| Chlorine | Cl₂ | Gas | Green |
| Bromine | Br₂ | Liquid | Red-brown (orange in solution) |
| Iodine | I₂ | Solid | Dark grey (purple vapour; brown in solution) |

With metals, halogens form ionic compounds containing halide ions with a −1 charge (sodium chloride, Na⁺Cl⁻). With non-metals, they form covalent compounds (hydrogen chloride, HCl).

**Why reactivity decreases down the group.** A halogen reacts by gaining one electron. Further down, the outer shell is further from the nucleus and more shielded, so an incoming electron is less strongly attracted. It is gained less easily.

**Displacement.**

| | Potassium chloride solution | Potassium bromide solution | Potassium iodide solution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chlorine water | No reaction | Turns orange (bromine made) | Turns brown (iodine made) |
| Bromine water | No reaction | No reaction | Turns brown (iodine made) |
| Iodine solution | No reaction | No reaction | No reaction |

Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂

(HT only) Ionic equation: Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying chlorine has "Cl" as its formula. Halogens are diatomic: Cl₂.
- Saying Group 7 reactivity increases down the group. It decreases; Group 1 increases.

### 4.1.3.1 Comparison with Group 1 elements (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- The transition elements are metals with similar properties, which are different from those of the elements in Group 1.
- You should be able to describe the difference compared with Group 1 in melting points, densities, strength, hardness and reactivity with oxygen, water and halogens.

## 4.8 Chemical analysis

Chemists test substances to find out what they are and how pure they are. This topic covers purity, formulations, chromatography and gas tests, then (Chemistry only) tests for ions and instrumental methods.

### 4.8.1.1 Pure substances

#### What you need to know

- In chemistry, a pure substance is a single element or compound, not mixed with any other substance.
- Pure elements and compounds melt and boil at specific temperatures. Melting point and boiling point data can be used to distinguish pure substances from mixtures.
- In everyday language, a pure substance can mean a substance that has had nothing added to it, so it is unadulterated and in its natural state, for example pure milk.
- You should be able to use melting point and boiling point data to distinguish pure from impure substances.

#### Explained

| | Pure substance | Mixture (impure) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Melting | At one sharp temperature | Over a range of temperatures, usually lower |
| Boiling | At one sharp temperature | Over a range, usually higher |

Pure water melts at exactly 0 °C and boils at exactly 100 °C (at normal pressure). Salt water freezes below 0 °C and boils above 100 °C.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling orange juice with "no added sugar" pure in a chemistry answer. Chemically, it is a mixture.

### 4.8.1.2 Formulations

#### What you need to know

- A formulation is a mixture that has been designed as a useful product. Many products are complex mixtures in which each chemical has a particular purpose.
- Formulations are made by mixing the components in carefully measured quantities to ensure that the product has the required properties.
- Formulations include fuels, cleaning agents, paints, medicines, alloys, fertilisers and foods.
- You should be able to identify formulations given appropriate information.

#### Explained

A paint is a formulation: a pigment gives colour, a binder holds the pigment to the surface, and a solvent makes it spread. A medicine tablet contains the active drug plus fillers, a coating and flavourings, each in a set amount.

### 4.8.1.3 Chromatography

#### What you need to know

- Chromatography can be used to separate mixtures and can give information to help identify substances.
- Chromatography involves a stationary phase and a mobile phase. Separation depends on the distribution of substances between the phases.
- The ratio of the distance moved by a compound (centre of spot from origin) to the distance moved by the solvent can be expressed as its Rf value.
- Different compounds have different Rf values in different solvents, which can be used to help identify the compounds.
- The compounds in a mixture may separate into different spots depending on the solvent, but a pure compound will produce a single spot in all solvents.
- You should be able to explain how paper chromatography separates mixtures, suggest how chromatographic methods can be used for distinguishing pure substances from impure substances, interpret chromatograms and determine Rf values from chromatograms.

```latex
R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by substance}}{\text{distance moved by solvent}}
```

#### Explained

In paper chromatography, the stationary phase is the paper and the mobile phase is the solvent. A substance that is more soluble in the solvent, and less attracted to the paper, moves further. Rf values are always between 0 and 1 and have no units.

#### Worked example

A spot moves 3.6 cm. The solvent front moves 8.0 cm. Rf = 3.6 ÷ 8.0 = 0.45.

#### Practical

**Required practical: investigate how paper chromatography can be used to separate and tell the difference between coloured substances, and calculate Rf values.**

1. Draw a start line in pencil about 2 cm from the bottom of the chromatography paper.
2. Put a small, concentrated spot of each ink on the line, with known dyes for comparison.
3. Stand the paper in a beaker with solvent below the start line. Put a lid on the beaker.
4. Leave until the solvent has nearly reached the top. Remove the paper and mark the solvent front in pencil.
5. Let it dry. Measure the distance from the start line to the centre of each spot, and to the solvent front.
6. Calculate the Rf value of each spot, and compare with the known dyes.

Why pencil: graphite does not dissolve in the solvent, so the line does not run. Why the solvent is below the line: otherwise the spots would dissolve into the solvent in the beaker. Why a lid: stops the solvent evaporating.

#### Common mistakes

- Measuring to the top of the spot. Measure to the centre.
- Drawing the start line in pen.

### 4.8.2.1 Test for hydrogen

#### What you need to know

The test for hydrogen uses a burning splint held at the open end of a test tube of the gas. Hydrogen burns rapidly with a pop sound.

### 4.8.2.2 Test for oxygen

#### What you need to know

The test for oxygen uses a glowing splint inserted into a test tube of the gas. The splint relights in oxygen.

### 4.8.2.3 Test for carbon dioxide

#### What you need to know

The test for carbon dioxide uses an aqueous solution of calcium hydroxide (lime water). When carbon dioxide is shaken with or bubbled through limewater, the limewater turns milky (cloudy).

### 4.8.2.4 Test for chlorine

#### What you need to know

The test for chlorine uses litmus paper. When damp litmus paper is put into chlorine gas, the litmus paper is bleached and turns white.

#### Explained (gas tests)

| Gas | Test | Positive result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrogen | Lit (burning) splint | Squeaky pop |
| Oxygen | Glowing splint | Relights |
| Carbon dioxide | Bubble through limewater | Turns milky / cloudy |
| Chlorine | Damp litmus paper | Bleached white (blue litmus may turn red first) |

#### Common mistakes

- Mixing up the splints: hydrogen needs a burning splint; oxygen needs a glowing one.
- Using dry litmus paper for chlorine. It must be damp.

### 4.8.3.1 Flame tests (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Flame tests can be used to identify some metal ions (cations). Lithium, sodium, potassium, calcium and copper compounds produce distinctive colours in flame tests:
  - lithium compounds result in a crimson flame
  - sodium compounds result in a yellow flame
  - potassium compounds result in a lilac flame
  - calcium compounds result in an orange-red flame
  - copper compounds result in a green flame.
- If a sample containing a mixture of ions is used, some flame colours can be masked.

#### Explained

Method: clean a nichrome wire loop by dipping it in dilute hydrochloric acid and holding it in a blue Bunsen flame until it gives no colour. Dip it in the sample and hold it in the edge of the blue flame. Note the colour.

The yellow sodium flame is very bright and can hide other colours in a mixture.

### 4.8.3.2 Metal hydroxides (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Sodium hydroxide solution can be used to identify some metal ions (cations).
- Solutions of aluminium, calcium and magnesium ions form white precipitates when sodium hydroxide solution is added, but only the aluminium hydroxide precipitate dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide solution.
- Solutions of copper(II), iron(II) and iron(III) ions form coloured precipitates when sodium hydroxide solution is added:
  - copper(II) forms a blue precipitate
  - iron(II) a green precipitate
  - iron(III) a brown precipitate.
- You should be able to write balanced equations for the reactions to produce the insoluble hydroxides.

#### Explained

| Metal ion | Precipitate with a few drops of NaOH | With excess NaOH |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aluminium, Al³⁺ | White | Dissolves (colourless solution) |
| Calcium, Ca²⁺ | White | Stays |
| Magnesium, Mg²⁺ | White | Stays |
| Copper(II), Cu²⁺ | Blue | Stays |
| Iron(II), Fe²⁺ | Green | Stays |
| Iron(III), Fe³⁺ | Brown | Stays |

Calcium and magnesium both give a white precipitate that stays. Tell them apart with a flame test: calcium gives orange-red; magnesium gives no colour.

Equations:

- CuSO₄(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s) + Na₂SO₄(aq)
- Ionic: Cu²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s)
- Fe³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₃(s)

### 4.8.3.3 Carbonates (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

Carbonates react with dilute acids to form carbon dioxide gas. Carbon dioxide can be identified with limewater.

### 4.8.3.4 Halides (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

Halide ions in solution produce precipitates with silver nitrate solution in the presence of dilute nitric acid:

- silver chloride is white
- silver bromide is cream
- silver iodide is yellow.

#### Explained

Dilute nitric acid is added first to remove carbonate ions, which would also form a precipitate with silver nitrate. Hydrochloric acid cannot be used, because it contains chloride ions.

### 4.8.3.5 Sulfates (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

Sulfate ions in solution produce a white precipitate with barium chloride solution in the presence of dilute hydrochloric acid.

#### Explained

Dilute hydrochloric acid is added first to remove carbonate ions, which would also give a white precipitate. Sulfuric acid cannot be used, because it contains sulfate ions.

Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)

#### Practical

**Required practical (Chemistry only): use of chemical tests to identify the ions in unknown single ionic compounds, covering the ions from 4.8.3.1 to 4.8.3.5.**

1. Dissolve a small amount of the unknown solid in water.
2. Test for the metal ion: a flame test, then sodium hydroxide solution.
3. Test separate samples for the negative ion: dilute acid and limewater (carbonate); dilute nitric acid and silver nitrate (halide); dilute hydrochloric acid and barium chloride (sulfate).
4. Combine the results to name the compound. Example: lilac flame and a cream precipitate with silver nitrate means potassium bromide.

### 4.8.3.6 Instrumental methods (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Elements and compounds can be detected and identified using instrumental methods. Instrumental methods are accurate, sensitive and rapid.
- You should be able to state advantages of instrumental methods compared with the chemical tests in this specification.

#### Explained

| Advantage | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Accurate | Gives the correct identity |
| Sensitive | Works on very small samples |
| Rapid | Gives results quickly, and can be automated |

Disadvantages: the instruments are expensive and need trained operators.

### 4.8.3.7 Flame emission spectroscopy (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Flame emission spectroscopy is an example of an instrumental method used to analyse metal ions in solutions.
- The sample is put into a flame and the light given out is passed through a spectroscope. The output is a line spectrum that can be analysed to identify the metal ions in the solution and measure their concentrations.
- You should be able to interpret an instrumental result given appropriate data in chart or tabular form, when accompanied by a reference set in the same form.

#### Explained

Each metal ion gives a unique pattern of lines, like a fingerprint. Compare the lines from the sample with reference spectra: if all of a metal's lines appear in the sample's spectrum, that metal is present. The brightness of the lines shows the concentration. Unlike a flame test, it works on mixtures.

## 4.3 Quantitative chemistry

Chemists measure how much of each substance reacts and is made. This topic covers conservation of mass, relative formula mass, moles (HT), concentration, yield and atom economy (Chemistry only), and gas volumes (Chemistry only, HT).

### 4.3.1.1 Conservation of mass and balanced chemical equations

#### What you need to know

- The law of conservation of mass states that no atoms are lost or made during a chemical reaction, so the mass of the products equals the mass of the reactants.
- This means that chemical reactions can be represented by symbol equations, which are balanced in terms of the numbers of atoms of each element involved on both sides of the equation.
- You should understand the use of the multipliers in equations in normal script before a formula and in subscript within a formula.

#### Explained

In 2H₂O, the big 2 in front means two molecules of water; the small 2 means each molecule has two hydrogen atoms. So 2H₂O contains 4 hydrogen atoms and 2 oxygen atoms.

#### Worked example

24 g of magnesium burns completely in oxygen to make 40 g of magnesium oxide. What mass of oxygen reacted?

Mass of reactants = mass of products, so 24 g + mass of oxygen = 40 g. Oxygen = 16 g.

### 4.3.1.2 Relative formula mass

#### What you need to know

- The relative formula mass (Mr) of a compound is the sum of the relative atomic masses of the atoms in the numbers shown in the formula.
- In a balanced chemical equation, the sum of the relative formula masses of the reactants in the quantities shown equals the sum of the relative formula masses of the products in the quantities shown.
- You should be able to calculate the percentage by mass in a compound given the relative formula mass and the relative atomic masses.

#### Worked example

Calculate the Mr of calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂. (Ar: Ca = 40, O = 16, H = 1)

40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 40 + 34 = 74.

Calculate the percentage by mass of oxygen in Ca(OH)₂.

```latex
\%\ \text{by mass} = \frac{A_r \times \text{number of atoms}}{M_r} \times 100
```

(16 × 2) ÷ 74 × 100 = 43.2%.

#### Common mistakes

- Multiplying only the oxygen by 2 in Ca(OH)₂. The bracket means the whole OH is doubled.

### 4.3.1.3 Mass changes when a reactant or product is a gas

#### What you need to know

- Some reactions may appear to involve a change in mass, but this can usually be explained because a reactant or product is a gas and its mass has not been taken into account.
  - When a metal reacts with oxygen, the mass of the oxide produced is greater than the mass of the metal.
  - In thermal decompositions of metal carbonates, carbon dioxide is produced and escapes into the atmosphere, leaving the metal oxide as the only solid product.
- You should be able to explain any observed changes in mass in non-enclosed systems during a chemical reaction, given the balanced symbol equation, and explain these changes in terms of the particle model.

#### Explained

| Reaction | Apparent change | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Magnesium burned in an open crucible: 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s) | Mass increases | Oxygen atoms from the air are now part of the solid |
| Copper carbonate heated: CuCO₃(s) → CuO(s) + CO₂(g) | Mass decreases | Carbon dioxide gas escapes into the air |

In a sealed container, the mass stays the same in both reactions.

### 4.3.1.4 Chemical measurements

#### What you need to know

- Whenever a measurement is made there is always some uncertainty about the result obtained.
- You should be able to represent the distribution of results and make estimations of uncertainty, and use the range of a set of measurements about the mean as a measure of uncertainty.

#### Worked example

A student measures a temperature rise four times: 12.4, 12.8, 12.2 and 12.6 °C.

1. Mean = (12.4 + 12.8 + 12.2 + 12.6) ÷ 4 = 12.5 °C.
2. Range = 12.8 − 12.2 = 0.6 °C.
3. Uncertainty = half the range = ±0.3 °C.

Result: 12.5 ± 0.3 °C.

### 4.3.2.1 Moles (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Chemical amounts are measured in moles. The symbol for the unit mole is mol.
- The mass of one mole of a substance in grams is numerically equal to its relative formula mass.
- One mole of a substance contains the same number of the stated particles (atoms, molecules or ions) as one mole of any other substance.
- The number of atoms, molecules or ions in a mole of a given substance is the Avogadro constant. The value of the Avogadro constant is 6.02 × 10²³ per mole.
- You should be able to use the relative formula mass of a substance to calculate the number of moles in a given mass of that substance, and vice versa.

```latex
\text{moles} = \frac{\text{mass (g)}}{M_r}
```

#### Worked example

How many moles are in 88 g of carbon dioxide, CO₂? (Ar: C = 12, O = 16)

1. Mr = 12 + 2 × 16 = 44.
2. Moles = 88 ÷ 44 = 2 mol.

How many molecules is that? 2 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.204 × 10²⁴ molecules.

### 4.3.2.2 Amounts of substances in equations (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- The masses of reactants and products can be calculated from balanced symbol equations.
- Chemical equations can be interpreted in terms of moles. For example, Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ shows that 1 mol of magnesium reacts with 2 mol of hydrochloric acid to produce 1 mol of magnesium chloride and 1 mol of hydrogen gas.
- You should be able to calculate the masses of substances shown in a balanced symbol equation, and calculate the masses of reactants and products from the balanced symbol equation and the mass of a given reactant or product.

#### Worked example

What mass of calcium oxide is made by heating 50 g of calcium carbonate? CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (Ar: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16)

1. Mr of CaCO₃ = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100. Moles = 50 ÷ 100 = 0.5 mol.
2. The ratio CaCO₃ : CaO is 1 : 1, so 0.5 mol of CaO.
3. Mr of CaO = 56. Mass = 0.5 × 56 = 28 g.

The method: mass → moles → use the ratio from the equation → moles → mass.

### 4.3.2.3 Using moles to balance equations (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- The balancing numbers in a symbol equation can be calculated from the masses of reactants and products by converting the masses in grams to amounts in moles and converting the numbers of moles to simple whole number ratios.
- You should be able to balance an equation given the masses of reactants and products.

#### Worked example

8.0 g of methane (CH₄) reacts with 32.0 g of oxygen (O₂) to give 22.0 g of carbon dioxide (CO₂) and 18.0 g of water (H₂O). Balance the equation. (Mr: CH₄ = 16, O₂ = 32, CO₂ = 44, H₂O = 18)

1. Moles: CH₄ 8 ÷ 16 = 0.5; O₂ 32 ÷ 32 = 1; CO₂ 22 ÷ 44 = 0.5; H₂O 18 ÷ 18 = 1.
2. Divide by the smallest (0.5): 1 : 2 : 1 : 2.
3. CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.

### 4.3.2.4 Limiting reactants (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- In a chemical reaction involving two reactants, it is common to use an excess of one of the reactants to ensure that all of the other reactant is used. The reactant that is completely used up is called the limiting reactant because it limits the amount of products.
- You should be able to explain the effect of a limiting quantity of a reactant on the amount of products it is possible to obtain in terms of amounts in moles or masses in grams.

#### Worked example

4.8 g of magnesium reacts with 0.5 mol of hydrochloric acid. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. Which is the limiting reactant? (Ar: Mg = 24)

1. Moles of Mg = 4.8 ÷ 24 = 0.2 mol.
2. 0.2 mol of Mg needs 0.4 mol of HCl. There is 0.5 mol, so HCl is in excess.
3. Magnesium is the limiting reactant. It will make 0.2 mol of hydrogen.

### 4.3.2.5 Concentration of solutions

#### What you need to know

- Many chemical reactions take place in solutions. The concentration of a solution can be measured in mass per given volume of solution, for example grams per dm³ (g/dm³).
- You should be able to calculate the mass of solute in a given volume of solution of known concentration in terms of mass per given volume of solution.
- (HT only) You should be able to explain how the mass of a solute and the volume of a solution is related to the concentration of the solution.

```latex
\text{concentration (g/dm}^3\text{)} = \frac{\text{mass of solute (g)}}{\text{volume of solution (dm}^3\text{)}}
```

1 dm³ = 1000 cm³, so divide cm³ by 1000 to get dm³.

#### Worked example

25 g of sodium chloride is dissolved to make 500 cm³ of solution. Calculate the concentration.

1. 500 cm³ = 0.5 dm³.
2. 25 ÷ 0.5 = 50 g/dm³.

(HT only) More solute in the same volume gives a higher concentration; the same solute in a bigger volume gives a lower concentration.

#### Common mistakes

- Forgetting to convert cm³ to dm³.

### 4.3.3.1 Percentage yield (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Even though no atoms are gained or lost in a chemical reaction, it is not always possible to obtain the calculated amount of a product because:
  - the reaction may not go to completion because it is reversible
  - some of the product may be lost when it is separated from the reaction mixture
  - some of the reactants may react in ways different to the expected reaction.
- The amount of a product obtained is known as the yield. When compared with the maximum theoretical amount as a percentage, it is called the percentage yield.
- You should be able to calculate the percentage yield of a product from the actual yield of a reaction.
- (HT only) You should be able to calculate the theoretical amount of a product from a given amount of reactant and the balanced equation for the reaction.

```latex
\%\ \text{yield} = \frac{\text{mass of product actually made}}{\text{maximum theoretical mass of product}} \times 100
```

#### Worked example

The theoretical yield of a product is 28 g. A student makes 21 g. Percentage yield = 21 ÷ 28 × 100 = 75%.

(HT only) If the theoretical mass is not given, work it out first using the mole method in 4.3.2.2.

### 4.3.3.2 Atom economy (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- The atom economy (atom utilisation) is a measure of the amount of starting materials that end up as useful products. It is important for sustainable development and for economic reasons to use reactions with high atom economy.
- You should be able to calculate the atom economy of a reaction to form a desired product from the balanced equation.
- (HT only) You should be able to explain why a particular reaction pathway is chosen to produce a specified product, given appropriate data such as atom economy (if not calculated), yield, rate, equilibrium position and usefulness of by-products.

```latex
\text{atom economy} = \frac{M_r \text{ of desired product from the equation}}{\text{sum of } M_r \text{ of all reactants from the equation}} \times 100
```

#### Worked example

Calculate the atom economy for making calcium oxide: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (Mr: CaCO₃ = 100, CaO = 56)

56 ÷ 100 × 100 = 56%.

A reaction with only one product has an atom economy of 100%.

(HT only) Choosing a route: a chemist prefers a high atom economy (less waste), a high yield, a fast rate, an equilibrium that favours the product, and by-products that can be sold or used.

#### Common mistakes

- Confusing yield (what you actually got) with atom economy (what the equation allows to end up in the useful product).

### 4.3.4 Using concentrations of solutions in mol/dm³ (Chemistry only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- The concentration of a solution can be measured in mol/dm³.
- The amount in moles of solute or the mass in grams of solute in a given volume of solution can be calculated from its concentration in mol/dm³.
- If the volumes of two solutions that react completely are known, and the concentration of one solution is known, the concentration of the other solution can be calculated.
- You should be able to explain how the concentration of a solution in mol/dm³ is related to the mass of the solute and the volume of the solution.

```latex
\text{concentration (mol/dm}^3\text{)} = \frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{volume (dm}^3\text{)}}
```

To convert mol/dm³ to g/dm³, multiply by the Mr.

#### Worked example

25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution is neutralised by 20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid. NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O. Calculate the concentration of the sodium hydroxide in mol/dm³ and in g/dm³. (Mr of NaOH = 40)

1. Moles of HCl = 0.100 × (20.0 ÷ 1000) = 0.00200 mol.
2. Ratio 1 : 1, so moles of NaOH = 0.00200 mol.
3. Concentration of NaOH = 0.00200 ÷ (25.0 ÷ 1000) = 0.0800 mol/dm³.
4. In g/dm³: 0.0800 × 40 = 3.20 g/dm³.

### 4.3.5 Use of amount of substance in relation to volumes of gases (Chemistry only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Equal amounts in moles of gases occupy the same volume under the same conditions of temperature and pressure.
- The volume of one mole of any gas at room temperature and pressure (20 °C and 1 atmosphere pressure) is 24 dm³.
- The volumes of gaseous reactants and products can be calculated from the balanced equation for the reaction.
- You should be able to calculate the volume of a gas at room temperature and pressure from its mass and relative formula mass, calculate volumes of gaseous reactants and products from a balanced equation and a given volume of a gaseous reactant or product, and change the subject of the equation.

```latex
\text{volume of gas (dm}^3\text{)} = \text{moles} \times 24
```

#### Worked example

What volume of hydrogen is made when 2.4 g of magnesium reacts with excess acid at room temperature and pressure? Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ (Ar: Mg = 24)

1. Moles of Mg = 2.4 ÷ 24 = 0.1 mol.
2. Ratio Mg : H₂ = 1 : 1, so 0.1 mol of H₂.
3. Volume = 0.1 × 24 = 2.4 dm³.

Gas reacting volumes: 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(g). 100 cm³ of hydrogen reacts with 50 cm³ of oxygen, because the volumes are in the same ratio as the moles.

1. 12.0 g of carbon burns in oxygen to make 44.0 g of carbon dioxide. Calculate the mass of oxygen used. [1 mark]
2. Calculate the Mr of magnesium nitrate, Mg(NO₃)₂. (Ar: Mg = 24, N = 14, O = 16) [2 marks]
3. Calculate the percentage by mass of nitrogen in ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃. (Ar: N = 14, H = 1, O = 16) [2 marks]

## 4.7 Organic chemistry

Carbon forms millions of compounds. This topic covers crude oil, alkanes, fractional distillation, combustion and cracking, then (Chemistry only) alkenes, alcohols, carboxylic acids, esters and polymers.

### 4.7.1.1 Crude oil, hydrocarbons and alkanes

#### What you need to know

- Crude oil is a finite resource found in rocks. Crude oil is the remains of an ancient biomass consisting mainly of plankton that was buried in mud.
- Crude oil is a mixture of a very large number of compounds. Most of the compounds in crude oil are hydrocarbons, which are molecules made up of hydrogen and carbon atoms only.
- Most of the hydrocarbons in crude oil are hydrocarbons called alkanes. The general formula for the homologous series of alkanes is CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.
- The first four members of the alkanes are methane, ethane, propane and butane.
- Alkane molecules can be represented in the following forms: C₂H₆, or as a displayed formula showing every bond.
- You should be able to recognise substances as alkanes given their formulae in these forms.

#### Explained

| Alkane | Formula | Carbon atoms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Methane | CH₄ | 1 |
| Ethane | C₂H₆ | 2 |
| Propane | C₃H₈ | 3 |
| Butane | C₄H₁₀ | 4 |

Remember the order: "Monkeys Eat Peanut Butter" (meth-, eth-, prop-, but-).

Alkanes are saturated: every carbon–carbon bond is a single bond, so the molecule holds as many hydrogen atoms as possible.

A homologous series is a family of compounds with the same general formula, similar chemical properties, and a trend in physical properties. Each member differs from the next by CH₂.

#### Worked example

What is the formula of the alkane with 8 carbon atoms? n = 8, so H = 2 × 8 + 2 = 18. Answer: C₈H₁₈.

### 4.7.1.2 Fractional distillation and petrochemicals

#### What you need to know

- The many hydrocarbons in crude oil may be separated into fractions, each of which contains molecules with a similar number of carbon atoms, by fractional distillation.
- The fractions can be processed to produce fuels and feedstock for the petrochemical industry.
- Many of the fuels on which we depend for our modern lifestyle, such as petrol, diesel oil, kerosene, heavy fuel oil and liquefied petroleum gases, are produced from crude oil.
- Many useful materials on which modern life depends are produced by the petrochemical industry, such as solvents, lubricants, polymers and detergents.
- The vast array of natural and synthetic carbon compounds occur due to the ability of carbon atoms to form families of similar compounds.
- You should be able to explain how fractional distillation works in terms of evaporation and condensation.

#### Explained

1. Crude oil is heated until most of it evaporates.
2. The vapour enters a fractionating column, which is hot at the bottom and cooler at the top.
3. The vapour rises. Each hydrocarbon condenses when it reaches the height where the temperature is below its boiling point.
4. Fractions are collected at different heights. Very large molecules with high boiling points do not evaporate and are collected at the bottom as bitumen.

| Fraction (top to bottom) | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Liquefied petroleum gases (LPG) | Camping gas, bottled gas |
| Petrol | Car fuel |
| Kerosene | Aircraft fuel |
| Diesel oil | Fuel for lorries, trains, some cars |
| Heavy fuel oil | Ships, power stations |
| Bitumen | Roads and roofs |

### 4.7.1.3 Properties of hydrocarbons

#### What you need to know

- Some properties of hydrocarbons depend on the size of their molecules, including boiling point, viscosity and flammability. These properties influence how hydrocarbons are used as fuels.
- The combustion of hydrocarbon fuels releases energy. During combustion, the carbon and hydrogen in the fuels are oxidised. The complete combustion of a hydrocarbon produces carbon dioxide and water.
- You should be able to recall how boiling point, viscosity and flammability change with increasing molecular size, and write balanced equations for the complete combustion of hydrocarbons with a given formula.

#### Explained

| As the molecules get bigger | Boiling point | Viscosity (thickness) | Flammability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | Higher | Higher (thicker, flows less easily) | Lower (harder to ignite) |

#### Worked example

Balance the complete combustion of propane: C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.

1. 3 carbons, so 3CO₂.
2. 8 hydrogens, so 4H₂O.
3. Oxygen on the right: 3 × 2 + 4 = 10 atoms, so 5O₂.

C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O.

### 4.7.1.4 Cracking and alkenes

#### What you need to know

- Hydrocarbons can be broken down (cracked) to produce smaller, more useful molecules.
- Cracking can be done by various methods including catalytic cracking and steam cracking.
- You should be able to describe in general terms the conditions used for catalytic cracking and steam cracking.
- The products of cracking include alkanes and another type of hydrocarbon called alkenes.
- Alkenes are more reactive than alkanes and react with bromine water, which is used as a test for alkenes.
- You should be able to recall the colour change when bromine water reacts with an alkene.
- There is a high demand for fuels with small molecules and so some of the products of cracking are useful as fuels.
- Alkenes are used to produce polymers and as starting materials for the production of many other chemicals.
- You should be able to balance chemical equations as examples of cracking given the formulae of the reactants and products, and give examples to illustrate the usefulness of cracking. You should be able to explain how modern life depends on the uses of hydrocarbons.

#### Explained

| Method | Conditions |
| --- | --- |
| Catalytic cracking | Heat the long hydrocarbons to a vapour and pass them over a hot powdered catalyst |
| Steam cracking | Mix the hydrocarbon vapour with steam and heat to a very high temperature |

Why crack: crude oil contains more long-chain hydrocarbons than are needed, and not enough short ones for petrol. Cracking turns surplus long molecules into shorter, more useful fuels, plus alkenes for making plastics.

Test for alkenes: add bromine water and shake. An alkene turns it from orange to colourless. An alkane leaves it orange.

#### Worked example

Complete: C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + ?

Carbon: 10 − 8 = 2. Hydrogen: 22 − 18 = 4. The missing product is C₂H₄ (ethene).

### 4.7.2.1 Structure and formulae of alkenes (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Alkenes are hydrocarbons with a double carbon–carbon bond. The general formula for the homologous series of alkenes is CₙH₂ₙ.
- Alkene molecules are unsaturated because they contain two fewer hydrogen atoms than the alkane with the same number of carbon atoms.
- The first four members of the homologous series of alkenes are ethene, propene, butene and pentene.
- Alkene molecules can be represented in the following forms: C₃H₆, or as a displayed formula showing the C=C bond.
- You do not need to know the names of individual isomers.

#### Explained

| Alkene | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| Ethene | C₂H₄ |
| Propene | C₃H₆ |
| Butene | C₄H₈ |
| Pentene | C₅H₁₀ |

There is no "methene": an alkene needs at least two carbons to have a C=C bond.

### 4.7.2.2 Reactions of alkenes (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Alkenes are hydrocarbons with the functional group C=C. It is the generality of reactions of functional groups that determine the reactions of organic compounds.
- Alkenes react with oxygen in combustion reactions in the same way as other hydrocarbons, but they tend to burn in air with smoky flames because of incomplete combustion.
- Alkenes react with hydrogen, water and the halogens, by the addition of atoms across the carbon–carbon double bond so that the double bond becomes a single carbon–carbon bond.
- You should be able to describe the reactions and conditions for the addition of hydrogen, water and halogens to alkenes, and draw fully displayed structural formulae of the first four members of the alkenes and the products of their addition reactions with hydrogen, water, chlorine, bromine and iodine.

#### Explained

| Adding | Conditions | Product | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrogen (hydrogenation) | About 60 °C, nickel catalyst | Alkane | Ethene + hydrogen → ethane |
| Water (steam, hydration) | High temperature, catalyst (phosphoric acid) | Alcohol | Ethene + steam → ethanol |
| A halogen | Room temperature | Dihaloalkane | Ethene + bromine → dibromoethane |

In each case the double bond opens, and one atom or group joins each of the two carbons.

### 4.7.2.3 Alcohols (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Alcohols contain the functional group –OH.
- Methanol, ethanol, propanol and butanol are the first four members of a homologous series of alcohols.
- Alcohols can be represented in the following forms: CH₃CH₂OH, or as a displayed formula.
- You should be able to describe what happens when any of the first four alcohols react with sodium, burn in air, are added to water, and react with an oxidising agent; and recall the main uses of these alcohols.
- Aqueous solutions of ethanol are produced when sugar solutions are fermented using yeast.
- You should know the conditions used for fermentation of sugar using yeast.

#### Explained

| Alcohol | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| Methanol | CH₃OH |
| Ethanol | C₂H₅OH (CH₃CH₂OH) |
| Propanol | C₃H₇OH |
| Butanol | C₄H₉OH |

| Reaction | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| With sodium | Fizzes, producing hydrogen |
| Burning in air | Burns cleanly, producing carbon dioxide and water |
| In water | Dissolves to give a neutral solution (the smaller alcohols mix completely) |
| With an oxidising agent | Oxidised to a carboxylic acid (ethanol becomes ethanoic acid) |

Uses: as fuels (ethanol in some car fuels), as solvents (in perfumes and cleaning products), and ethanol in alcoholic drinks.

**Fermentation.** Sugar (glucose) → ethanol + carbon dioxide, using yeast. Conditions: a warm temperature (about 30 °C), no oxygen (anaerobic), and yeast. Too hot and the yeast enzymes are denatured; with oxygen, the yeast respires aerobically and no ethanol forms.

### 4.7.2.4 Carboxylic acids (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Carboxylic acids have the functional group –COOH.
- The first four members of a homologous series of carboxylic acids are methanoic acid, ethanoic acid, propanoic acid and butanoic acid.
- You should be able to describe what happens when any of the first four carboxylic acids react with carbonates, dissolve in water, and react with alcohols.

(HT only) Carboxylic acids are weak: they only partly ionise in water, so they release fewer H⁺ ions than a strong acid of the same concentration, and have a higher pH.

### 4.7.3.1 Addition polymerisation (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Alkenes can be used to make polymers such as poly(ethene) and poly(propene) by addition polymerisation.
- In addition polymerisation reactions, many small molecules (monomers) join together to form very large molecules (polymers).
- In addition polymers the repeating unit has the same atoms as the monomer because no other molecule is formed in the reaction.
- You should be able to recognise addition polymers and monomers from diagrams in the forms shown, and from the presence of the functional group C=C in the monomers; draw diagrams to represent the formation of a polymer from a given alkene monomer; and relate the repeating unit to the monomer.

#### Explained

### 4.7.3.2 Condensation polymerisation (Chemistry only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Condensation polymerisation involves monomers with two functional groups. When these types of monomers react, they join together, usually losing small molecules such as water, and so the reactions are called condensation reactions.
- The simplest polymers are produced from two different monomers with two of the same functional groups on each monomer. For example, ethane diol and hexanedioic acid polymerise to produce a polyester.
- You should be able to explain the basic principles of condensation polymerisation by reference to the functional groups in the monomers and the repeating units in the polymers.

#### Explained

| | Addition | Condensation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monomers | One type, with a C=C | Usually two types, each with two functional groups |
| Other product | None | A small molecule, usually water |
| Repeating unit | Same atoms as the monomer | Fewer atoms than the monomers combined |

A diol (two –OH groups) and a dicarboxylic acid (two –COOH groups) link through ester bonds, losing one water molecule at each link, to make a polyester.

### 4.7.3.3 Amino acids (Chemistry only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Amino acids have two different functional groups in a molecule. Amino acids react by condensation polymerisation to produce polypeptides.
- For example, glycine (H₂NCH₂COOH) polymerises to produce the polypeptide –(HNCH₂COO)ₙ– + n H₂O.
- Different amino acids can be combined in the same chain to produce proteins.

#### Explained

Each amino acid has an amine group (–NH₂) and a carboxylic acid group (–COOH). The amine group of one reacts with the acid group of the next, losing water. Because one molecule has both groups, a single type of monomer can form a condensation polymer.

### 4.7.3.4 DNA and other naturally occurring polymers (Chemistry only)

- Most DNA molecules are two polymer chains, made from four different monomers called nucleotides, in the form of a double helix.
- Other naturally occurring polymers important for life include proteins, starch and cellulose.
- You should be able to name the types of monomers from which these naturally occurring polymers are made.

| Polymer | Monomer |

## 4.4 Chemical changes

Metals, acids and electricity drive many useful reactions. This topic covers the reactivity series, extracting metals, oxidation and reduction, reactions of acids, making salts, pH, titrations (Chemistry only), strong and weak acids (HT), and electrolysis.

### 4.4.1.1 Metal oxides

#### What you need to know

- Metals react with oxygen to produce metal oxides. The reactions are oxidation reactions because the metals gain oxygen.
- You should be able to explain reduction and oxidation in terms of loss or gain of oxygen.

#### Explained

| Term | In terms of oxygen |
| --- | --- |
| Oxidation | Gain of oxygen |
| Reduction | Loss of oxygen |

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO: magnesium is oxidised.

### 4.4.1.2 The reactivity series

#### What you need to know

- When metals react with other substances the metal atoms form positive ions. The reactivity of a metal is related to its tendency to form positive ions.
- Metals can be arranged in order of their reactivity in a reactivity series. The metals potassium, sodium, lithium, calcium, magnesium, zinc, iron and copper can be put in order of their reactivity from their reactions with water and dilute acids.
- The non-metals hydrogen and carbon are often included in the reactivity series.
- A more reactive metal can displace a less reactive metal from a compound.
- You should be able to recall and describe the reactions, if any, of potassium, sodium, lithium, calcium, magnesium, zinc, iron and copper with water or dilute acids, and where appropriate place them in order of reactivity; explain how the reactivity of metals with water or dilute acids is related to the tendency of the metal to form its positive ion; and deduce an order of reactivity of metals based on experimental results.

#### Explained

Most reactive at the top:

| Metal | With water | With dilute acid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Potassium | Violent; melts, lilac flame | Explosive |
| Sodium | Vigorous; melts into a ball | Explosive |
| Lithium | Steady fizzing | Very vigorous |
| Calcium | Fizzes steadily | Vigorous |
| Magnesium | Very slow with cold water | Fast fizzing |
| (Carbon) | | |
| Zinc | Very slow (with steam) | Steady fizzing |
| Iron | Very slow (rusts) | Slow fizzing |
| (Hydrogen) | | |
| Copper | No reaction | No reaction |

Metals above hydrogen react with dilute acids to make hydrogen. Copper is below hydrogen, so it does not.

**Displacement.** Magnesium is more reactive than copper, so it displaces copper from copper sulfate solution: Mg + CuSO₄ → MgSO₄ + Cu. The blue colour fades, and brown copper forms on the magnesium.

### 4.4.1.3 Extraction of metals and reduction

#### What you need to know

- Unreactive metals such as gold are found in the Earth as the metal itself, but most metals are found as compounds that require chemical reactions to extract the metal.
- Metals less reactive than carbon can be extracted from their oxides by reduction with carbon.
- Reduction involves the loss of oxygen.
- Knowledge and understanding are limited to the reduction of oxides using carbon. Knowledge of the details of processes used in the extraction of metals is not required.
- You should be able to interpret or evaluate specific metal extraction processes when given appropriate information, and identify the substances which are oxidised or reduced in terms of gain or loss of oxygen.

#### Explained

| Position in the reactivity series | Extraction method |
| --- | --- |
| More reactive than carbon (potassium to aluminium) | Electrolysis of the molten compound |
| Less reactive than carbon (zinc, iron, copper) | Heating the oxide with carbon |
| Very unreactive (gold) | Found as the pure metal |

2Fe₂O₃ + 3C → 4Fe + 3CO₂. Iron oxide loses oxygen, so it is reduced. Carbon gains oxygen, so it is oxidised.

### 4.4.1.4 Oxidation and reduction in terms of electrons (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Oxidation is the loss of electrons and reduction is the gain of electrons.
- You should be able to write ionic equations for displacement reactions, and identify in a given reaction, symbol equation or half equation which species are oxidised and which are reduced.

#### Explained

OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).

Displacement: Mg + CuSO₄ → MgSO₄ + Cu.

- Ionic equation (sulfate is a spectator ion): Mg + Cu²⁺ → Mg²⁺ + Cu.
- Half equations: Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ (magnesium is oxidised); Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (copper ions are reduced).

### 4.4.2.1 Reactions of acids with metals

#### What you need to know

- Acids react with some metals to produce salts and hydrogen.
- Knowledge of reactions limited to those of magnesium, zinc and iron with hydrochloric and sulfuric acids.
- (HT only) You should be able to explain in terms of gain or loss of electrons that these are redox reactions, and identify which species are oxidised and which are reduced in given chemical equations.

#### Explained

metal + acid → salt + hydrogen

- Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂
- Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂

(HT only) Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ (oxidised); 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ (hydrogen ions reduced).

### 4.4.2.2 Neutralisation of acids and salt production

#### What you need to know

- Acids are neutralised by alkalis (soluble metal hydroxides) and bases (insoluble metal hydroxides and metal oxides) to produce salts and water, and by metal carbonates to produce salts, water and carbon dioxide.
- The particular salt produced in any reaction between an acid and a base or alkali depends on the acid used (hydrochloric acid produces chlorides, nitric acid produces nitrates, sulfuric acid produces sulfates) and the positive ions in the base, alkali or carbonate.
- You should be able to predict products from given reactants, use the formulae of common ions to deduce the formulae of salts, and write balanced chemical equations.

#### Explained

| Reaction | Products | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acid + metal hydroxide | Salt + water | HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O |
| Acid + metal oxide | Salt + water | H₂SO₄ + CuO → CuSO₄ + H₂O |
| Acid + metal carbonate | Salt + water + carbon dioxide | 2HCl + CaCO₃ → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ |

| Acid | Salt name | Ion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrochloric, HCl | Chloride | Cl⁻ |
| Sulfuric, H₂SO₄ | Sulfate | SO₄²⁻ |
| Nitric, HNO₃ | Nitrate | NO₃⁻ |

The salt's first name comes from the metal: copper oxide + sulfuric acid → copper sulfate.

### 4.4.2.3 Soluble salts

#### What you need to know

- Soluble salts can be made from acids by reacting them with solid insoluble substances, such as metals, metal oxides, hydroxides or carbonates. The solid is added to the acid until no more reacts and the excess solid is filtered off to produce a solution of the salt.
- Salt solutions can be crystallised to produce solid salts.
- You should be able to describe how to make pure, dry samples of named soluble salts from information provided.

#### Practical

**Required practical: preparation of a pure, dry sample of a soluble salt from an insoluble oxide or carbonate.**

Making copper sulfate crystals from copper oxide and sulfuric acid:

1. Warm dilute sulfuric acid in a beaker (this speeds up the reaction).
2. Add copper oxide a spatula at a time, stirring, until no more dissolves. The black solid left over shows the acid is used up and the oxide is in excess.
3. Filter to remove the excess copper oxide. The filtrate is copper sulfate solution.
4. Heat the solution in an evaporating basin to evaporate some of the water, until crystals start to form.
5. Leave it to cool and crystallise.
6. Filter off the crystals and dry them between filter paper.

Why excess solid: so all the acid reacts, leaving no acid in the salt. Why not boil it dry: the crystals may decompose and would not be as large or pure.

### 4.4.2.4 The pH scale and neutralisation

#### What you need to know

- Acids produce hydrogen ions (H⁺) in aqueous solutions.
- Aqueous solutions of alkalis contain hydroxide ions (OH⁻).
- The pH scale, from 0 to 14, is a measure of the acidity or alkalinity of a solution, and can be measured using universal indicator or a pH probe.
- A solution with pH 7 is neutral. Aqueous solutions of acids have pH values of less than 7 and aqueous solutions of alkalis have pH values greater than 7.
- In neutralisation reactions between an acid and an alkali, hydrogen ions react with hydroxide ions to produce water. This reaction can be represented by the equation: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).
- You should be able to describe the use of universal indicator or a wide range indicator to measure the approximate pH of a solution, and use the pH scale to identify acidic or alkaline solutions.

#### Explained

| pH | Universal indicator colour | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–2 | Red | Stomach acid, hydrochloric acid |
| 3–6 | Orange to yellow | Vinegar, lemon juice |
| 7 | Green | Pure water |
| 8–11 | Blue | Soap, baking soda |
| 12–14 | Purple | Sodium hydroxide, bleach |

A pH probe gives a more precise numerical reading than universal indicator.

### 4.4.2.5 Titrations (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- The volumes of acid and alkali solutions that react with each other can be measured by titration using a suitable indicator.
- You should be able to describe how to carry out titrations using strong acids and strong alkalis only (sulfuric, hydrochloric and nitric acids only) to find the reacting volumes accurately.
- (HT only) You should be able to calculate the chemical quantities in titrations involving concentrations in mol/dm³ and in g/dm³.

#### Practical

**Required practical (Chemistry only): determination of the reacting volumes of solutions of a strong acid and a strong alkali by titration.**

1. Use a pipette and pipette filler to put exactly 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution into a conical flask.
2. Add a few drops of an indicator such as phenolphthalein (pink in alkali, colourless in acid) or methyl orange (yellow in alkali, red in acid).
3. Fill a burette with hydrochloric acid and record the starting volume.
4. Add acid from the burette while swirling the flask. Near the end point, add it drop by drop.
5. Stop when the indicator just changes colour. Record the final volume.
6. Repeat until you have concordant results (within 0.10 cm³ of each other) and calculate the mean.

Accuracy: stand the flask on a white tile to see the colour change clearly; read the burette at eye level from the bottom of the meniscus; rinse the burette with the acid first.

(HT only) The calculation is in 4.3.4.

### 4.4.2.6 Strong and weak acids (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- A strong acid is completely ionised in aqueous solution. Examples of strong acids are hydrochloric, nitric and sulfuric acids.
- A weak acid is only partially ionised in aqueous solution. Examples of weak acids are ethanoic, citric and carbonic acids.
- For a given concentration of aqueous solutions, the stronger an acid, the lower the pH.
- As the pH decreases by one unit, the hydrogen ion concentration of the solution increases by a factor of 10.
- You should be able to use and explain the terms dilute and concentrated (in terms of amount of substance), and weak and strong (in terms of the degree of ionisation) in relation to acids, and describe neutrality and relative acidity in terms of the effect on hydrogen ion concentration and the numerical value of pH.

#### Explained

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Strong | Fully ionised: every molecule releases H⁺ |
| Weak | Partly ionised: only some molecules release H⁺ |
| Concentrated | A lot of acid dissolved in a given volume |
| Dilute | A little acid dissolved in a given volume |

You can have a dilute strong acid or a concentrated weak acid. Strength is about ionisation; concentration is about amount.

pH 2 has 10 times the H⁺ concentration of pH 3, and 100 times that of pH 4.

#### Common mistakes

- Using "strong" to mean "concentrated".

### 4.4.3.1 The process of electrolysis

#### What you need to know

- When an ionic compound is melted or dissolved in water, the ions are free to move about within the liquid or solution. These liquids and solutions are able to conduct electricity and are called electrolytes.
- Passing an electric current through electrolytes causes the ions to move to the electrodes. Positively charged ions move to the negative electrode (the cathode), and negatively charged ions move to the positive electrode (the anode). Ions are discharged at the electrodes producing elements. This process is called electrolysis.
- (HT only) Throughout this section, you should be able to write half equations for the reactions occurring at the electrodes during electrolysis.

#### Explained

Remember PANIC: Positive is Anode, Negative Is Cathode. Positive ions (cations) go to the cathode; negative ions (anions) go to the anode.

### 4.4.3.2 Electrolysis of molten ionic compounds

#### What you need to know

- When a simple ionic compound (for example lead bromide) is electrolysed in the molten state using inert electrodes, the metal (lead) is produced at the cathode and the non-metal (bromine) is produced at the anode.
- You should be able to predict the products of the electrolysis of binary ionic compounds in the molten state.

#### Explained

Molten lead bromide (PbBr₂): grey lead forms at the cathode; brown bromine vapour forms at the anode.

(HT only) Cathode: Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb. Anode: 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻.

### 4.4.3.3 Using electrolysis to extract metals

#### What you need to know

- Metals can be extracted from molten compounds using electrolysis. Electrolysis is used if the metal is too reactive to be extracted by reduction with carbon or if the metal reacts with carbon.
- Large amounts of energy are used in the extraction process to melt the compounds and to produce the electrical current.
- Aluminium is manufactured by the electrolysis of a molten mixture of aluminium oxide and cryolite using carbon as the positive electrode (anode).
- You should be able to explain why a mixture is used as the electrolyte, and why the positive electrode must be continually replaced.

#### Explained

- Why cryolite: aluminium oxide has a very high melting point (over 2000 °C). Dissolving it in molten cryolite lowers the melting point, which saves energy and money.
- At the cathode: aluminium forms. (HT) Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al.
- At the anode: oxygen forms. (HT) 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻.
- Why the anodes are replaced: the oxygen reacts with the hot carbon anodes to make carbon dioxide, so they burn away.

### 4.4.3.4 Electrolysis of aqueous solutions

#### What you need to know

- The ions discharged when an aqueous solution is electrolysed using inert electrodes depend on the relative reactivity of the elements involved.
- At the negative electrode (cathode), hydrogen is produced if the metal is more reactive than hydrogen.
- At the positive electrode (anode), oxygen is produced unless the solution contains halide ions, when the halogen is produced.
- This happens because in the aqueous solution water molecules break down, producing hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions that are discharged.
- You should be able to predict the products of the electrolysis of aqueous solutions containing a single ionic compound.

#### Explained

| Solution | Cathode product | Anode product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sodium chloride | Hydrogen (sodium is more reactive than hydrogen) | Chlorine (halide present) |
| Copper sulfate | Copper (copper is less reactive than hydrogen) | Oxygen (no halide) |
| Sulfuric acid | Hydrogen | Oxygen |
| Potassium bromide | Hydrogen | Bromine |

Electrolysis of sodium chloride solution also leaves sodium hydroxide in solution.

#### Practical

**Required practical: investigate what happens when aqueous solutions are electrolysed using inert electrodes.**

1. Pour the solution into a beaker and add two inert electrodes (graphite or platinum), not touching.
2. Connect them to a low-voltage DC supply.
3. Observe each electrode: bubbles show a gas; a coating shows a metal.
4. Test gases: hydrogen gives a squeaky pop with a lit splint; oxygen relights a glowing splint; chlorine bleaches damp litmus paper.

Do this in a well-ventilated room, because chlorine is toxic. Test a hypothesis such as "a metal forms at the cathode only if it is less reactive than hydrogen".

- At the anode (positive electrode), negatively charged ions lose electrons, so the reactions are oxidations.

| Anode | Cl⁻ | 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ | Oxidation |
| Anode | OH⁻ | 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ | Oxidation |

## 4.5 Energy changes

Reactions transfer energy to or from their surroundings. This topic covers exothermic and endothermic reactions, reaction profiles, bond energy calculations (HT), and chemical and fuel cells (Chemistry only).

### 4.5.1.1 Energy transfer during exothermic and endothermic reactions

#### What you need to know

- Energy is conserved in chemical reactions. The amount of energy in the universe at the end of a chemical reaction is the same as before the reaction takes place. If a reaction transfers energy to the surroundings, the product molecules must have less energy than the reactants, by the amount transferred.
- An exothermic reaction is one that transfers energy to the surroundings so the temperature of the surroundings increases. Exothermic reactions include combustion, many oxidation reactions and neutralisation. Everyday uses of exothermic reactions include self-heating cans and hand warmers.
- An endothermic reaction is one that takes in energy from the surroundings so the temperature of the surroundings decreases. Endothermic reactions include thermal decompositions and the reaction of citric acid and sodium hydrogencarbonate. Some sports injury packs are based on endothermic reactions.
- You should be able to distinguish between exothermic and endothermic reactions on the basis of the temperature change of the surroundings, and evaluate uses and applications of exothermic and endothermic reactions given appropriate information.

#### Explained

| | Exothermic | Endothermic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Energy | Transferred to the surroundings | Taken in from the surroundings |
| Temperature of the surroundings | Rises | Falls |
| Examples | Combustion; neutralisation; oxidation; respiration | Thermal decomposition; citric acid + sodium hydrogencarbonate; photosynthesis |
| Uses | Hand warmers; self-heating cans | Instant cold packs for sports injuries |

Limitations of hand warmers and cold packs: many can be used only once; some reusable hand warmers need to be reset by heating.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying an endothermic reaction "gives out cold". It takes in energy, so the surroundings cool.

#### Practical

**Required practical: investigate the variables that affect temperature changes in reacting solutions such as acid plus metals, acid plus carbonates, neutralisations, and displacement of metals.**

Example: the effect of the volume of sodium hydroxide on the temperature change when it reacts with hydrochloric acid.

1. Put 30 cm³ of dilute hydrochloric acid in a polystyrene cup inside a beaker, which holds it steady.
2. Measure the starting temperature.
3. Add 5 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution, put on a lid, stir, and record the highest temperature reached.
4. Repeat, adding 5 cm³ at a time up to 40 cm³.
5. Plot the highest temperature against the volume of sodium hydroxide added.

The polystyrene cup and lid reduce energy transferred to the surroundings, so the result is more accurate. The temperature rises until the acid is neutralised, then falls as more cool alkali is added; the peak shows the volume needed for neutralisation.

### 4.5.1.2 Reaction profiles

#### What you need to know

- Chemical reactions can occur only when reacting particles collide with each other and with sufficient energy. The minimum amount of energy that particles must have to react is called the activation energy.
- Reaction profiles can be used to show the relative energies of reactants and products, the activation energy and the overall energy change of a reaction.
- You should be able to draw simple reaction profiles (energy level diagrams) for exothermic and endothermic reactions showing the relative energies of reactants and products, the activation energy and the overall energy change, with a curved line to show the energy as the reaction proceeds; and use reaction profiles to identify reactions as exothermic or endothermic.

#### Explained

A reaction profile has energy on the y-axis and the progress of the reaction on the x-axis.

| | Exothermic profile | Endothermic profile |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Products | Lower than reactants | Higher than reactants |
| Overall energy change | Arrow pointing down (energy released) | Arrow pointing up (energy taken in) |
| Activation energy | Arrow from the reactants up to the peak of the curve | Arrow from the reactants up to the peak of the curve |

#### Common mistakes

- Drawing the activation energy arrow from the products. It always starts at the reactants.

### 4.5.1.3 The energy change of reactions (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- During a chemical reaction, energy must be supplied to break bonds in the reactants, and energy is released when bonds in the products are formed.
- The difference between the sum of the energy needed to break bonds in the reactants and the sum of the energy released when bonds in the products are formed is the overall energy change of the reaction.
- In an exothermic reaction, the energy released from forming new bonds is greater than the energy needed to break existing bonds.
- In an endothermic reaction, the energy needed to break existing bonds is greater than the energy released from forming new bonds.
- You should be able to calculate the energy transferred in chemical reactions using bond energies supplied.

```latex
\text{energy change} = \text{energy to break bonds} - \text{energy released making bonds}
```

A negative answer means the reaction is exothermic. A positive answer means it is endothermic.

#### Worked example

Calculate the energy change for: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl. Bond energies (kJ/mol): H–H = 436, Cl–Cl = 243, H–Cl = 432.

1. Bonds broken: 1 H–H + 1 Cl–Cl = 436 + 243 = 679 kJ/mol.
2. Bonds made: 2 H–Cl = 2 × 432 = 864 kJ/mol.
3. Energy change = 679 − 864 = −185 kJ/mol.

The answer is negative, so the reaction is exothermic.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying breaking bonds releases energy. Breaking bonds always needs energy; making bonds always releases it.
- Forgetting to multiply by the number of each bond (2 H–Cl here).

### 4.5.2.1 Cells and batteries (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Cells contain chemicals which react to produce electricity.
- The voltage produced by a cell is dependent upon a number of factors, including the type of electrode and electrolyte.
- A simple cell can be made by connecting two different metals in contact with an electrolyte.
- Batteries consist of two or more cells connected together in series to provide a greater voltage.
- In non-rechargeable cells and batteries the chemical reactions stop when one of the reactants has been used up. Alkaline batteries are non-rechargeable.
- Rechargeable cells and batteries can be recharged because the chemical reactions are reversed when an external electrical current is supplied.
- You should be able to interpret data for relative reactivity of different metals and evaluate the use of cells.

#### Explained

The bigger the difference in reactivity between the two metals, the bigger the voltage. A zinc and copper cell gives a higher voltage than an iron and copper cell, because zinc is more reactive than iron.

### 4.5.2.2 Fuel cells (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Fuel cells are supplied by an external source of fuel (for example hydrogen) and oxygen or air. The fuel is oxidised electrochemically within the fuel cell to produce a potential difference.
- The overall reaction in a hydrogen fuel cell involves the oxidation of hydrogen to produce water.
- Hydrogen fuel cells offer a potential alternative to rechargeable cells and batteries.
- You should be able to evaluate the use of hydrogen fuel cells in comparison with rechargeable cells and batteries.
- (HT only) You should be able to write the half equations for the electrode reactions in the hydrogen fuel cell.

#### Explained

Overall: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

(HT only) With an acid electrolyte:

- At the negative electrode: 2H₂ → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ (hydrogen is oxidised).
- At the positive electrode: O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O (oxygen is reduced).

| Hydrogen fuel cells | Rechargeable batteries |
| --- | --- |
| Only product is water, so no pollution where used | Contain toxic metal compounds that must be disposed of carefully |
| Do not need recharging; keep working while fuel is supplied | Need recharging, which takes time |
| Can be refuelled quickly | Wear out after many recharges |
| Hydrogen is a flammable gas that is hard to store | Easy to store |
| Making hydrogen often uses fossil fuels or electricity | Charging electricity may come from fossil fuels |

## 4.6 The rate and extent of chemical change

Some reactions are fast and some slow; some go both ways. This topic covers measuring rate, what changes it, collision theory, catalysts, reversible reactions and equilibrium.

### 4.6.1.1 Calculating rates of reactions

#### What you need to know

- The rate of a chemical reaction can be found by measuring the quantity of a reactant used or the quantity of product formed over time.
- The quantity of reactant or product can be measured by the mass in grams or by a volume in cm³. The units of rate of reaction may be given as g/s or cm³/s.
- (HT only) For the Higher Tier, you are also required to use quantity of reactants in terms of moles and units for rate of reaction in mol/s.
- You should be able to calculate the mean rate of a reaction from given information about the quantity of a reactant used or the quantity of a product formed and the time taken; draw and interpret graphs showing the quantity of product formed or quantity of reactant used up against time; and use the slope of the tangent as a measure of the rate of reaction.
- (HT only) You should be able to calculate the gradient of a tangent to the curve on these graphs as a measure of rate of reaction at a specific time.

```latex
\text{mean rate of reaction} = \frac{\text{quantity of reactant used or product formed}}{\text{time taken}}
```

#### Explained

On a graph of product made against time, the curve is steepest at the start (fastest rate), then gets less steep as reactants are used up, and goes flat when the reaction has finished.

(HT only) To find the rate at one moment: draw a tangent (a straight line touching the curve at that point), then calculate its gradient = change in y ÷ change in x, using a large triangle for accuracy.

#### Worked example

A reaction makes 60 cm³ of gas in 30 seconds. Mean rate = 60 ÷ 30 = 2 cm³/s.

(HT only) A tangent at 20 s passes through (0 s, 10 cm³) and (40 s, 50 cm³). Gradient = (50 − 10) ÷ (40 − 0) = 1 cm³/s.

### 4.6.1.2 Factors which affect the rates of chemical reactions

#### What you need to know

Factors which affect the rates of chemical reactions include:

- the concentrations of reactants in solution
- the pressure of reacting gases
- the surface area of solid reactants
- the temperature
- the presence of catalysts.

You should be able to recall how changing these factors affects the rate of chemical reactions.

#### Practical

**Required practical: investigate how changes in concentration affect the rates of reactions by a method involving measuring the volume of a gas produced and a method involving a change in colour or turbidity.**

Method 1, gas volume: magnesium ribbon and hydrochloric acid.

1. Put a set volume of hydrochloric acid of one concentration in a conical flask.
2. Connect a gas syringe (or an upturned measuring cylinder in water).
3. Add a measured length of magnesium ribbon, seal the flask and start a stopwatch.
4. Record the volume of hydrogen every 10 seconds until it stops increasing.
5. Repeat with different concentrations of acid.

Method 2, turbidity: sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid.

1. Draw a black cross on paper and put a conical flask on it.
2. Add a set volume of sodium thiosulfate solution of one concentration.
3. Add hydrochloric acid, start a stopwatch, and swirl.
4. The reaction makes sulfur, which turns the solution cloudy. Stop timing when you can no longer see the cross from above.
5. Repeat with different concentrations of sodium thiosulfate. Rate = 1 ÷ time.

Variables: the independent variable is concentration; the dependent variable is the volume of gas or the time for the cross to disappear; control variables include temperature, volumes, and the mass or length of magnesium. A limitation of method 2 is that judging when the cross disappears varies between people.

### 4.6.1.3 Collision theory and activation energy

#### What you need to know

- Collision theory explains how various factors affect rates of reactions. According to this theory, chemical reactions can occur only when reacting particles collide with each other and with sufficient energy. The minimum amount of energy that particles must have to react is called the activation energy.
- Increasing the concentration of reactants in solution, the pressure of reacting gases, and the surface area of solid reactants increases the frequency of collisions and so increases the rate of reaction.
- Increasing the temperature increases the frequency of collisions and makes the collisions more energetic, and so increases the rate of reaction.
- You should be able to predict and explain using collision theory the effects of changing conditions of concentration, pressure and temperature on the rate of a reaction; predict and explain the effects of changes in the size of pieces of a reacting solid in terms of surface area to volume ratio; and use simple ideas about proportionality when using collision theory to explain the effect of a factor on the rate of a reaction.

#### Explained

| Change | Effect on particles | Effect on rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Higher concentration | More particles in the same volume, so more frequent collisions | Faster |
| Higher pressure (gases) | Particles closer together, so more frequent collisions | Faster |
| Larger surface area (smaller pieces, powder) | More particles exposed, so more frequent collisions | Faster |
| Higher temperature | Particles move faster: more frequent collisions, and more collisions have at least the activation energy | Faster |
| Catalyst | Lowers the activation energy, so more collisions are successful | Faster |

Proportionality: doubling the concentration roughly doubles the frequency of collisions, so it roughly doubles the rate.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying "more collisions". Say "more frequent collisions" (more collisions per second).
- For temperature, giving only the frequency. The key point is more collisions have energy equal to or above the activation energy.

### 4.6.1.4 Catalysts

#### What you need to know

- Catalysts change the rate of chemical reactions but are not used up during the reaction. Different reactions need different catalysts. Enzymes act as catalysts in biological systems.
- Catalysts increase the rate of reaction by providing a different pathway for the reaction that has a lower activation energy.
- A reaction profile for a catalysed reaction can be drawn in the form of an energy level diagram.
- You should be able to identify catalysts in reactions from their effect on the rate of reaction and because they are not included in the chemical equation for the reaction, and explain catalytic action in terms of activation energy.

#### Explained

On a reaction profile, a catalysed reaction has a lower peak than the uncatalysed reaction. The reactants and products stay at the same energy, so the overall energy change does not change.

### 4.6.2.1 Reversible reactions

#### What you need to know

- In some chemical reactions, the products of the reaction can react to produce the original reactants. Such reactions are called reversible reactions and are represented: A + B ⇌ C + D.
- The direction of reversible reactions can be changed by changing the conditions. For example: ammonium chloride ⇌ ammonia + hydrogen chloride. Heating drives the forward reaction; cooling drives the reverse.

### 4.6.2.2 Energy changes and reversible reactions

#### What you need to know

- If a reversible reaction is exothermic in one direction, it is endothermic in the opposite direction. The same amount of energy is transferred in each case.
- For example: hydrated copper sulfate (blue) ⇌ anhydrous copper sulfate (white) + water. The forward reaction is endothermic; the reverse is exothermic.

#### Explained

Heat blue hydrated copper sulfate and it turns white as water is driven off. Add water to the white powder and it turns blue again and gets hot. This is used as a test for water.

### 4.6.2.3 Equilibrium

#### What you need to know

- When a reversible reaction occurs in apparatus which prevents the escape of reactants and products, equilibrium is reached when the forward and reverse reactions occur at exactly the same rate.

#### Explained

At equilibrium, the reactions have not stopped. Both are still happening, at the same rate, so the amounts of reactants and products stay constant. This is called dynamic equilibrium. It can only happen in a closed system.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the reaction stops at equilibrium.
- Saying the amounts of reactants and products are equal at equilibrium. They are constant, not necessarily equal.

### 4.6.2.4 The effect of changing conditions on equilibrium (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- The relative amounts of all the reactants and products at equilibrium depend on the conditions of the reaction.
- If a system is at equilibrium and a change is made to any of the conditions, then the system responds to counteract the change.
- The effects of changing conditions on a system at equilibrium can be predicted using Le Chatelier's Principle.
- You should be able to make qualitative predictions about the effect of changes on systems at equilibrium when given appropriate information.

### 4.6.2.5 The effect of changing concentration (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- If the concentration of one of the reactants or products is changed, the system is no longer at equilibrium and the concentrations of all the substances will change until equilibrium is reached again.
- If the concentration of a reactant is increased, more products will be formed until equilibrium is reached again.
- If the concentration of a product is decreased, more reactants will react until equilibrium is reached again.
- You should be able to interpret appropriate given data to predict the effect of a change in concentration of a reactant or product on given reactions at equilibrium.

### 4.6.2.6 The effect of temperature changes on equilibrium (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- If the temperature of a system at equilibrium is increased, the relative amount of products at equilibrium increases for an endothermic reaction, and the relative amount of products at equilibrium decreases for an exothermic reaction.
- If the temperature of a system at equilibrium is decreased, the relative amount of products at equilibrium increases for an exothermic reaction, and the relative amount of products at equilibrium decreases for an endothermic reaction.
- You should be able to interpret appropriate given data to predict the effect of a change in temperature on given reactions at equilibrium.

### 4.6.2.7 The effect of pressure changes on equilibrium (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- For a gaseous system at equilibrium, an increase in pressure causes the equilibrium position to shift towards the side with the smaller number of molecules, as shown by the symbol equation for that reaction.
- A decrease in pressure causes the equilibrium position to shift towards the side with the larger number of molecules, as shown by the symbol equation for that reaction.
- You should be able to interpret appropriate given data to predict the effect of pressure changes on given reactions at equilibrium.

#### Explained (4.6.2.4 to 4.6.2.7)

The system always shifts to oppose the change.

Example: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g). The forward reaction is exothermic. There are 4 molecules on the left and 2 on the right.

| Change | Equilibrium shifts | Effect on the yield of ammonia |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Add more nitrogen | Right, to use it up | More ammonia |
| Remove ammonia | Right, to replace it | More ammonia |
| Increase temperature | Left (the endothermic direction, to take in the extra energy) | Less ammonia |
| Decrease temperature | Right (the exothermic direction) | More ammonia |
| Increase pressure | Right (fewer molecules) | More ammonia |

A catalyst does not change the position of equilibrium. It only makes equilibrium reached faster.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a catalyst increases the yield.
- Counting atoms instead of molecules when deciding the effect of pressure.

## 4.10 Using resources

Chemistry turns the Earth's resources into useful products, and has to do it sustainably. This topic covers sustainable development, drinking water, waste water, new ways to extract metals (HT), life cycle assessment and recycling, then (Chemistry only) corrosion, alloys, materials, the Haber process and fertilisers.

### 4.10.1.1 Using the Earth's resources and sustainable development

#### What you need to know

- Humans use the Earth's resources to provide warmth, shelter, food and transport.
- Natural resources, supplemented by agriculture, provide food, timber, clothing and fuels.
- Finite resources from the Earth, oceans and atmosphere are processed to provide energy and materials.
- Chemistry plays an important role in improving agricultural and industrial processes to provide new products, and in sustainable development, which is development that meets the needs of current generations without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
- You should be able to state examples of natural products that are supplemented or replaced by agricultural and synthetic products, and distinguish between finite and renewable resources given appropriate information.

#### Explained

| Finite resources (will run out) | Renewable resources (can be replaced) |
| --- | --- |
| Crude oil, coal, natural gas | Timber from managed forests |
| Metal ores | Crops such as cotton |
| | Wind, solar energy |

Examples of natural products replaced by synthetic ones: wool and cotton by polyester; natural rubber by synthetic rubber; wood by plastics in some uses.

### 4.10.1.2 Potable water

#### What you need to know

- Water of appropriate quality is essential for life. For humans, drinking water should have sufficiently low levels of dissolved salts and microbes. Water that is safe to drink is called potable water. Potable water is not pure water in the chemical sense because it contains dissolved substances.
- The methods used to produce potable water depend on available supplies of water and local conditions.
- In the United Kingdom, rain provides water with low levels of dissolved substances (fresh water) that collects in the ground and in lakes and rivers, and most potable water is produced by:
  - choosing an appropriate source of fresh water
  - passing the water through filter beds
  - sterilising.
- Sterilising agents used for potable water include chlorine, ozone or ultraviolet light.
- If supplies of fresh water are limited, desalination of salty water or sea water may be required. Desalination can be done by distillation or by processes that use membranes, such as reverse osmosis. These processes require large amounts of energy.
- You should be able to distinguish between potable water and pure water, describe the differences in treatment of ground water and salty water, and give reasons for the steps used to produce potable water.

#### Explained

| Step | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Filter beds | Remove solid particles such as leaves and grit |
| Sterilising with chlorine, ozone or UV | Kills microbes |

Desalination is expensive because of the energy needed to boil water (distillation) or push it through membranes at high pressure (reverse osmosis). It is used where fresh water is scarce, such as in some Middle Eastern countries.

#### Practical

**Required practical: analysis and purification of water samples from different sources, including pH, dissolved solids and distillation.**

1. Test the pH of each sample with universal indicator or a pH probe.
2. Measure the mass of an empty evaporating basin. Add a measured volume of the sample, heat gently until all the water evaporates, and reweigh. The increase in mass is the dissolved solids.
3. Distil a sample of salt water: heat it in a flask, pass the steam through a condenser, and collect the pure water. Check the distilled water has pH 7 and leaves no solid when evaporated.

### 4.10.1.3 Waste water treatment

#### What you need to know

- Urban lifestyles and industrial processes produce large amounts of waste water that require treatment before being released into the environment. Sewage and agricultural waste water require removal of organic matter and harmful microbes. Industrial waste water may require removal of organic matter and harmful chemicals.
- Sewage treatment includes:
  - screening and grit removal
  - sedimentation to produce sewage sludge and effluent
  - anaerobic digestion of sewage sludge
  - aerobic biological treatment of effluent.
- You should be able to comment on the relative ease of obtaining potable water from waste, ground and salt water.

#### Explained

1. Screening removes large objects and grit.
2. In settlement tanks, heavy solids sink to form sludge; the liquid on top is effluent.
3. Sludge is broken down by anaerobic bacteria (anaerobic digestion), which produces methane that can be burned as fuel, and leftover material can be used as fertiliser.
4. Effluent has air bubbled through it so aerobic bacteria break down the remaining organic matter. It can then be released into rivers.

Ease of getting potable water: ground water is easiest (just filter and sterilise); waste water needs much more treatment; salt water needs desalination, which uses a lot of energy.

### 4.10.1.4 Alternative methods of extracting metals (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- The Earth's resources of metal ores are limited.
- Copper ores are becoming scarce and new ways of extracting copper from low-grade ores include phytomining and bioleaching. These methods avoid traditional mining methods of digging, moving and disposing of large amounts of rock.
- Phytomining uses plants to absorb metal compounds. The plants are harvested and then burned to produce ash that contains metal compounds.
- Bioleaching uses bacteria to produce leachate solutions that contain metal compounds.
- The metal compounds can be processed to obtain the metal. For example, copper can be obtained from solutions of copper compounds by displacement using scrap iron or by electrolysis.
- You should be able to evaluate alternative biological methods of metal extraction, given appropriate information.

#### Explained

| | Phytomining | Bioleaching |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How | Plants grown on low-grade ore absorb copper compounds; they are burned and the ash is processed | Bacteria break down the ore, releasing a solution of copper compounds (leachate) |
| Advantages | Less damage to the landscape than mining; uses low-grade ore; less waste rock | No high temperatures needed; uses low-grade ore |
| Disadvantages | Very slow | Slow; the leachate can be toxic if it escapes |

Getting copper from the solution: Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu (scrap iron is cheap), or electrolysis, which gives very pure copper.

### 4.10.2.1 Life cycle assessment

#### What you need to know

- Life cycle assessments (LCAs) are carried out to assess the environmental impact of products in each of these stages:
  - extracting and processing raw materials
  - manufacturing and packaging
  - use and operation during its lifetime
  - disposal at the end of its useful life, including transport and distribution at each stage.
- Use of water, resources, energy sources and production of some wastes can be fairly easily quantified. Allocating numerical values to pollutant effects is less straightforward and requires value judgements, so LCA is not a purely objective process.
- Selective or abbreviated LCAs can be devised to evaluate a product, but these can be misused to reach pre-determined conclusions, for example in support of claims for advertising purposes.
- You should be able to carry out simple comparative LCAs for shopping bags made from plastic and paper.

#### Explained

| Stage | Plastic bag | Paper bag |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raw materials | Crude oil (finite) | Trees (renewable) |
| Manufacture | Uses less energy and water | Uses more energy and water |
| Use | Strong; can be reused many times | Weaker; usually used once |
| Disposal | Not biodegradable; lasts in landfill for a long time; can be recycled | Biodegradable; easily recycled |

The conclusion depends on how many times each bag is reused and which impacts are weighted most, which is why LCAs need value judgements.

### 4.10.2.2 Ways of reducing the use of resources

#### What you need to know

- The reduction in use, reuse and recycling of materials by end users reduces the use of limited resources, use of energy sources, waste and environmental impacts.
- Metals, glass, building materials, clay ceramics and most plastics are produced from limited raw materials. Much of the energy for the processes comes from limited resources. Obtaining raw materials from the Earth by quarrying and mining causes environmental impacts.
- Some products, such as glass bottles, can be reused. Glass bottles can be crushed and melted to make different glass products. Other products cannot be reused and so are recycled for a different use.
- Metals can be recycled by melting and recasting or reforming into different products. The amount of separation required for recycling depends on the material and the properties required of the final product. For example, some scrap steel can be added to iron from a blast furnace to reduce the amount of iron that needs to be extracted from iron ore.
- You should be able to evaluate ways of reducing the use of limited resources, given appropriate information.

#### Explained

Recycling saves raw materials and usually uses less energy than extracting new material, produces less waste in landfill, and reduces mining. But collecting, transporting and sorting cost energy and money.

### 4.10.3.1 Corrosion and its prevention (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Corrosion is the destruction of materials by chemical reactions with substances in the environment. Rusting is an example of corrosion. Both air and water are necessary for iron to rust.
- Corrosion can be prevented by applying a coating that acts as a barrier, such as greasing, painting or electroplating. Aluminium has an oxide coating that protects the metal from further corrosion.
- Some coatings are reactive and contain a more reactive metal to provide sacrificial protection, such as zinc is used to galvanise iron.
- You should be able to describe experiments and interpret results to show that both air and water are necessary for rusting, and explain sacrificial protection in terms of relative reactivity.

#### Explained

Experiment with three iron nails:

| Tube | Conditions | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Air and water | Rusts |
| B | Boiled water (no dissolved air) with a layer of oil on top | Does not rust |
| C | Dry air, with calcium chloride to absorb water | Does not rust |

Only the nail with both air and water rusts.

**Sacrificial protection.** Zinc is more reactive than iron, so it reacts (oxidises) instead of the iron, even when the coating is scratched. In galvanising, iron is coated with zinc. Blocks of magnesium are bolted to ship hulls for the same reason.

### 4.10.3.2 Alloys as useful materials (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Most metals in everyday use are alloys.
- Bronze is an alloy of copper and tin. Brass is an alloy of copper and zinc.
- Gold used as jewellery is usually an alloy with silver, copper and zinc. The proportion of gold in the alloy is measured in carats. 24 carat is 100% (pure gold), and 18 carat gold is 75% gold.
- Steels are alloys of iron that contain specific amounts of carbon and other metals. High carbon steel is strong but brittle. Low carbon steel is softer and more easily shaped. Steels containing chromium and nickel (stainless steels) are hard and resistant to corrosion.
- Aluminium alloys are low density.
- You should be able to recall a use of each of the alloys specified, and interpret and evaluate the composition and uses of alloys other than those specified given appropriate information.

#### Explained

| Alloy | Made from | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bronze | Copper and tin | Statues, medals, bells |
| Brass | Copper and zinc | Door handles, musical instruments, plumbing fittings |
| Gold alloys | Gold with silver, copper, zinc | Jewellery (harder than pure gold) |
| High carbon steel | Iron with more carbon | Cutting tools |
| Low carbon steel | Iron with less carbon | Car bodies, wire |
| Stainless steel | Iron with chromium and nickel | Cutlery, sinks |
| Aluminium alloys | Aluminium with other metals | Aircraft |

#### Worked example

What percentage of gold is in 9 carat gold? 9 ÷ 24 × 100 = 37.5%.

### 4.10.3.3 Ceramics, polymers and composites (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Most of the glass we use is soda-lime glass, made by heating a mixture of sand, sodium carbonate and limestone. Borosilicate glass, made from sand and boron trioxide, melts at higher temperatures than soda-lime glass.
- Clay ceramics, including pottery and bricks, are made by shaping wet clay and then heating in a furnace.
- The properties of polymers depend on what monomers they are made from and the conditions under which they are made. For example, low density (LD) and high density (HD) poly(ethene) are produced from ethene.
- Thermosoftening polymers melt when they are heated. Thermosetting polymers do not melt when they are heated.
- Most composites are made of two materials, a matrix or binder surrounding and binding together fibres or fragments of the other material, which is called the reinforcement.
- You should be able to explain how low density and high density poly(ethene) are both produced from ethene, explain the difference between thermosoftening and thermosetting polymers in terms of their structures, and compare quantitatively the physical properties of glass and clay ceramics, polymers, composites and metals.

#### Explained

LD and HD poly(ethene): different conditions (temperature, pressure, catalyst) produce different chain structures. LD has branched chains, so it is flexible (carrier bags). HD has straighter chains packed closer together, so it is stronger and more rigid (bottles, pipes).

| | Thermosoftening | Thermosetting |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structure | Separate chains, with weak forces between them | Chains joined by cross-links (covalent bonds) |
| When heated | Softens and melts, so can be reshaped | Does not melt; chars at high temperature |
| Example | Poly(ethene) | Melamine (used in kitchen worktops) |

Composites: reinforced concrete (steel rods in concrete), fibreglass (glass fibres in a polymer), carbon fibre (carbon fibres in a polymer), and wood (cellulose fibres in lignin).

### 4.10.4.1 The Haber process (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- The Haber process is used to manufacture ammonia, which can be used to produce nitrogen-based fertilisers.
- The raw materials for the Haber process are nitrogen and hydrogen. Nitrogen is obtained from the air, and hydrogen may be obtained from natural gas or other sources.
- The purified gases are passed over a catalyst of iron at a high temperature (about 450 °C) and a high pressure (about 200 atmospheres). Some of the hydrogen and nitrogen reacts to form ammonia. The reaction is reversible, so some of the ammonia produced breaks down into nitrogen and hydrogen: nitrogen + hydrogen ⇌ ammonia (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃).
- On cooling, the ammonia liquefies and is removed. The remaining hydrogen and nitrogen are recycled.
- (HT only) You should be able to interpret graphs of reaction conditions versus rate, apply the principles of dynamic equilibrium to the Haber process, explain the trade-off between rate of production and position of equilibrium, and explain how the commercially used conditions relate to the availability and cost of raw materials and energy supplies, control of equilibrium position and rate.

#### Explained

(HT only) The forward reaction is exothermic, and there are 4 gas molecules on the left and 2 on the right.

| Condition | Effect on yield | Effect on rate | Compromise chosen |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | A lower temperature gives a higher yield | A lower temperature makes it too slow | 450 °C: a reasonable yield at a reasonable rate |
| Pressure | A higher pressure gives a higher yield (fewer molecules on the right) | Higher pressure also increases rate | 200 atmospheres: higher pressures cost too much to build and run, and are more dangerous |
| Catalyst (iron) | No effect | Faster | Used, because it saves time and so money |

Recycling the unreacted gases means almost nothing is wasted, even though only a small percentage reacts each time.

### 4.10.4.2 Production and uses of NPK fertilisers (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Compounds of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium are used as fertilisers to improve agricultural productivity. NPK fertilisers contain compounds of all three elements.
- Industrial production of NPK fertilisers can be achieved using a variety of raw materials in several integrated processes. NPK fertilisers are formulations of various salts containing appropriate percentages of the elements.
- Ammonia can be used to manufacture ammonium salts and nitric acid.
- Potassium chloride, potassium sulfate and phosphate rock are obtained by mining, but phosphate rock cannot be used directly as a fertiliser.
- Phosphate rock is treated with nitric acid or sulfuric acid to produce soluble salts that can be used as fertilisers.
- You should be able to recall the names of the salts produced when phosphate rock is treated with nitric acid, sulfuric acid and phosphoric acid, and compare the industrial production of fertilisers with laboratory preparations of the same compounds, given appropriate information.

## 4.9 Chemistry of the atmosphere

The air has changed over billions of years and is still changing. This topic covers the composition of the atmosphere, how it evolved, greenhouse gases and climate change, carbon footprints, and pollutants from burning fuels.

### 4.9.1.1 The proportions of different gases in the atmosphere

#### What you need to know

For 200 million years, the proportions of different gases in the atmosphere have been much the same as they are today:

- about four-fifths (approximately 80%) nitrogen
- about one-fifth (approximately 20%) oxygen
- small proportions of various other gases, including carbon dioxide, water vapour and noble gases.

#### Explained

More precisely, dry air is about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, 0.9% argon and about 0.04% carbon dioxide. In the exam, "about 80% nitrogen and 20% oxygen" is what is expected.

### 4.9.1.2 The Earth's early atmosphere

#### What you need to know

- Theories about what was in the Earth's early atmosphere and how the atmosphere was formed have changed and developed over time. Evidence for the early atmosphere is limited because of the time scale of 4.6 billion years.
- One theory suggests that during the first billion years of the Earth's existence there was intense volcanic activity that released gases that formed the early atmosphere and water vapour that condensed to form the oceans.
- At the start of this period the Earth's atmosphere may have been like the atmospheres of Mars and Venus today, consisting of mainly carbon dioxide with little or no oxygen gas.
- Volcanoes also produced nitrogen, which gradually built up in the atmosphere, and there may have been small proportions of methane and ammonia.
- When the oceans formed, carbon dioxide dissolved in the water and carbonates were precipitated, producing sediments. This reduced the amount of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere.
- You should be able to interpret evidence and evaluate different theories about the Earth's early atmosphere.

#### Explained

Why the evidence is limited: there were no people to record it, rocks from that time have mostly been destroyed or changed, and scientists must work from indirect clues, such as gases trapped in ancient rocks and the atmospheres of other planets.

### 4.9.1.3 How oxygen increased

#### What you need to know

- Algae and plants produced the oxygen that is now in the atmosphere by photosynthesis, which can be represented by: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂).
- Algae first produced oxygen about 2.7 billion years ago and soon after this oxygen appeared in the atmosphere. Over the next billion years plants evolved and the percentage of oxygen gradually increased to a level that enabled animals to evolve.

### 4.9.1.4 How carbon dioxide decreased

#### What you need to know

- Algae and plants decreased the percentage of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere by photosynthesis.
- Carbon dioxide was also decreased by the formation of sedimentary rocks and fossil fuels that contain carbon.
- You should be able to describe the main changes in the atmosphere over time and some of the likely causes of these changes, and describe and explain the formation of deposits of limestone, coal, crude oil and natural gas.

#### Explained

| Carbon store | How it formed |
| --- | --- |
| Limestone | Shells and skeletons of sea creatures, made of calcium carbonate, settled and were compressed into rock |
| Coal | Plant material buried and compressed over millions of years |
| Crude oil and natural gas | Plankton buried in mud, then heated and compressed over millions of years |

Summary of the changes: carbon dioxide fell (dissolving in the oceans, photosynthesis, sedimentary rocks and fossil fuels); oxygen rose (photosynthesis); nitrogen built up (from volcanoes, and it is unreactive so it stayed).

### 4.9.2.1 Greenhouse gases

#### What you need to know

- Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere maintain temperatures on Earth high enough to support life. Water vapour, carbon dioxide and methane are greenhouse gases.
- You should be able to describe the greenhouse effect in terms of the interaction of short and long wavelength radiation with matter.

#### Explained

1. Short wavelength radiation from the Sun passes through the atmosphere and reaches the Earth's surface.
2. The Earth absorbs it and warms up, then radiates energy back out as longer wavelength (infrared) radiation.
3. Greenhouse gases absorb some of this long wavelength radiation and re-emit it in all directions, including back towards the Earth.
4. This keeps the Earth warmer than it would be with no atmosphere.

Without the greenhouse effect, the Earth would be too cold for most life. The problem is an enhanced greenhouse effect from extra greenhouse gases.

### 4.9.2.2 Human activities which contribute to an increase in greenhouse gases in the atmosphere

#### What you need to know

- Some human activities increase the amounts of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere. These include carbon dioxide (from burning fossil fuels and deforestation) and methane (from animal farming, through digestion and waste decomposition, and from decomposition of rubbish in landfill sites).
- Based on peer-reviewed evidence, many scientists believe that human activities will cause the temperature of the Earth's atmosphere to increase at the surface and that this will result in global climate change.
- However, it is difficult to model such complex systems as global climate change. This can lead to simplified models, speculation and opinions presented in the media that may be based on only parts of the evidence and which may be biased.
- You should be able to evaluate the quality of evidence in a report about global climate change given appropriate information, and describe uncertainties in the evidence base.

#### Explained

Evaluating evidence: is it peer reviewed? Is the sample large and from many places over a long time? Who paid for it, and could they be biased? Does a news story use all the evidence or only part of it?

### 4.9.2.3 Global climate change

#### What you need to know

- An increase in average global temperature is a major cause of climate change.
- There are several potential effects of global climate change:
  - sea level rise, which may cause flooding and increased coastal erosion
  - more frequent and severe storms
  - changes in the amount, timing and distribution of rainfall
  - temperature and water stress for humans and wildlife
  - changes in the food producing capacity of some regions
  - changes to the distribution of wildlife species.
- You should be able to describe briefly four potential effects of global climate change, and discuss the scale, risk and environmental implications of global climate change.

### 4.9.2.4 The carbon footprint and its reduction

#### What you need to know

- The carbon footprint is the total amount of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases emitted over the full life cycle of a product, service or event.
- The carbon footprint can be reduced by reducing emissions of carbon dioxide and methane.
- You should be able to describe actions to reduce emissions of carbon dioxide and methane, and give reasons why actions may be limited.

#### Explained

| Action | How it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Alternative energy (wind, solar, nuclear) | Less fossil fuel burned |
| Energy conservation (insulation, efficient appliances) | Less energy needed |
| Carbon capture and storage | CO₂ from power stations is captured and stored underground |
| Carbon taxes and licences | Make emitting more expensive, so less is emitted |
| Carbon offsetting, including tree planting | Trees take in CO₂ by photosynthesis |
| Carbon neutrality | Balancing emissions so there is no net release |

Why actions are limited: scientific disagreement over causes and effects; lack of public information and education; people not wanting to change their lifestyles; economic costs, especially for developing countries; and incomplete international cooperation.

### 4.9.3.1 Atmospheric pollutants from fuels

#### What you need to know

- The combustion of fuels is a major source of atmospheric pollutants.
- Most fuels, including coal, contain carbon and/or hydrogen and may also contain some sulfur.
- The gases released into the atmosphere when a fuel is burned may include carbon dioxide, water vapour, carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen. Solid particles and unburned hydrocarbons may also be released, which form particulates in the atmosphere.
- You should be able to describe how carbon monoxide, soot (carbon particles), sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen are produced by burning fuels, and predict the products of combustion of a fuel given appropriate information about the composition of the fuel and the conditions in which it is used.

#### Explained

| Pollutant | How it forms |
| --- | --- |
| Carbon monoxide (CO) | Incomplete combustion: not enough oxygen to make CO₂ |
| Soot (carbon particles) | Incomplete combustion |
| Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) | Sulfur impurities in the fuel react with oxygen |
| Oxides of nitrogen (NOₓ) | Nitrogen and oxygen from the air react at the high temperatures in engines |

### 4.9.3.2 Properties and effects of atmospheric pollutants

#### What you need to know

- Carbon monoxide is a toxic gas. It is colourless and odourless and so is not easily detected.
- Sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen cause respiratory problems in humans and cause acid rain.
- Particulates cause global dimming and health problems for humans.
- You should be able to describe and explain the problems caused by increased amounts of these pollutants in the air.

#### Explained

| Pollutant | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Carbon monoxide | Binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells, so less oxygen is carried; can cause death; cannot be seen or smelled |
| Sulfur dioxide, oxides of nitrogen | Dissolve in rain to form acid rain, which damages buildings, trees and lakes; cause breathing problems |
| Particulates | Reflect sunlight, so less reaches the Earth (global dimming); damage lungs |

## 3.1.1 Engineering materials and their properties

Engineers choose a material by matching its properties to the job. This file covers the mechanical properties used to describe materials, then the four families in the spec: metals and alloys, polymers, composites, and other materials (timber and ceramics). For each you need to recognise the material from its appearance and properties, know how it behaves when it is handled and machined, and know how its properties can be changed.

### 3.1.1 Materials and their properties

#### What you need to know

- Materials can be identified by their appearance and by their properties.
- The key mechanical properties: toughness, brittleness, ductility, malleability, hardness, strength and stiffness.
- How materials behave when they are handled and when they are machined (cut, drilled, turned, milled).

#### Explained

| Property | Meaning | High in | Low in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strength | How much force a material can take before it breaks or permanently deforms. Tensile strength resists pulling; compressive strength resists squashing | Steel (tension), concrete (compression) | Lead, polystyrene |
| Stiffness | How well it resists bending or stretching under load (it springs back only a tiny amount) | Steel, carbon-fibre composite | Rubber, low-density polythene |
| Hardness | How well it resists scratching, wear and denting | Ceramics, hardened tool steel | Lead, pure aluminium |
| Toughness | How much energy it absorbs before it breaks; it resists cracking under sudden impact | Low carbon steel, nylon, polycarbonate | Glass, cast iron, acrylic |
| Brittleness | Breaks suddenly with little or no bending; the opposite of tough | Cast iron, glass, ceramics, acrylic | Copper, low carbon steel |
| Ductility | Can be pulled out into a thin wire without breaking | Copper, aluminium, gold | Cast iron |
| Malleability | Can be hammered, pressed or rolled into shape without cracking | Lead, copper, aluminium | Cast iron, high carbon steel |

Hardness and toughness are often confused. A hard material resists surface damage; a tough material resists breaking. Many hard materials (glass, a fully hardened file) are brittle, so they are hard but not tough.

Strength and stiffness are also different. A thin steel ruler is strong (hard to snap) but not very stiff in that shape (it bends easily). Stiffness depends on both the material and the shape of the part.

Identifying materials by appearance:

- Metals are shiny when cut or polished, cold to touch, and heavy for their size (apart from aluminium, which is light). A magnet picks up most ferrous metals.
- Copper is pinkish-orange; brass is yellow-gold; bronze is a darker brown-gold; aluminium is light, silver-grey; lead is dull grey, very heavy and soft enough to scratch with a fingernail.
- Thermoplastics are often brightly coloured or clear and can be smooth and glossy.
- Composites often show their structure: woven fibres in carbon fibre and GRP, layers in plywood, flakes in OSB.

Handling and machining behaviour: soft, ductile metals such as aluminium and copper machine easily but can clog cutters; cast iron machines well because the graphite flakes break chips off cleanly; hardened steel is very hard to cut and is usually machined before hardening. Thermoplastics can melt and smear if cutting speeds are too high. Composites can splinter or delaminate at the edges, and carbon-fibre dust is hazardous, so dust extraction and masks are needed.

#### Common mistakes

- Using "strong" for every good property. Say which property you mean: strong in tension, hard, tough or stiff.
- Saying hard means tough. Glass is hard but brittle.
- Forgetting to link the property to the use in a "justify the choice" question. Name the property and say why the product needs it.

### 3.1.1.1 Metals and alloys

#### What you need to know

- Ferrous metals contain iron: cast iron, low carbon steel, high carbon steel, stainless steel.
- Non-ferrous metals contain no iron: aluminium, copper, lead, zinc, and the alloys brass and bronze.
- An alloy is a mixture of a metal with at least one other element.
- Properties can be changed by alloying, by changing grain size through heating, by cold working (work hardening), by hardening and quenching, and by changing the carbon content of steel.
- Corrosion and how it affects metals.

#### Explained

| Metal | Type | Key properties | Typical uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cast iron | Ferrous (about 2 to 4% carbon) | Hard, brittle, strong in compression, casts well, machines well | Engine blocks, machine beds, vices, manhole covers |
| Low carbon (mild) steel | Ferrous (under about 0.3% carbon) | Tough, ductile, malleable, easy to weld, rusts; cannot be hardened much by heat treatment | Car bodies, girders, nuts and bolts, brackets |
| High carbon steel | Ferrous (about 0.7 to 1.4% carbon) | Very hard after heat treatment but more brittle; harder to bend | Cutting tools, chisels, springs, drill bits |
| Stainless steel | Ferrous alloy (iron, chromium, nickel) | Resists corrosion, hard, tough, hard to machine | Sinks, cutlery, surgical tools, food plant |
| Aluminium | Non-ferrous | Light (low density), good conductor, corrosion resistant (forms an oxide layer), ductile, malleable | Aircraft panels, drinks cans, heat sinks, bike frames (as alloys) |
| Copper | Non-ferrous | Excellent electrical and thermal conductor, ductile, malleable, corrosion resistant | Wiring, PCB tracks, pipes, heat exchangers |
| Lead | Non-ferrous | Very dense, very soft and malleable, corrosion resistant, toxic | Roof flashing, radiation shielding, battery plates |
| Zinc | Non-ferrous | Corrosion resistant, low melting point | Coating steel (galvanising), die-cast parts |
| Brass | Non-ferrous alloy (copper and zinc) | Corrosion resistant, machines very well, attractive gold colour, low friction | Plumbing fittings, electrical terminals, screws |
| Bronze | Non-ferrous alloy (copper and tin) | Tough, hard wearing, corrosion resistant (including sea water) | Bearings, bushes, ship propellers, statues |

How properties are changed:

- Alloying. Mixing in other elements changes the properties. Pure metals have regular rows of atoms that slide over each other easily. Atoms of a different size disrupt the rows, so layers slide less and the alloy is harder and stronger. Chromium and nickel in stainless steel also give corrosion resistance.
- Carbon content. More carbon in steel makes it harder and stronger but less ductile and more brittle. Low carbon steel bends; high carbon steel can be hardened to cut other metals.
- Grain size. Metals are made of crystals called grains. Heating and then cooling slowly lets large grains grow, giving a softer, more ductile metal. Faster cooling gives smaller grains, which are generally harder and stronger. (The heat treatments are in file 04.)
- Cold working (work hardening). Bending, hammering or rolling a metal at room temperature distorts and squashes the grains. The metal becomes harder and stronger but more brittle. Bend a piece of copper back and forth and it gets stiffer, then cracks. Annealing reverses this.
- Hardening and quenching. High carbon steel heated to red heat then plunged into water or oil becomes very hard but brittle. Tempering afterwards trades some hardness for toughness.
- Corrosion. Iron and steel rust when exposed to both water and oxygen; the rust flakes off, so corrosion continues and the part weakens. Aluminium, copper and zinc form a thin oxide layer that sticks and protects the metal underneath. Corrosion is prevented by coatings (paint, galvanising, plating), by choosing a resistant metal, or by keeping parts dry and oiled.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling stainless steel non-ferrous. It is mostly iron, so it is ferrous.
- Saying "aluminium is strong" as the reason for using it in aircraft. The key property is low density (light) with a good strength-to-weight ratio when alloyed.
- Saying all ferrous metals are magnetic. Some stainless steels are not.
- Mixing up brass (copper and zinc) and bronze (copper and tin).

### 3.1.1.2 Polymers

#### What you need to know

- Thermoplastics: ABS, acrylic (PMMA), nylon, polycarbonate, polystyrene.
- Thermosetting polymers: epoxy resin, polyester resin, melamine resin (melamine-formaldehyde), polyurethane, vulcanised rubber.
- The effects of heat on thermoplastics and thermosetting polymers.

#### Explained

The difference comes from the structure. In a thermoplastic, long polymer chains are tangled but not joined to each other. Heating gives the chains enough energy to slide apart, so the plastic softens and can be reshaped; cooling sets it again. This can be repeated, so thermoplastics can usually be recycled.

In a thermosetting polymer, the chains are joined to each other by strong cross-links that form when the polymer is first cured (set). Heating cannot break these links without breaking down the material, so a thermoset does not soften. Too much heat makes it char and burn. Thermosets cannot be remoulded, so they are much harder to recycle, but they cope with heat and keep their shape.

| Polymer | Type | Properties | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ABS | Thermoplastic | Tough, impact resistant, good surface finish, easily moulded | Phone cases, appliance housings, 3D printer filament, toy bricks |
| Acrylic (PMMA) | Thermoplastic | Clear like glass but lighter, hard, scratches, brittle, can be laser cut and line bent | Displays, signs, light diffusers, guards |
| Nylon | Thermoplastic | Tough, low friction, wear resistant, self-lubricating | Gears, bearings, bushes, cable ties |
| Polycarbonate | Thermoplastic | Extremely tough and impact resistant, transparent, heat resistant for a thermoplastic | Safety glasses, riot shields, machine guards, headlamp lenses |
| Polystyrene | Thermoplastic | Rigid, light, cheap; expanded form is a good insulator | Packaging, disposable cups, model kits |
| Epoxy resin | Thermoset | Strong adhesive, good electrical insulator, resists chemicals | Adhesives, PCB base board (with glass fibre), matrix for carbon fibre |
| Polyester resin | Thermoset | Rigid, cheap, sets at room temperature with a hardener | Matrix for GRP boat hulls and car panels |
| Melamine resin | Thermoset | Hard, scratch and heat resistant | Laminate worktops, heat-resistant handles, tableware |
| Polyurethane | Thermoset (rigid forms) | Can be a rigid or flexible foam or a tough coating | Insulation foam, varnishes, wheels, seals |
| Vulcanised rubber | Thermoset (cross-linked with sulfur) | Elastic, tough, hard wearing, keeps its shape when warm | Tyres, drive belts, seals, hoses |

Vulcanisation is a good example of how cross-links change properties: raw natural rubber goes sticky when warm and brittle when cold; sulfur cross-links make it elastic and stable over a wide temperature range.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying thermosets "melt at a higher temperature". They do not melt at all; they char.
- Saying thermoplastics are weaker than thermosets as a rule. Polycarbonate and nylon are very tough. The real difference is how they respond to heat.
- Forgetting that a thermoset is set by a chemical curing reaction, usually with a hardener or catalyst, not by cooling.

### 3.1.1.3 Composites

#### What you need to know

- A composite is two or more materials combined so that the result has better properties than either on its own.
- Fibre-reinforced polymers: carbon-fibre reinforced polymer (CFRP) and glass-reinforced plastic (GRP).
- Manufactured boards: plywood, MDF (medium density fibreboard), OSB (oriented strand board).
- Structural (reinforced) concrete.
- Properties can be changed by the direction of the reinforcement, the matrix material, the amount of reinforcement, and the size and shape of the reinforcement.

#### Explained

Most composites have a reinforcement (fibres, strands, layers or bars that carry the load) held in a matrix (the material that binds them and spreads the load between them).

| Composite | Reinforcement | Matrix | Properties | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CFRP | Carbon fibres | Epoxy resin | Very high strength and stiffness for its weight, expensive | Racing car bodies, bike frames, aircraft parts, sports equipment |
| GRP | Glass fibres | Polyester resin | Strong, light, corrosion proof, cheaper than CFRP, can be moulded to complex shapes | Boat hulls, car body panels, water tanks |
| Plywood | Thin wood veneers (plies), grain at right angles in each layer | Adhesive | Strong in all directions along the sheet, resists splitting, stable | Furniture, floors, formwork for concrete |
| MDF | Fine wood fibres | Resin adhesive | Smooth, even, no grain, machines and paints well; swells if wet; weak at screws in edges | Flat-pack furniture, panels, templates |
| OSB | Large wood strands in layers | Resin adhesive | Strong, cheap, rough surface, uses waste timber | Roof and floor decking, wall sheathing |
| Reinforced concrete | Steel bars (rebar) | Concrete | Concrete is strong in compression; the steel adds tensile strength | Beams, floors, foundations, bridges |

How the properties can be changed:

- Direction of reinforcement. Fibres carry load best along their length. All fibres in one direction gives maximum strength in that direction only; woven or layered fibres at different angles give strength in several directions (as in plywood's alternating grain).
- Matrix. Changing the resin changes heat resistance, cost and toughness. Epoxy is stronger and sticks better than polyester but costs more.
- Amount of reinforcement. More fibre (up to a limit) gives a stronger, stiffer part. Too little resin leaves dry fibres that are not bonded.
- Size and shape of reinforcement. Long continuous fibres give higher strength than short chopped fibres; chopped strand mat is cheaper and easier to shape. Larger strands in OSB versus tiny fibres in MDF give different strengths and surface finishes. Ribbed rebar grips the concrete better than smooth bar.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying concrete is strong in tension. It cracks under tension; the steel is there to carry the tension.
- Calling MDF a natural timber. It is a manufactured board, a composite.
- Saying carbon fibre is used because it is cheap. It is chosen for strength-to-weight ratio despite its cost.

### 3.1.1.4 Other materials

#### What you need to know

- Structural timber and its grades.
- Ceramics.

#### Explained

Structural timber is solid wood used to carry loads in buildings, such as joists, rafters and studs. Wood is a natural composite of cellulose fibres in a lignin matrix, so it is strongest along the grain. Every piece is different: knots, splits and the slope of the grain weaken it. So structural timber is strength graded: each piece is checked (by eye or by a machine that measures its stiffness) and given a strength class. In the UK common classes are C16 and C24 for softwoods; the number relates to the bending strength, so C24 can carry more load than C16. Grading lets engineers use calculated loads safely. Timber is renewable, light for its strength and easy to work, but it can rot, burn and change size with moisture.

Ceramics are inorganic, non-metallic materials made by shaping and then firing at high temperature, for example clay products, porcelain, glass and engineering ceramics such as alumina and silicon carbide. Their properties: very hard, wear resistant, strong in compression, resist high temperatures, good electrical and thermal insulators, chemically inert, but brittle (low toughness) and weak in tension. Uses: spark plug insulators, cutting tool tips, brake discs on high-performance cars, furnace linings, circuit-board substrates, tiles.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying timber is equally strong in all directions. It is much stronger along the grain.
- Describing ceramics as tough. They are hard, but they shatter under impact.

## 3.1.2 to 3.1.3 Material costs and supply, and factors influencing design

Choosing a material is not only about its properties. It must also be affordable, available in a usable form, easy to process and sensible at the end of the product's life. This file covers the cost and supply of materials, with production cost calculations, and the wider factors that shape a design: the energy it uses, how long it is meant to last, how it is maintained, tolerances and statistics, and user needs.

### 3.1.2 Material costs and supply

#### What you need to know

- Material choice depends on cost, availability, the form it is supplied in, and how it is supplied.
- Compare the costs of different materials, for example copper against gold for electrical contacts, or timber against steel for a building frame; give at least three examples.
- Calculate production costs.
- Stock forms and sizes; economies of scale and price breaks; reducing waste.
- Machinability, ability to be treated (heat or surface), ease of shaping and recyclability affect the choice.
- Maths: decimals, standard form; equations E1 to E5 (areas and volumes) to work out material quantity.

#### Explained

Cost and availability. A rare material costs more because supply is limited and extraction is harder. Gold is a better contact material than copper (it never tarnishes) but costs tens of thousands of times more per kilogram, so it is used only as a micro-thin plating on connectors where reliability matters. Further comparisons:

| Choice | Cheaper option | Costlier option | Why choose the costlier one |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Electrical contacts | Copper | Gold | Does not oxidise, so the contact stays reliable |
| House frame | Softwood timber | Steel | Longer spans, fire and rot resistance |
| Bike frame | Low carbon steel | Titanium or CFRP | Much lighter for the same strength |
| Boat hull | GRP | Aluminium alloy | Tougher, easier to repair by welding |
| Cutlery | Low carbon steel (plated) | Stainless steel | Corrosion resistance through the whole metal |

Stock forms. Materials are bought in standard forms and sizes, which are cheaper than custom sizes:

- Metals: sheet, plate, bar (round, square, flat, hexagonal), tube, angle, channel and I-section, wire.
- Polymers: sheet, rod, tube, film, granules or pellets (for moulding), filament (for 3D printing).
- Timber and boards: standard planks (planed or sawn) and boards, often 2440 × 1220 mm sheets.
- Composites: woven fibre cloth, chopped strand mat, pre-preg (fibres already soaked in resin), resin in tins.

Designing parts to fit standard sizes saves money: if a bracket can be cut from standard 25 × 3 mm flat bar, no expensive machining is needed to reach a non-standard thickness.

Economies of scale. Buying in bulk lowers the cost of each unit because suppliers offer price breaks (lower unit prices above set quantities), and setup costs of machines and tooling are shared over more products. A mould for injection moulding may cost thousands of pounds, which is not worth it for ten parts but tiny per part for a million.

Waste. Waste costs money twice: you pay for material you throw away, and sometimes you pay to dispose of it. It is reduced by nesting shapes close together on a sheet, choosing stock close to the finished size, using additive processes instead of cutting away material, and recycling offcuts (metal swarf and thermoplastic sprues can be melted and reused).

Processing properties:

- Machinability: how easily it can be cut. Brass and aluminium machine quickly; stainless steel and titanium are slow and wear tools, which adds cost.
- Treatability: whether its properties can be improved by heat treatment or finishing. High carbon steel can be hardened; low carbon steel cannot be hardened much.
- Shaping: whether it can be cast, moulded, bent or pressed. Thermoplastics mould easily; thermosets need curing in the mould; cast iron can be cast but not bent.
- Recyclability: metals and thermoplastics can be recycled; thermosets and many composites are hard to separate and recycle.

#### Worked example

A batch of 200 aluminium spacer plates is made, each 80 mm × 50 mm × 6 mm. Aluminium plate costs £0.012 per cm³. Each plate takes 3 minutes of machine time at £24 per hour. Find the total production cost of the batch.

1. Volume of one plate (E2): 8.0 cm × 5.0 cm × 0.6 cm = 24 cm³.
2. Material cost of one plate: 24 × £0.012 = £0.288.
3. Machine cost per plate: £24 per hour = £0.40 per minute; 3 × £0.40 = £1.20.
4. Cost per plate: £0.288 + £1.20 = £1.488.
5. Batch of 200: 200 × £1.488 = £297.60.

If the supplier gives a price break of 15% off material for orders over 4000 cm³: total volume is 200 × 24 = 4800 cm³, which qualifies. New material cost = 4800 × £0.012 × 0.85 = £48.96 instead of £57.60, saving £8.64.

#### Common mistakes

- Mixing units in volume calculations. Convert all lengths to the same unit (here cm) before multiplying.
- Leaving out labour or machine time when asked for production cost, rather than material cost.
- Saying a material is "cheap" without comparing it. Compare it with a named alternative.

### 3.1.3 Factors influencing the design of solutions

#### What you need to know

- Sources of energy for production and use: wind, solar, tidal, nuclear, fossil fuels and biomass, with their benefits, drawbacks and environmental impacts.
- Engineered lifespans: planned obsolescence, sealed parts, maintenance needs.
- Maintenance: why it is needed (safety, efficiency), types (lubrication, preventing corrosion, allowing for wear), and end of life (disposal, recovering materials).
- Measurement and tolerances, and using statistics to set service intervals and predict lifetime.
- Availability: non-standard forms or scarce materials raise cost.
- User needs, such as strength or low weight, can justify materials such as titanium or carbon-fibre composites.
- Maths: time and cost calculations, ratios and percentages, means, presenting data.

#### Explained

Energy sources. Engineering products need energy to make and to use, and engineers design the generating systems themselves.

| Source | Renewable? | Benefits | Drawbacks and environmental impact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) | No | Reliable, can be turned up and down to meet demand, existing infrastructure | Release carbon dioxide (climate change) and other pollutants; will run out; mining and drilling damage habitats |
| Nuclear | No (uranium is finite) | Huge output, no carbon dioxide in operation, reliable base supply | Radioactive waste lasting thousands of years, very high build and decommissioning cost, risk of accidents |
| Wind | Yes | No fuel cost, no emissions in use, offshore sites are windy | Variable output, visual and noise impact, risk to birds, needs backup |
| Solar | Yes | Can be fitted on roofs close to users, no emissions in use, little maintenance | No output at night, lower in winter, panels use energy and some scarce materials to make |
| Tidal | Yes | Tides are predictable, no emissions in use | Very expensive to build, few suitable sites, affects estuary habitats |
| Biomass | Yes (if replanted) | Can use waste; carbon released was recently absorbed by plants | Needs land that could grow food, releases carbon dioxide and particulates when burned |

Engineered lifespans. Every product is designed to last a certain time.

- Planned obsolescence: designing a product to become out of date or stop working after a set time, so people buy a new one. This can be technological (new features make the old one look dated), or physical (parts that wear out and cannot be replaced). It keeps manufacturers' sales up but creates waste and uses up resources.
- Sealed parts: units glued or welded shut, such as some phone batteries or sealed bearings, cannot be serviced. Sealing can make a product cheaper, smaller and waterproof, but means the whole unit is replaced when one part fails.
- Maintenance requirements: a design that needs no maintenance suits users who will not service it; a machine expected to last decades is designed so that worn parts can be replaced.

Maintenance. Products are maintained to keep them safe (worn brakes, frayed cables), efficient (friction wastes energy) and working for their intended life. Main types:

- Lubrication: oil or grease between moving parts reduces friction, heating and wear.
- Preventing corrosion: repainting, re-oiling, replacing sacrificial coatings.
- Compensating for wear: adjusting chain or belt tension, replacing brake pads, bearings and seals at set intervals.

At end of life a product should be easy to take apart so materials can be recovered: metals sorted and recycled, thermoplastics identified (by their recycling codes) and reprocessed, hazardous parts such as batteries disposed of safely. Designing for disassembly (using screws instead of glue, labelling materials) helps.

Measurement and tolerances. No part can be made to an exact size, so a design states a tolerance: the acceptable range either side of the nominal size, such as 20.0 ± 0.1 mm. Parts that must fit together need tighter tolerances, which cost more to achieve. Statistics are used to plan maintenance: if testing shows a type of bearing lasts a mean of 12 000 hours, with most failing between 10 000 and 14 000 hours, the service interval might be set at 9000 hours so nearly all bearings are replaced before they fail.

Availability and user needs. A material supplied only in non-standard forms, or in short supply, costs more to buy and process. But user needs can justify the cost: titanium is used for aircraft fasteners and medical implants because it is strong, light, corrosion resistant and not rejected by the body; CFRP is used for racing bikes because riders value low weight above price.

#### Worked example

A pump bearing's lifetime was tested on six samples: 9800, 10 400, 11 000, 10 200, 9600 and 10 600 hours.

1. Mean = (9800 + 10 400 + 11 000 + 10 200 + 9600 + 10 600) ÷ 6 = 61 600 ÷ 6 ≈ 10 267 hours.
2. The shortest life was 9600 hours, so a sensible service interval is below this, for example 9000 hours.
3. If the pump runs 18 hours a day: 9000 ÷ 18 = 500 days between services.

A shaft is specified as 25.00 ± 0.05 mm. The largest acceptable diameter is 25.05 mm and the smallest 24.95 mm. A shaft measured at 24.93 mm is rejected.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling nuclear power renewable. Uranium is a finite resource, although nuclear power is low-carbon in operation.
- Saying solar or wind "produce no pollution". They produce no emissions in use, but making them uses energy and materials.
- Setting a service interval at the mean lifetime. Half the parts would fail before then; the interval must be below the shortest expected life.

## 3.2.1 to 3.2.2 Additive manufacturing and material removal

For each process you need to know which materials it suits and how it is carried out. This file covers the two opposite approaches: building a part up layer by layer (additive) and cutting material away from a larger piece (subtractive, or material removal). It includes cutting speed and spindle speed calculations and the angles used when turning a taper.

### 3.2.1 Additive manufacturing

#### What you need to know

- Fused deposition modelling (FDM): building polymer parts by extruding melted filament layer by layer.
- Sintering: fusing powdered material (including metals) with a laser.
- Rapid prototyping with polymers: making quick physical models to test a design.

#### Explained

All additive processes start from a 3D CAD model. Software slices the model into thin horizontal layers and sends instructions for each layer to the machine. The part is built one layer on top of the last.

Fused deposition modelling (FDM):

1. A spool of thermoplastic filament (commonly PLA or ABS) is fed into a heated nozzle.
2. The nozzle melts the filament and moves in X and Y, laying a thin bead of molten plastic in the shape of the layer.
3. The plastic cools and solidifies; the build platform drops (or the nozzle rises) by one layer height, typically 0.1 to 0.3 mm.
4. Overhangs need support structures, printed in the same or a dissolvable material and removed afterwards.

Sintering (selective laser sintering, and direct metal laser sintering for metals):

1. A thin layer of powder (nylon, or a metal such as titanium, stainless steel or aluminium alloy) is spread across the build bed.
2. A laser scans the shape of the layer, heating the powder so that the particles fuse together without fully melting into a pool.
3. The bed lowers, a new layer of powder is spread and the process repeats.
4. Loose powder supports the part as it builds, so complex shapes need few supports. The unused powder is recovered and reused.

Rapid prototyping: making a physical model quickly and cheaply, usually by FDM, so a design can be checked for size, fit, ergonomics and appearance before expensive tooling is made. Problems are found early, when they are cheap to fix.

Advantages of additive manufacturing: very little waste; complex internal shapes (lattices, channels) that cannot be machined or moulded; no tooling, so one-off and custom parts are economical; designs can be changed instantly. Disadvantages: slow for large numbers; parts can be weaker between layers; surface finish shows layer lines; metal machines are very expensive.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a 3D printer "carves" the shape. Additive means adding material, layer by layer.
- Saying sintering melts the powder into liquid. The particles fuse at their surfaces.
- Forgetting that FDM parts are weakest between layers, which matters if the part is loaded.

### 3.2.2 Material removal

#### What you need to know

- Cutting: sawing, shearing, laser cutting. Calculate cutting speeds.
- Turning on a centre lathe: parallel (cylindrical) turning, taper turning, boring. Calculate angles using trigonometry and Pythagoras.
- Milling: face milling and slot milling.
- Drilling: on a pillar drill, and centre drilling on a lathe. Calculate spindle speeds and use order-of-magnitude estimates.
- Chemical etching, including making printed circuit boards (PCBs).

#### Explained

Cutting:

- Sawing: a blade with teeth removes a narrow strip of material (the kerf). Hand saws (hacksaw for metal, tenon saw for wood), band saws and power hacksaws are used. It suits bar, tube and sheet in metals, polymers and wood.
- Shearing: two hardened blades pass each other closely, like scissors, and fracture the material along a line with no kerf and no swarf. Tin snips, bench shears and guillotines shear thin sheet metal. It is fast but can distort the edge and only suits thin sheet.
- Laser cutting: a focused high-power laser beam melts, burns or vaporises material along a path controlled by CNC. It gives a very narrow kerf, accurate, smooth edges and complex shapes. It suits acrylic, wood, card, fabric and (with powerful lasers) sheet metal. PVC must never be laser cut because it releases toxic chlorine-containing fumes.

Turning on a centre lathe. The work is held in a chuck and rotates; a single-point cutting tool on the saddle and cross-slide moves along or across it.

- Parallel turning (cylindrical turning): the tool moves along the axis of the work, parallel to it, to reduce the diameter evenly.
- Facing: the tool moves across the end to make it flat and square.
- Taper turning: the compound slide is set at an angle so the tool moves at an angle to the axis, making a cone. The angle is found with trigonometry.
- Boring: a boring bar enlarges an existing hole to an accurate size and good finish.
- Centre drilling: a short, stiff centre drill in the tailstock chuck makes an accurate starting point so a twist drill does not wander.

Milling. A rotating multi-toothed cutter removes material from work clamped to a moving table.

- Face milling: a wide cutter machines a large flat surface.
- Slot milling: a slot drill or end mill cuts a groove or keyway; a slot drill can plunge straight down into the work.

CNC milling machines and routers follow coordinates from CAD/CAM software, so complex shapes are repeatable.

Drilling. A pillar drill rotates a twist drill held in a chuck; the work is clamped in a machine vice or to the table (never held by hand). The correct spindle speed depends on drill diameter and material: large drills and hard materials need lower speeds.

Chemical etching. A chemical (etchant) dissolves unprotected metal. To make a PCB:

1. A copper-clad board is coated with a light-sensitive resist.
2. It is exposed to UV light through a mask of the track layout; the developer removes the resist that was exposed (for positive resist), leaving resist only on the tracks.
3. The board is placed in etchant (such as ferric chloride), which dissolves the unprotected copper.
4. The remaining resist is removed, leaving copper tracks; holes are drilled for components.

Etching gives very fine detail with no mechanical force. Etchant is corrosive, so gloves and eye protection are needed and waste must be disposed of safely. PCBs can also be made by CNC routing (isolation milling) the copper.

Cutting speed and spindle speed. The cutting speed is the speed at which the surface of the work (lathe) or the cutting edge (drill, mill) moves past the material, in metres per minute. Each material has a recommended cutting speed: roughly 20 to 30 m/min for low carbon steel with high-speed steel tools, and much higher (over 100 m/min) for aluminium. The relationship is:

spindle speed (rev/min) = (cutting speed in m/min × 1000) ÷ (π × diameter in mm)

The × 1000 converts metres to millimetres. (This relationship is not one of the numbered equations; it is an application of circumference = π × diameter.)

#### Worked example

1. Spindle speed. A 10 mm drill is used on low carbon steel with a recommended cutting speed of 25 m/min.
   - Circumference = π × 10 = 31.4 mm per revolution.
   - Cutting speed in mm/min = 25 × 1000 = 25 000 mm/min.
   - Spindle speed = 25 000 ÷ 31.4 ≈ 796 rev/min. Choose the nearest lower available speed, for example 750 rev/min.
   - Order-of-magnitude check: hundreds of rev/min, which is sensible for a 10 mm drill in steel; a 3 mm drill would need a few thousand.

2. Cutting speed. Aluminium bar 40 mm in diameter turns at 900 rev/min. Cutting speed = π × 40 × 900 ÷ 1000 = 113 m/min.

3. Taper angle. A taper reduces a bar from 30 mm diameter to 20 mm diameter over a length of 50 mm.
   - The radius changes by (30 − 20) ÷ 2 = 5 mm.
   - tan θ = opposite ÷ adjacent = 5 ÷ 50 = 0.1, so θ = tan⁻¹(0.1) ≈ 5.7°. The compound slide is set to 5.7° from the axis.
   - Length of the sloping surface (Pythagoras) = √(50² + 5²) = √2525 ≈ 50.2 mm.

#### Common mistakes

- Using the diameter difference instead of half of it in taper calculations. The tool moves by the change in radius.
- Forgetting the × 1000 when converting between m/min and mm.
- Running large drills too fast. Speed must fall as diameter rises.
- Saying shearing produces swarf. It does not; sawing and machining do.

## 3.2.3 to 3.2.7 Shaping, casting and moulding, joining, heat treatment and surface finishing

These processes change a material's shape without removing it, join parts together, change a metal's internal structure, or protect and improve its surface. For each you need to know which materials it suits and how it is done.

### 3.2.3 Shaping

#### What you need to know

- Bending, folding, press forming, composite lay-up, punching and stamping.
- Calculations: angles, tolerances, and pressure and force (E10: pressure = force ÷ area); decimals, standard form, squares and square roots.

#### Explained

- Bending: forming a bar, tube or rod round a curve. Metal bar is bent in a vice or on a bending jig; tube is bent with a former to stop it kinking. Acrylic and other thermoplastic sheet is heated along a line with a strip heater (line bending) and then bent over a former.
- Folding: making a sharp, straight bend in sheet metal, usually on a folding machine (box and pan folder) or press brake. The inside radius of a fold must not be too tight, or the outer surface cracks. Brittle metals cannot be folded.
- Press forming: a sheet of metal is pressed between a shaped punch (male) and die (female) to make a 3D form, such as a car door panel or a sink. Large forces stretch the sheet into shape. Thermoplastic sheet can be formed in a similar way when heated (and by vacuum forming).
- Composite lay-up: layers of fibre cloth or mat (glass or carbon) are laid into a mould that has been coated with release agent and often a gel coat. Each layer is wetted out with resin and rolled to remove air bubbles. The resin cures to make a rigid shell. Pre-preg sheets can be laid up and cured under heat and pressure in an oven or autoclave for higher strength.
- Punching: a punch forces through sheet into a die to cut out a hole; the piece removed is waste (the slug).
- Stamping (blanking): the same action, but the piece pushed out is the part you want (the blank), for example washers or electrical contacts. Stamping can also mean pressing a pattern or lettering into the surface.

Punching and stamping are very fast, so they suit mass production of sheet metal parts.

#### Worked example

A press punches a 10 mm diameter hole. The ram area of the hydraulic cylinder that drives it is 0.005 m² and the oil pressure is 4.0 × 10⁶ Pa.

- Force = pressure × area = 4.0 × 10⁶ × 0.005 = 2.0 × 10⁴ N (20 kN).

A sheet must be folded to 90° ± 1°. A check finds 88.5°. This is outside the tolerance (89° to 91°), so it is rejected or re-worked.

#### Common mistakes

- Confusing punching and stamping (blanking). In punching the hole is wanted; in blanking the cut-out piece is wanted.
- Forgetting release agent in composite lay-up; the part sticks to the mould.

### 3.2.4 Casting and moulding

#### What you need to know

- Pressure die casting, sand casting and injection moulding: which materials each suits and how each is done.

#### Explained

Sand casting (metals such as cast iron, aluminium, bronze):

1. A pattern (a copy of the part, slightly oversized to allow for shrinkage, with tapered sides called draft) is packed in special sand in a two-part box (cope and drag).
2. The pattern is removed, leaving a cavity. A runner (sprue) for pouring and a riser to let gases out and feed shrinkage are cut.
3. Molten metal is poured in and left to solidify.
4. The sand is broken away; the runner and riser are cut off and the part is fettled (cleaned) and machined where needed.

Sand casting suits large parts and small numbers (each mould is used only once), but gives a rough surface and lower accuracy.

Pressure die casting (non-ferrous metals with low melting points: aluminium, zinc, magnesium alloys):

1. Molten metal is forced under high pressure into a steel die (permanent mould) that has been cooled with water.
2. The metal solidifies quickly; the die opens and ejector pins push the part out.

It gives accurate parts with a smooth finish and thin walls, very quickly, so it suits mass production (for example, gearbox housings, toy cars). The steel dies are very expensive.

Injection moulding (thermoplastics, supplied as granules):

1. Granules are fed from a hopper into a heated barrel.
2. A rotating screw carries them forward; heaters and friction melt them.
3. The screw acts as a ram, forcing the molten plastic under pressure into a closed, cooled steel mould.
4. The plastic cools and sets; the mould opens and ejector pins push out the part. The cycle may take only seconds.

It suits very high volumes of complex, accurate parts (housings, bottle caps, gears). The mould is very expensive, so small numbers are uneconomic. Waste (sprues and runners) can be reground and reused.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying sand casting moulds are reused. The sand mould is destroyed to remove the casting.
- Using injection moulding for thermosets in an exam answer about granules being remelted; thermosets cannot be remelted.
- Saying die casting is used for steel. The high melting point would destroy the dies.

### 3.2.5 Joining and assembly

#### What you need to know

- Permanent and temporary (non-permanent) joining methods.
- Rivets, threaded fastenings, soft soldering, hard soldering (silver soldering), brazing and welding.

#### Explained

A permanent joint cannot be taken apart without damaging the parts; a temporary joint can be undone and remade.

| Method | Type | How it works | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rivets | Permanent | A metal pin with a head is put through holes in both parts and the other end is hammered or squeezed to form a second head. Pop (blind) rivets are set from one side with a riveting tool | Aircraft skins, joining sheet metal, where heat would distort parts or parts are different metals |
| Threaded fastenings | Temporary | Nuts and bolts, machine screws, set screws and self-tapping screws clamp parts. Holes may be tapped (internal thread cut) to take a screw | Anything that must be dismantled for maintenance: engine parts, machinery guards |
| Soft soldering | Permanent (can be unsoldered) | A tin-based alloy melting at around 180 to 230 °C flows between the parts; flux cleans the surface | Electronic components on PCBs, copper plumbing |
| Hard soldering (silver soldering) | Permanent | A silver alloy melting at roughly 600 to 800 °C, heated with a brazing torch, with flux | Stronger joints in copper, brass, steel; jewellery; small fittings |
| Brazing | Permanent | A brass (copper-zinc) filler melting at around 900 °C joins steel parts; the parts themselves do not melt | Bicycle frames, joining tubes, repairing cast iron |
| Welding | Permanent | The parts themselves are melted at the joint, usually with filler, and fuse together as they cool. Methods include MIG, TIG, arc and oxy-acetylene; plastics can be welded with heat too | Steel structures, car bodies, pipelines, ships |

Soldering and brazing use a filler that melts at a lower temperature than the parts, so the parts do not melt; joint strength rises from soft solder (weakest) to hard solder to brazing. Welding is strongest because the joint becomes part of the material, but the heat can distort thin parts and alter the metal's properties near the weld.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying soldering melts the parts. Only the solder melts; in welding the parts melt.
- Calling rivets temporary. They have to be drilled out to undo them.
- Forgetting flux: without it, oxides stop the solder wetting the surface.

### 3.2.6 Heat and chemical treatment

#### What you need to know

- Normalising, annealing, hardening and quenching: what each does and how it is carried out.

#### Explained

Heat treatments change a metal's grain structure and therefore its properties.

- Annealing: heat the metal to a set temperature (for steel, just above its upper critical temperature, glowing red; for copper, a dull red; for aluminium, until soap on the surface turns black), hold it there, then cool it very slowly, often in the switched-off furnace or buried in sand or ash. Copper and brass may simply be left to cool. Result: large grains, so the metal is soft, ductile and easy to work. Used to remove work hardening.
- Normalising (steel): heat to just above the upper critical temperature and cool in still air. Cooling is faster than annealing, so grains are finer and more even. Result: relieves internal stresses from forging, welding or cold working and gives a uniform structure that is tougher than annealed steel.
- Hardening: heat medium or high carbon steel to cherry red (above the upper critical temperature) so its structure changes.
- Quenching: then plunge it into cold water, brine or oil. Rapid cooling traps a very hard but brittle structure. Oil quenching is gentler and less likely to crack the part. Quenching is the cooling step of hardening.
- Tempering usually follows hardening: the hardened steel is cleaned bright and gently reheated to a lower temperature (judged by the oxide colours, from pale straw to blue) then quenched. This removes some brittleness and gives toughness. Cutting tools are tempered to pale straw (hard); springs to blue (tough).

Low carbon steel does not have enough carbon to be hardened by quenching. Its surface can be case hardened by adding carbon to the outer layer and then quenching.

| Treatment | Heat to | Cool by | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Annealing | Above critical temperature | Very slowly | Softest, most ductile |
| Normalising | Above critical temperature | In still air | Removes stresses, refines grain, tougher |
| Hardening and quenching | Above critical temperature | Very fast in water or oil | Very hard, brittle |
| Tempering | Lower temperature (about 230 to 300 °C) | Quench | Less brittle, tougher |

#### Common mistakes

- Mixing up annealing (slow cooling, soft) and quenching (fast cooling, hard).
- Saying any steel can be hardened by quenching. It needs enough carbon.
- Missing out tempering when asked how to make a hard but not brittle chisel.

### 3.2.7 Surface finishing

#### What you need to know

- Painting, dip coating, electroplating, galvanising and polishing: how each is done and why.

#### Explained

Finishes protect a surface from corrosion and wear, and improve appearance. Preparation is essential: the surface must be clean, degreased and free of rust or the finish will not stick.

- Painting: a primer is applied to help adhesion and protect bare metal, then undercoat and top coats, by brush, roller or spray. Cheap and in any colour, but it chips, and rust can spread under damaged paint. Powder coating, where dry powder is sprayed electrostatically then baked to a tough skin, is an industrial version.
- Dip coating (plastic dip coating): a metal part is heated (to about 180 °C) and dipped into a fluidised bath of thermoplastic powder (air blown through the powder makes it behave like a liquid). The powder melts onto the hot part, giving a thick, soft-feel, coloured, insulating coating. Used on tool handles, dish racks and wire fencing.
- Electroplating: the part is the cathode (negative) in a solution of a metal salt, with an anode of the plating metal. A current deposits a thin layer of metal (chromium, nickel, zinc, silver or gold) onto the part. Gives corrosion resistance and a decorative or hard-wearing surface; for example chrome-plated taps or gold-plated connectors.
- Galvanising: steel is cleaned (pickled in acid) and dipped into molten zinc at about 450 °C. The zinc coats and bonds to the steel. Zinc protects twice: as a barrier, and because zinc is more reactive than iron, it corrodes in preference (sacrificial protection), so even a scratched area does not rust. Used on crash barriers, nails, buckets, fencing.
- Polishing: abrasives of finer and finer grades (emery cloth, then polishing compounds on a buffing wheel) remove scratches to give a smooth, shiny surface. It improves appearance and can reduce friction, but gives no protection to metals that corrode, so polished steel is often lacquered or oiled. Polishing suits metals that resist corrosion (stainless steel, brass, aluminium) and acrylic edges.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying galvanising is a paint. It is a zinc coating applied by hot dipping.
- Saying polishing protects steel from rust. It does not on its own.
- Forgetting surface preparation in a "describe how to finish" answer.

## 3.3 to 3.3.1 Systems and mechanical systems

A system takes an input, processes it and gives an output. This file covers how engineers describe systems (block diagrams, schematics and flow charts) and the mechanical building blocks: linkages, gears, chains, cams, pulleys and bearings, with gear ratio and mechanical advantage calculations.

### 3.3 Systems

#### What you need to know

- The function of system building blocks; dividing a system into sub-systems.
- Representing systems with block diagrams (input, process, output), schematic diagrams and flow charts.

#### Explained

Every system can be broken down into three kinds of block:

- Input: senses or receives something (a switch pressed, a pedal pushed, light falling on a sensor).
- Process: changes or decides (a gear train changes speed; a microcontroller compares a reading).
- Output: does something (a wheel turns, a motor runs, a lamp lights).

A complex product is divided into sub-systems, each with its own inputs and outputs. A bicycle has a drive sub-system (pedals, chain, sprockets), a braking sub-system and a steering sub-system. Breaking a problem down this way lets engineers design, test and fix each part separately.

A block diagram shows the sub-systems as labelled boxes linked by arrows showing the flow of energy, signals or motion. Feedback is shown by an arrow from the output back to the process (for example a thermostat sensing the room temperature it has changed).

A schematic diagram uses standard symbols (electrical, pneumatic or mechanical) to show how components connect, without showing what they look like.

A flow chart shows the sequence of a process or program using standard shapes: rounded box for start and end, rectangle for a process, diamond for a decision (yes or no), parallelogram for input or output.

#### Common mistakes

- Putting a process inside the input box. Each box should do one thing.
- Drawing arrows the wrong way. Arrows show the direction of flow from input to output.

### 3.3.1 Mechanical systems

#### What you need to know

- Linkages, and changing one type of motion into another: rotary to reciprocating, linear to oscillating.
- Gear trains, including chains and sprockets. Gear ratio (E14) and mechanical advantage (E15).
- Cams and followers, including their use in an engine.
- Pulleys, to reduce the effort needed and to transmit power.
- Bearings.

#### Explained

Four types of motion:

| Motion | Description | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Linear | In a straight line, one direction | A train on a straight track |
| Rotary | Round in a circle | A wheel |
| Reciprocating | Backwards and forwards in a straight line | A piston, a sewing machine needle |
| Oscillating | Backwards and forwards in an arc | A pendulum, a windscreen wiper |

Linkages are rigid bars (links) joined by pivots that transfer force and change the direction or size of motion.

- Reverse motion linkage: a bar pivoted in the middle; pushing one end makes the other end move the opposite way.
- Parallel motion linkage: the input and output move in the same direction (used in toolbox trays and lazy tongs).
- Bell crank: changes the direction of motion through 90°, as in bicycle brake mechanisms.
- Crank and slider: a rotating crank connected by a rod to a slider converts rotary motion to reciprocating (as in a pump) or reciprocating to rotary (as in a car engine, where pistons turn the crankshaft).
- Rack and pinion: a gear (pinion) meshes with a toothed bar (rack); rotary motion becomes linear (car steering, a pillar drill's feed).
- Linear to oscillating: a linkage such as a lever pushed along a slot converts straight-line movement into swinging movement, for example a push rod driving a wiper arm.

Gear trains. Gears are toothed wheels that mesh to transmit rotary motion. In a simple gear train, the driver gear is turned by the input; the driven gear is the output.

- Meshing gears turn in opposite directions. An idler gear placed between them makes the driver and driven gears turn the same way without changing the ratio.
- Gear ratio (E14) = number of teeth on driven gear ÷ number of teeth on driver gear.
- A ratio greater than 1 (small driver, large driven) reduces speed and increases torque (turning force). A ratio less than 1 increases speed and reduces torque.
- Output speed = input speed ÷ gear ratio.
- In a compound gear train, two gears are fixed on the same shaft. The total ratio is the product of the individual ratios.
- Other types: bevel gears change the direction of rotation through 90°; a worm and wheel gives a very large reduction in a small space and cannot be driven backwards.

Chains and sprockets transmit rotary motion over a distance without slipping (as on a bike). Both sprockets turn the same way. The ratio is worked out from the numbers of teeth exactly as for gears.

Cams and followers. A cam is a shaped wheel fixed to a rotating shaft; a follower rests against it and rises and falls as the cam turns, converting rotary motion into reciprocating motion.

- Pear-shaped cam: the follower stays still (dwell) for about half a turn, then rises and falls. Used in car engines to open the valves.
- Eccentric (offset circular) cam: smooth continuous rise and fall.
- Snail (drop) cam: slow rise, then a sudden drop; it can rotate in one direction only.
- Followers can be flat, knife-edged or roller types; roller followers reduce friction.

In an engine, cams on the camshaft (driven from the crankshaft by a belt or chain at half crankshaft speed in a four-stroke engine) push open the inlet and exhaust valves at exactly the right moments; springs close them.

Pulleys.

- Lifting pulleys (block and tackle): using several pulleys means the load is supported by several sections of rope, so the effort needed is less. The mechanical advantage is roughly the number of supporting rope sections, but the effort must move further: no energy is gained.
- Belt and pulley drives transmit rotary power between shafts (as on a pillar drill or in a car's fan belt). Pulleys on a belt turn the same way unless the belt is crossed. The speed ratio = diameter of driven pulley ÷ diameter of driver pulley. Belts can slip, which protects the machine if it jams, but toothed belts are used where timing matters.

Mechanical advantage (E15) = load ÷ effort. A value above 1 means the machine multiplies force. Levers, pulley systems, gear trains and screw jacks give mechanical advantage at the cost of the effort moving further.

Bearings support a rotating or sliding part and reduce friction and wear between it and its housing.

- Plain bearings (bushes): a sleeve of bronze, brass, nylon or PTFE around a shaft. Cheap and quiet; suit low speeds or occasional movement; need lubrication (some bronze bushes are porous and soak up oil).
- Ball bearings: hardened steel balls between inner and outer rings (races) roll rather than slide, giving very low friction at high speeds.
- Roller bearings: cylinders instead of balls give a larger contact area and carry heavier loads.

#### Worked example

1. A driver gear with 15 teeth meshes with a driven gear of 60 teeth. The driver turns at 1200 rev/min.
   - Gear ratio = 60 ÷ 15 = 4 (written 4:1).
   - Output speed = 1200 ÷ 4 = 300 rev/min, and torque is about four times larger (ignoring losses).

2. Compound train: stage 1 is a 10-tooth gear driving a 40-tooth gear; on the same shaft as the 40-tooth gear is a 12-tooth gear driving a 36-tooth gear.
   - Ratio 1 = 40 ÷ 10 = 4. Ratio 2 = 36 ÷ 12 = 3. Total = 4 × 3 = 12.
   - A motor at 3600 rev/min gives 3600 ÷ 12 = 300 rev/min output.

3. A bike's front chainring has 48 teeth and the rear sprocket has 16 teeth. Ratio = 16 ÷ 48 = 1/3, so the rear wheel turns 3 times for each turn of the pedals.

4. A pulley system lifts a 1200 N load with an effort of 400 N. Mechanical advantage = 1200 ÷ 400 = 3.

#### Common mistakes

- Dividing driver by driven. Gear ratio is driven ÷ driver.
- Adding the ratios in a compound train. Multiply them.
- Saying an idler changes the ratio. It changes only the direction.
- Saying a machine with mechanical advantage gives you extra energy. Less effort is needed, but it moves further.

## 3.3.2 to 3.3.3 Electrical and electronic systems

Electrical systems deal with supplying and switching power to loads such as motors and lamps. Electronic systems sense, process and control using small signals. This file covers power supplies, switches and relays, output devices, AC and DC, sensors, analogue and digital signals, processing devices, microcontrollers and flow-chart programs, drivers, and the discrete components, with Ohm's law (E12) and series resistance (E13). For the physics of current, potential difference and resistance, see also [AQA GCSE Physics 4.2 Electricity](../../../aqa/gcse/physics-8463/4.2-electricity.md).

### 3.3.2 Electrical systems

#### What you need to know

- Power supplies: mains and batteries.
- Input control: switches and relays.
- Outputs: motors, buzzers, bells, lamps and solenoids.
- The difference between alternating current (AC) and direct current (DC).
- Ohm's law (E12): current = voltage ÷ resistance; rearranging and substituting.

#### Explained

Power supplies:

- Mains: in the UK, 230 V AC at 50 Hz. It can supply large power continuously but is dangerous; products need insulation, earthing or double insulation, and fuses. Electronic products use a power supply unit (adapter) that steps the voltage down with a transformer and rectifies it to low-voltage DC.
- Batteries: portable DC supplies made of cells (about 1.5 V each for alkaline, 3.7 V for lithium-ion). They suit low-power portable products. Capacity is in milliamp-hours (mAh): a 2000 mAh battery could supply 200 mA for about 10 hours. Rechargeable batteries cost more but can be reused hundreds of times; disposal must be safe because of toxic and reactive chemicals.

AC and DC: in direct current, charge flows one way only and the voltage is steady (batteries, solar cells). In alternating current, the direction reverses many times a second (50 times a second in the UK mains). AC is used for transmitting power because transformers can only change AC voltages; most electronics need DC.

Switches:

- Push-to-make (PTM): contacts close only while pressed (doorbell). Push-to-break (PTB): contacts open while pressed.
- Toggle and rocker switches stay in position (light switches).
- Microswitches: small, sensitive switches operated by a lever; used as limit switches to detect when a moving part reaches a position (a lift door closing).
- Reed switches close when a magnet is near (security alarms on doors).
- Poles and throws: SPST (one circuit, on/off), SPDT (one input switched to either of two outputs), DPDT (two separate circuits switched together, used to reverse a motor).

Relays: an electrically operated switch. A small current through a coil makes it an electromagnet, which pulls a contact across and switches a separate circuit. A low-voltage, low-current control circuit can therefore safely switch a high-current or mains circuit, with the two circuits electrically isolated. A diode is connected across the coil (reverse biased) to protect the driving transistor from the voltage spike when the coil switches off.

Outputs:

| Output | What it does | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DC motor | Rotary motion | Reverses when polarity reverses; speed controlled by voltage or pulsed power |
| Buzzer | Steady sound from a DC supply | Low current, cheap |
| Bell | Loud sound; electromagnet strikes a gong repeatedly | Fire alarms, school bells |
| Lamp (filament) | Light | Inefficient (much energy wasted as heating); being replaced by LEDs |
| Solenoid | Linear motion: a coil pulls in an iron plunger when current flows | Door locks, valves, pinball flippers |

#### Worked example

A 12 V lamp has a resistance of 48 Ω. Current = 12 ÷ 48 = 0.25 A.

A motor draws 1.5 A from a 6 V supply. Its resistance = voltage ÷ current = 6 ÷ 1.5 = 4 Ω (rearranging E12).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a relay amplifies. It switches a separate circuit; it does not make a signal bigger.
- Forgetting the protective diode across a relay coil.
- Mixing up mAh (charge capacity) and mA (current).

### 3.3.3 Electronic systems

#### What you need to know

- Inputs: light and temperature sensors.
- Analogue and digital signals; the analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) in a programmable device.
- Process devices: timers, counters, comparators, logic gates (AND, OR, NOT).
- Programmable devices: microcontrollers (such as PIC) for more complex operations.
- Interfacing: driving loads with a transistor or FET (field-effect transistor).
- Outputs: LEDs, seven-segment displays, buzzers, piezo sounders.
- Discrete components: fixed and variable resistors, signal and rectifier diodes, polarised and non-polarised capacitors.
- Programming using simple flow charts for monitoring and control, with up to three inputs and three outputs.
- Calculations: E12, E13; significant figures; order of magnitude.

#### Explained

Sensors.

- Light-dependent resistor (LDR): its resistance falls as light intensity increases (high in the dark, low in bright light).
- Thermistor (NTC): its resistance falls as temperature increases.

A sensor is used in a voltage divider: it is placed in series with a fixed or variable resistor across the supply, and the voltage at the junction changes with light or temperature. The voltage across each part is in proportion to its resistance. Swapping the sensor and the resistor reverses the action (for example, output rises in the dark instead of in the light). A variable resistor in place of the fixed resistor lets the switching point be adjusted.

Analogue and digital. An analogue signal can have any value within a range and changes smoothly (the voltage from an LDR divider). A digital signal has only two states: high (1, on) or low (0, off). A microcontroller's ADC converts an analogue input voltage into a number (for example 0 to 255 for an 8-bit ADC) so the program can compare it with a threshold.

Process devices:

- Timer: produces a set time delay (monostable, one pulse) or a continuous stream of on-off pulses (astable). The 555 timer IC is a common example; the time depends on a resistor and capacitor: larger values give longer times.
- Counter: counts input pulses and outputs the total in binary, often to drive a display through a decoder.
- Comparator: compares two voltages and switches its output high or low depending on which is bigger. A sensor voltage compared with a reference voltage set by a variable resistor gives a clean on/off switch at a chosen light level or temperature.
- Logic gates make decisions with digital inputs:

| A | B | AND | OR |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 |
| 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |

NOT has one input and inverts it: 0 gives 1 and 1 gives 0. Example: a machine runs only if the guard is closed AND the start button is pressed; an alarm sounds if the door OR the window opens.

Microcontrollers (such as PIC chips): a whole small computer on one chip, with processor, memory, and input and output pins. One programmed chip can replace many separate timers, counters and gates, and its behaviour can be changed by reprogramming instead of rebuilding the circuit. This reduces size, cost and component count.

Interfacing (drivers). Microcontroller and logic outputs can supply only a few milliamps, too little for a motor, relay, solenoid or bright lamp. A driver switches the larger current:

- Bipolar transistor (NPN): a small base current (through a base resistor that limits it) switches on a larger collector current through the load.
- FET (MOSFET): switched by voltage at the gate with almost no gate current; it can switch large currents with little heating. Often preferred for motors.

Output devices:

- LED (light-emitting diode): lights when current flows in the forward direction. It must have a series resistor to limit the current (typically 10 to 20 mA). Uses little power and lasts a long time.
- Seven-segment display: seven LED bars (a to g) arranged to show digits 0 to 9; driven through a decoder or a microcontroller.
- Buzzer: makes its own fixed tone from a DC supply.
- Piezo sounder: a crystal disc that flexes when a voltage is applied; it needs a changing (pulsed) signal, so a microcontroller can make different tones and tunes. Very low current.

Discrete components:

| Component | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Fixed resistor | Limits current, sets voltages; value from the colour code |
| Variable resistor (potentiometer, preset) | Adjustable resistance, for setting thresholds or volume |
| Signal diode | Lets current flow one way only, for small currents; protects against reverse voltage (for example across a relay coil) |
| Rectifier diode | One-way flow for larger currents; converts AC to DC in power supplies |
| Non-polarised capacitor (ceramic, polyester) | Small values; can be connected either way; used for smoothing noise and in timing circuits |
| Polarised (electrolytic) capacitor | Larger values; must be connected the right way round (marked negative leg); used for smoothing supplies and long time delays |

Resistor colour code: the first two bands give digits, the third gives the number of zeros (black 0, brown 1, red 2, orange 3, yellow 4, green 5, blue 6, violet 7, grey 8, white 9); a fourth band gives the tolerance (gold ±5%). Brown-black-orange = 10 000 Ω = 10 kΩ.

Resistors in series (E13): total resistance = R1 + R2 (and so on).

Flow-chart programming. Microcontroller programs are planned and often written as flow charts:

- Start → initialise outputs off.
- Input or read a sensor (for an analogue sensor, read the ADC value into a variable).
- Decision diamond: is the value above the threshold? Is input 1 high?
- Process boxes: switch an output on or off; wait for a set time.
- Loops: arrows back up the chart so the program repeats for ever.

Example: a greenhouse controller reads a temperature sensor (input 1) and a light sensor (input 2). If temperature > threshold, switch on the fan (output 1). If it is dark, switch on a lamp (output 2). Loop back.

#### Worked example

1. An LED needs 2 V and 15 mA. The supply is 9 V. The resistor has 9 − 2 = 7 V across it. R = V ÷ I = 7 ÷ 0.015 = 467 Ω, so choose the next preferred value up, 470 Ω (2 significant figures).
2. Two resistors, 2.2 kΩ and 4.7 kΩ, are in series across 6 V. Total resistance (E13) = 6.9 kΩ = 6900 Ω. Current = 6 ÷ 6900 = 0.00087 A = 0.87 mA.
3. In that voltage divider, the voltage across the 4.7 kΩ resistor is (4.7 ÷ 6.9) × 6 = 4.1 V.
4. Order of magnitude: a microcontroller pin supplies about 20 mA; a motor needs about 1 A, around 50 times more, so a driver transistor or FET is needed.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying an LDR's resistance rises with light. It falls.
- Leaving out the series resistor for an LED; it will burn out.
- Connecting an electrolytic capacitor the wrong way round.
- Driving a motor straight from a microcontroller pin.
- Confusing a comparator (two analogue voltages in, digital out) with a logic gate (digital in, digital out).

3. A solenoid has a resistance of 24 Ω and is connected to 12 V. Calculate the current. [2 marks]
4. Describe how the resistance of an LDR changes as it gets darker. [1 mark]

## 3.3.4 to 3.3.5 Structural, pneumatic and hydraulic systems

Structures carry loads safely to the ground; fluid power systems use air or oil under pressure to produce force and motion. This file covers types of load, frame and monocoque structures, bending, torsion and buckling, and pneumatic and hydraulic circuits, with the pressure equation (E10).

### 3.3.4 Structural systems

#### What you need to know

- Simple loads: imposed loads; dynamic (live) loads; static (dead) loads. How loads are applied to and transmitted through a structure.
- Structure types: space frames and monocoques.
- Bending, torsion and buckling.
- Factor of safety (E11) applied to structures.

#### Explained

Types of load:

- Static (dead) load: a load that does not change, mainly the weight of the structure itself and anything permanently fixed to it, such as a bridge deck's own weight.
- Dynamic (live) load: a load that changes or moves, such as traffic crossing a bridge, people walking, wind gusts, vibration from machines. Sudden or repeated loads have a bigger effect than the same weight applied gently.
- Imposed load: the load put on a structure by its use, beyond its own weight: people, furniture, vehicles, snow on a roof. Designers use set values (for example a floor rated for so many kN per square metre).

How loads are transmitted: each member passes the force it receives to the members and joints it is connected to, and finally to the foundations and ground. A member being pulled is in tension (a tie); a member being squashed is in compression (a strut). Triangulation (using triangles) makes frames rigid because a triangle cannot change shape without one side changing length.

Space frames and monocoques:

- Space frame: a three-dimensional framework of slim struts and ties joined at nodes, arranged in triangles, carrying loads through the members. Strong and light, uses little material, and covers large spans. Examples: exhibition hall roofs, racing car chassis made of welded tubes, electricity pylons (a form of frame).
- Monocoque: the outer skin itself carries the loads, like an egg shell, with no separate internal frame. Most cars use a monocoque body shell of pressed steel panels; aircraft fuselages and racing car tubs (in carbon fibre) are also monocoques. They make efficient use of material and leave the inside space free, but are damaged if the skin is punctured or dented, and are hard to repair.

Ways a structure can fail or deform:

- Bending: a load across a beam makes it bend; the top surface is compressed and the bottom stretched (for a beam supported at both ends and loaded from above). Deep sections such as I-beams resist bending well because most material is far from the centre.
- Torsion: twisting caused by a turning force about the long axis, for example a drive shaft or a car body when one wheel goes over a bump. Closed tube sections resist torsion well; open sections (channel, angle) twist easily.
- Buckling: a long, slender strut in compression bows sideways and collapses suddenly at a load much lower than the material's compressive strength. Shorter, thicker or braced struts, and tube sections, resist buckling.

Factor of safety: structures are designed to carry much more than the load expected in use so that unexpected loads, material defects and wear do not cause failure. The factor of safety compares the material's strength (or failure load) with the stress (or load) it actually carries in use; a factor of 3 means the part could take three times its working load. Higher factors are used where failure would endanger life (lifts, bridges); aircraft use lower factors because extra weight is costly, with careful testing instead.

#### Worked example

A steel tie has a breaking stress of 400 MPa. In use it carries 100 MPa. Factor of safety = 400 ÷ 100 = 4.

A lifting hook must have a factor of safety of 5 and will carry 2 kN. It must be designed not to fail below 5 × 2 = 10 kN.

#### Common mistakes

- Mixing up dead load (permanent, own weight) and live load (changing).
- Saying buckling is the same as crushing. A buckling strut bends sideways long before the material crushes.
- Describing a monocoque as having a frame. The skin is the structure.

### 3.3.5 Pneumatic systems

#### What you need to know

- The uses of, and differences between, pneumatic and hydraulic circuits.
- Example applications: robotics, factory automation, machinery.
- Pressure = force ÷ area (E10), used to calculate the force from a cylinder.

#### Explained

Pneumatics uses compressed air; hydraulics uses a liquid (oil). Both use pressure acting on a piston in a cylinder to produce a linear force.

Components of a pneumatic circuit:

- Compressor and receiver (storage tank): supply air at a set pressure, often around 6 bar (600 kPa) in industry.
- Valves: direct the air. A 3/2 valve (three ports, two positions) can control a single-acting cylinder; a 5/2 valve controls a double-acting cylinder. Valves may be operated by push button, lever, roller trip, solenoid (electrical signal) or air pilot signal.
- Cylinders (actuators): single-acting cylinders are pushed out by air and return by spring; double-acting cylinders are pushed both out and back by air.
- Flow control (restrictor) valves slow the piston by restricting airflow; a shuttle valve gives OR control (either of two buttons); two valves in series give AND control (both buttons pressed, a common safety arrangement to keep both hands clear).

Hydraulic circuits use a pump, oil reservoir, valves and cylinders in the same way. Because oil is (almost) incompressible and runs at much higher pressures (often 100 to 300 bar), hydraulics produces very large forces.

| | Pneumatic | Hydraulic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fluid | Air (compressible) | Oil (incompressible) |
| Typical pressure | Low (about 6 to 10 bar) | High (100 bar and above) |
| Force | Small to medium | Very large |
| Speed | Fast | Slower |
| Positioning | Springy, less precise mid-stroke | Precise, firm, holds position under load |
| Leaks | Air escapes harmlessly; clean | Oil leaks are messy, slippery and can be a fire hazard |
| Cost and noise | Cheaper; exhaust is noisy | More expensive components |
| Examples | Factory pick-and-place, robot grippers, bus doors, packaging machines, air tools | Excavators, car brakes, presses, aircraft landing gear, car lifts |

Both are safe in explosive or wet environments where electric motors could spark.

#### Worked example

A pneumatic cylinder has a piston diameter of 40 mm and air pressure of 0.6 N/mm² (600 kPa).

1. Piston area (E3) = π × r² = π × 20² = 1257 mm².
2. Force (E10 rearranged) = pressure × area = 0.6 × 1257 = 754 N.

A hydraulic press needs a force of 50 kN with oil at 10 N/mm². Area needed = force ÷ pressure = 50 000 ÷ 10 = 5000 mm². Radius = √(5000 ÷ π) = √1592 ≈ 39.9 mm, so a piston of about 80 mm diameter.

#### Common mistakes

- Using the diameter instead of the radius in πr².
- Mixing units: N/mm² × mm² gives N; Pa (N/m²) needs area in m².
- Saying pneumatic systems are better for heavy lifting. Hydraulics give much larger forces.

## 3.4 Testing and investigation

Before a product is made, engineers predict how it will behave using calculations and models; after it is made, they test it. This file covers the calculations the spec lists (area, volume, density, stress, strain, Young's modulus, factor of safety, stiffness, resistance, current and voltage, fluid forces), simulation and CAD modelling, testing materials and structures, destructive and non-destructive testing, testing control programs, quality control, and the forces of lift, drag and thrust.

### 3.4.1 Modelling and calculating

#### What you need to know

- Predict the performance of a system using calculations, simulation or modelling, by hand or with CAD. Exam systems have no more than four major parts.
- Calculate area, volume, stiffness, density, Young's modulus, factor of safety, forces, stress and strain from load and extension, series and parallel resistance, current and voltage.
- Design and test electronic circuits; calculate forces in hydraulic and pneumatic systems.
- Use equations E1 to E15, with units and multipliers (p, n, µ, m, k, M, G, T) and unit conversions.

#### Explained

Types of model:

- Iconic models look like the real thing: sketches, card mock-ups, 3D prints, CAD renders.
- Analogue models behave like the real thing in a different form: a circuit simulator, a flow chart of a control program, a scale wind tunnel model.
- Symbolic models use equations and numbers: a spreadsheet of stresses, a calculation of gear speeds.

CAD and simulation software let engineers test a design virtually: finite element analysis colours a 3D model to show where stress is highest; circuit simulators show voltages and currents; motion simulation checks linkages and gears. Benefits: fewer physical prototypes, cheaper and faster changes, testing of dangerous or expensive situations safely. Limits: a model is only as good as the data and assumptions in it, so final designs are still physically tested.

The equations:

| Ref | Equation | Units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| E1 | Area of rectangle = length × width | m², mm² |
| E2 | Volume of cuboid = length × width × height | m³, mm³ |
| E3 | Area of circle = π × r² | m², mm² |
| E4 | Volume of cylinder = area of end × length | m³ |
| E5 | Area of triangle = ½ × base × height | m² |
| E6 | Density = mass ÷ volume | kg/m³ or g/cm³ |
| E7 | Stress = force ÷ cross-sectional area | N/m² (Pa) or N/mm² (MPa) |
| E8 | Strain = change in length ÷ original length | no units |
| E9 | Young's modulus = stress ÷ strain | Pa (often GPa) |
| E10 | Pressure = force ÷ area | Pa or N/mm² |
| E11 | Factor of safety: material strength compared with the working stress | no units |
| E12 | Current = voltage ÷ resistance | A, V, Ω |
| E13 | Series resistance: R total = R1 + R2 | Ω |
| E14 | Gear ratio = driven teeth ÷ driver teeth | no units |
| E15 | Mechanical advantage = load ÷ effort | no units |

Stiffness of a part (spring or bar) is load ÷ extension (N/mm). Young's modulus is the stiffness of the material itself, independent of the part's size: a high Young's modulus means a stiff material (steel about 200 GPa, aluminium about 70 GPa, nylon about 2 to 4 GPa).

Parallel resistance is also listed: for two resistors in parallel, 1/R total = 1/R1 + 1/R2, or for two resistors R total = (R1 × R2) ÷ (R1 + R2). The total is always less than the smallest resistor.

Useful conversions: 1 m = 1000 mm; 1 m² = 1 000 000 mm²; 1 m³ = 10⁹ mm³ = 1000 litres; 1 litre = 1000 ml = 1000 cm³; 1 tonne = 1000 kg; weight (N) = mass (kg) × 9.81 N/kg; 1 N/mm² = 1 MPa.

#### Worked example

1. Density. An aluminium block measures 50 mm × 40 mm × 20 mm and has a mass of 108 g. Volume (E2) = 5 × 4 × 2 = 40 cm³. Density (E6) = 108 ÷ 40 = 2.7 g/cm³ (2700 kg/m³).
2. Cylinder volume. A steel rod is 20 mm in diameter and 300 mm long. Area (E3) = π × 10² = 314 mm². Volume (E4) = 314 × 300 = 94 200 mm³ = 94.2 cm³.
3. Triangle. A gusset plate is a right-angled triangle with sides 120 mm and 80 mm. Area (E5) = ½ × 120 × 80 = 4800 mm².
4. Stress, strain and Young's modulus. A steel wire 2.0 m long with cross-sectional area 0.50 mm² stretches by 1.6 mm under a load of 80 N.
   - Stress (E7) = 80 ÷ 0.50 = 160 N/mm² = 160 MPa.
   - Strain (E8) = 1.6 ÷ 2000 = 0.0008 (convert 2.0 m to 2000 mm first).
   - Young's modulus (E9) = 160 ÷ 0.0008 = 200 000 N/mm² = 200 GPa.
   - Stiffness of this wire = 80 ÷ 1.6 = 50 N/mm.
5. Factor of safety. If the wire's material fails at 480 MPa, the factor of safety = 480 ÷ 160 = 3.
6. Parallel resistance. 6 Ω and 3 Ω in parallel: (6 × 3) ÷ (6 + 3) = 18 ÷ 9 = 2 Ω. On 12 V: current = 12 ÷ 2 = 6 A.
7. Hydraulic force. A car lift's piston has area 0.02 m² and oil pressure 2.5 MPa. Force = 2.5 × 10⁶ × 0.02 = 50 000 N = 50 kN.

#### Common mistakes

- Forgetting to convert units so they match (mm with m, g with kg).
- Giving strain units. It is a ratio of two lengths.
- Squaring the diameter instead of the radius.
- Adding resistances in parallel. The total must be less than either one.

### 3.4.2 Testing

#### What you need to know

- Testing materials and structures under load, including tensile and compressive strength; plotting and interpreting load-extension and stress-strain graphs.
- Destructive and non-destructive testing (NDT): what each is, the differences, and their advantages.
- Testing and modifying control programs by modelling and enactment, for example changing motor speed or a sensor threshold in a microcontroller program.
- Quality control: tolerances and checks during production.

#### Explained

Tensile test: a standard specimen is clamped in a tensile testing machine and pulled with a steadily increasing force while its extension is measured until it breaks. A load-extension graph (or stress-strain graph) shows:

- A straight section from the origin: elastic region, extension proportional to load; the gradient of the stress-strain line is Young's modulus. Removing the load returns the specimen to its original length.
- The limit of proportionality and elastic limit: beyond here the material deforms plastically (permanently).
- Yield point: a sudden increase in extension with little extra load in some metals such as low carbon steel.
- Maximum load (ultimate tensile strength), then necking and fracture.

A ductile material shows a long plastic region before breaking; a brittle material breaks soon after the elastic region with little plastic stretching.

Compressive tests squash a specimen (such as a concrete cube) until it fails. Other tests: hardness tests press an indenter into the surface and measure the dent (smaller dent = harder); impact tests swing a pendulum to break a notched specimen and measure the energy absorbed (toughness); bending tests load a beam and measure deflection.

Structures are tested by loading prototypes or scale models with weights or a test rig and measuring deflection until a target load or failure.

| | Destructive testing | Non-destructive testing (NDT) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What happens | The sample is loaded until it breaks or is damaged | The part is examined without damaging it |
| Examples | Tensile, compression, impact, hardness (leaves a mark), crash tests | Visual inspection, dye penetrant, ultrasound, X-ray, magnetic particle testing |
| Advantages | Gives actual strengths and failure modes; reliable data for design | The tested part can still be used; every part (not just samples) can be checked; detects internal cracks |
| Disadvantages | Tested part is wasted; only samples can be tested, so results are assumed for the batch | Does not tell you the strength directly; equipment can be expensive and needs skilled operators |

NDT methods: dye penetrant (a coloured dye soaks into surface cracks, then a developer draws it out to show them); ultrasonic (sound pulses reflect from internal flaws); X-ray (internal voids show on the image, used on welds and castings); magnetic particle (iron powder gathers at cracks in a magnetised ferrous part).

Testing control programs: a microcontroller program is first tested by modelling (running it in a simulator, or tracing through the flow chart with sample input values) and by enactment (acting out the sequence step by step, or using switches and LEDs to stand in for real sensors and motors). Problems found are fixed by modifying the program, for example lowering the light threshold so a lamp comes on earlier, or changing the PWM (pulse-width) value to slow a motor.

Quality control (QC) is checking parts during and after production to make sure they meet the specification. Parts are measured against tolerances with Vernier calipers, micrometers, gauges (go/no-go gauges: the "go" end must fit, the "no-go" end must not) and coordinate measuring machines. Samples are tested at set intervals, results recorded, and means and ranges calculated to spot when a machine is drifting out of tolerance. Quality assurance is the wider system of procedures that makes sure QC happens.

#### Worked example

Five shafts are measured from a batch specified at 15.00 ± 0.05 mm: 15.02, 14.98, 15.04, 15.06, 15.01 mm.

- Acceptable range: 14.95 to 15.05 mm. The 15.06 mm shaft is rejected.
- Mean of all five = 75.11 ÷ 5 = 15.022 mm. The mean is above nominal, so the machine may need adjusting.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling an X-ray inspection a destructive test.
- Saying every part can be destructively tested. Only samples can.
- Reading the Young's modulus from the plastic region of a graph. Use the straight elastic part.

### 3.4.3 Aerodynamics

#### What you need to know

- The forces of lift, drag and thrust.
- An example application, such as a compressed-gas powered dragster.

#### Explained

- Thrust is the force that pushes a vehicle forwards: from a propeller, jet engine, or gas escaping backwards from a cylinder.
- Drag (air resistance) opposes motion through air. It increases with speed (roughly with the square of speed), frontal area and a less streamlined shape. Smooth, tapered, streamlined shapes reduce drag.
- Lift is a force at right angles to the airflow, produced when air flows faster over one surface of a wing or aerofoil than the other, giving lower pressure on that side. Aircraft need upward lift; racing cars use inverted wings to create downforce for grip.
- Weight acts downwards.

A vehicle speeds up while thrust is greater than drag (and friction), and reaches a top speed when they balance.

Example: a compressed-gas (CO₂ cartridge) dragster. When the cartridge is pierced, gas rushes out of the back, giving a short burst of thrust. To go fast, the design needs low mass (so the thrust gives more acceleration), a small frontal area and smooth, streamlined body (low drag), and no lift (lift reduces wheel contact and can make the car fly off the track). Wind tunnel or smoke tests, or CFD software, show the airflow.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying drag stays the same at all speeds. It increases sharply with speed.
- Confusing lift with thrust. Lift acts at right angles to the motion.

9. A light-activated lamp program switches on too late in the evening. Explain how it could be tested and modified. [3 marks]

### 3.5 Engineering industries and infrastructure

#### What you need to know

- The positive and negative impacts of engineering industries on social and economic infrastructure, with examples.

#### Explained

Infrastructure is the basic systems a society runs on: transport (roads, railways, airports), energy (power stations, the grid), water and sewerage, communications (phone and broadband networks), and buildings such as hospitals and schools.

Positive impacts:

- Economic: engineering industries create jobs directly (factories) and indirectly (suppliers, local shops); they bring investment and tax income; exports earn money for the country. A new car plant or offshore wind port can regenerate a whole region.
- Social: better transport links connect people to work and services; reliable power, clean water and sanitation improve health; communications networks allow remote work, learning and healthcare; apprenticeships and training raise skill levels.

Negative impacts:

- Economic: when a large plant closes or moves overseas, whole towns can lose jobs and income; automation can reduce employment; large projects can run over budget.
- Social: construction disrupts communities (noise, traffic); new roads or rail lines may demolish homes or split communities; dependence on one industry makes an area vulnerable.
- Environmental: factories and infrastructure use land, energy and resources, and can pollute air, water and land; traffic increases emissions.

Example: a high-speed rail line shortens journey times, creates construction and operating jobs, and draws investment to the cities it serves, but it costs a very large sum, disrupts communities and habitats along the route during building, and may draw business away from towns it bypasses.

#### Common mistakes

- Confusing infrastructure (shared systems such as transport and energy) with a single product.
- Forgetting that the same project can benefit one group and harm another.

## 3.6 Practical engineering skills

These skills are developed through practical work and the NEA, but the written paper can ask about any of them: reading and drawing to conventions, planning production, choosing tools and processes, measuring, and testing. This file covers the knowledge behind the skills, not the NEA project itself.

### 3.6 Problem solving and analysing existing solutions

#### What you need to know

- Use logical, systematic approaches to solve engineering problems.
- Analyse and evaluate existing engineered products.
- Use block diagrams and flow charts to represent systems and processes.

#### Explained

A systematic approach: understand the problem and who it is for; write a specification (measurable requirements such as maximum mass, load to carry, cost limit, size limits, safety standards); research existing solutions; generate and compare ideas against the specification; model and test; refine; make; test and evaluate against the specification.

Analysing an existing product (product analysis): identify its function, materials and why they were chosen, how it was made and assembled, how it meets user needs, its cost, safety features, maintenance, and environmental impact. Disassembling a product shows how parts are joined and which sub-systems it contains. Use this to find strengths to keep and weaknesses to improve.

#### Common mistakes

- Writing specification points that cannot be tested, such as "it must look nice". Make each one measurable, such as "mass under 500 g".

### 3.6 Engineering drawings and CAD

#### What you need to know

- Produce and read mechanical and electrical or electronic drawings to current conventions: orthographic projection (third angle), isometric, assembly drawings, section views and schematic diagrams.
- Use 2D CAD (circuit diagrams, PCB layouts, orthographic drawings) and 3D CAD (solid modelling, isometric views).

#### Explained

Third angle orthographic projection shows an object as separate flat views: the plan (top view) is drawn above the front elevation; the end (side) elevation viewed from the right is drawn to the right of the front elevation. The third angle symbol (a truncated cone drawn in two views) appears in the title block. Conventions:

- Line types: thick continuous for visible outlines; thin dashed for hidden detail; thin chain (long dash, short dash) for centre lines; thin continuous for dimension and projection lines.
- Dimensions in millimetres, with arrowheads touching the projection lines, numbers placed above the dimension line and readable from the bottom or right; each size given once.
- Title block: title, scale (for example 1:2 means drawing half the real size), units, projection symbol, name, date, drawing number.
- Diameters marked with ⌀ and radii with R.

Isometric drawing shows a 3D view with vertical lines vertical and horizontal edges drawn at 30° to the horizontal. All three faces are shown at true length along the axes.

Assembly drawings show how parts fit together, often exploded (parts drawn spread out along their assembly lines) with a parts list numbering each component and giving its material and quantity.

Section views show the inside of an object as if cut along a cutting plane (marked A–A on another view with arrows). The cut surfaces are hatched with thin lines at 45°; different parts are hatched in different directions or spacings.

Schematic diagrams show electrical, electronic or pneumatic circuits using standard symbols (BS and ISO) connected by straight lines, not showing physical layout.

CAD benefits: accurate, easy to edit and copy, files shared and sent straight to CNC machines (CAD/CAM), 3D models can be rendered and tested by simulation, standard parts come from libraries. Drawbacks: software and training cost, files can be lost or corrupted, sketching is still faster for first ideas. PCB design software converts a circuit diagram into a track layout that can be milled or etched.

#### Common mistakes

- Drawing the plan below the front view; that is first angle projection.
- Using solid lines for hidden detail.
- Hatching the whole drawing in a section view; only cut surfaces are hatched.

### 3.6 CNC and CAM

#### What you need to know

- Use CNC and CAM in 2D or 3D: laser cutting, vinyl cutting, PCB routing or drilling, turning, rapid prototyping, milling and routing.

#### Explained

CAM (computer-aided manufacture) software takes a CAD design and works out tool paths: the route the tool or laser will follow, the depth of each cut, speeds and feeds. It outputs a program (often G-code) that a CNC (computer numerical control) machine follows.

| Machine | Typical use |
| --- | --- |
| Laser cutter | Cutting and engraving acrylic, plywood, MDF, card |
| Vinyl cutter | Cutting self-adhesive vinyl for graphics, labels and masks |
| CNC router or mill | Machining pockets, profiles and 3D surfaces in wood, plastics and metals; PCB isolation routing and drilling |
| CNC lathe | Turning cylindrical parts to accurate diameters |
| 3D printer | Rapid prototypes and complex small parts |

Advantages: high accuracy and repeatability; complex shapes; identical batches; less operator skill needed during cutting; safer (enclosed machines). Disadvantages: high cost of machines; programming time; set-up time makes it slow for a single simple part; maintenance and breakdowns stop production.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying CNC machines need no checking. Set-up, workholding and first-part inspection still matter.

### 3.6 Planning, making and quality

#### What you need to know

- Test materials under load to choose a material for a component.
- Produce a production plan covering materials, processes, time and safety.
- Predict performance using calculations and modelling (iconic, analogue and symbolic).
- Select and safely use materials, parts, components, tools and equipment.
- Processes: measuring, marking out, turning, milling, drilling, forming, bending, casting, joining, fastening, folding, shaping and finishing.
- Quality control: work to tolerances; check with Vernier calipers, micrometers and depth gauges; use CNC and CAM to make sure parts fit.
- Design tests to check fitness for purpose and performance, and identify improvements.

#### Explained

Production plan: a sequence of numbered stages, each with the process, tools and equipment, materials, time allowed, safety precautions (risk assessment and PPE), and quality control checks at key points. A Gantt chart can show stages against time, including those that can run in parallel.

Marking out metal: coat the surface with marking blue; use a scriber, engineer's square, steel rule, odd-leg calipers (lines parallel to an edge), dividers (circles and arcs) and centre punch (to mark hole centres so the drill does not wander). Use a surface plate and height gauge for accurate lines. Wood is marked with a pencil, try square and marking gauge.

Measuring instruments:

- Steel rule: to about 0.5 mm.
- Vernier calipers: outside, inside and depth measurements to 0.02 mm (digital versions read directly).
- Micrometer: outside diameters to 0.01 mm; read the main sleeve scale (whole and half millimetres) and add the thimble reading (hundredths).
- Depth gauge: depth of holes, slots and steps.

Safety: wear goggles and appropriate PPE; tie back hair and loose clothing; use machine guards; clamp work; know emergency stops; use fume extraction for soldering, laser cutting and composites; handle hot metal with tongs; follow risk assessments (identify the hazard, assess the risk, put control measures in place).

Testing a prototype: decide what to measure from the specification (for example: carries 50 N without deflecting more than 2 mm; runs for 2 hours on one battery charge), carry out fair, repeatable tests, record results, compare with targets and suggest specific modifications.

#### Worked example

Reading a micrometer: the sleeve shows 12 mm and one half-millimetre line visible after it (12.5 mm), and the thimble line at 23. Reading = 12.5 + 0.23 = 12.73 mm.

#### Common mistakes

- A production plan without quality checks or timings.
- Forgetting to centre punch before drilling.
- Reading a micrometer and missing the half-millimetre line.

## 4.2 Electricity

Electric circuits move energy from a source, such as a cell or the mains, to components that do something useful. This topic covers circuit symbols, charge, current, potential difference and resistance, series and parallel circuits, mains electricity, power, the National Grid and (Physics only) static electricity.

### Equations for this topic

| Equation | Symbols and units | Recall or given |
| --- | --- | --- |
| charge flow = current × time | Q = It (C, A, s) | Recall |
| potential difference = current × resistance | V = IR (V, A, Ω) | Recall |
| power = potential difference × current | P = VI (W, V, A) | Recall |
| power = current² × resistance | P = I²R (W, A, Ω) | Recall |
| energy transferred = power × time | E = Pt (J, W, s) | Recall |
| energy transferred = charge flow × potential difference | E = QV (J, C, V) | Recall |
| total resistance in series | R_total = R₁ + R₂ | Recall (from the spec content) |

### 4.2.1.1 Standard circuit diagram symbols

#### What you need to know

- You should be able to draw and read circuit diagrams using the standard symbols for: switch (open and closed), cell, battery, diode, resistor, variable resistor, LED, lamp, fuse, voltmeter, ammeter, thermistor, LDR.
- You should be able to draw circuit diagrams for the circuits you investigate.

#### Explained

| Component | What the symbol looks like | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cell | A long thin line and a short thick line side by side; the long line is the positive end | Supplies a potential difference |
| Battery | Two or more cell symbols joined, often with a dashed line between | Two or more cells together |
| Open switch | A gap in the wire with a hinged line lifted away | Breaks the circuit |
| Closed switch | The hinged line lying across the gap | Completes the circuit |
| Resistor | A small rectangle | Limits the current |
| Variable resistor | A rectangle with a diagonal arrow through it | Resistance can be changed to control current |
| Lamp | A circle with a cross inside | Gives out light |
| Fuse | A rectangle with a line running through its length | Melts and breaks the circuit if the current is too high |
| Diode | A triangle pointing at a short bar | Lets current flow one way only |
| LED | A diode symbol in a circle with two arrows pointing out | Diode that gives out light |
| Ammeter | A circle with A inside | Measures current; placed in series |
| Voltmeter | A circle with V inside | Measures potential difference; placed in parallel |
| Thermistor | A rectangle with a bent line through it (like a hockey stick) | Resistance falls as temperature rises |
| LDR | A rectangle in a circle with two arrows pointing in | Resistance falls as light gets brighter |

#### Common mistakes

- Drawing a voltmeter in series. It always goes across (in parallel with) the component.
- Leaving gaps at corners. Draw wires as straight lines that join.

### 4.2.1.2 Electrical charge and current

#### What you need to know

- For electrical charge to flow through a closed circuit, the circuit must include a source of potential difference.
- Electric current is a flow of electrical charge. The size of the current is the rate of flow of charge.

```latex
Q = I t
```

- Charge flow Q in coulombs (C), current I in amperes (A), time t in seconds (s).
- A current has the same value at any point in a single closed loop.

#### Explained

In metal wires the charge carriers are electrons. A current of 1 A means 1 coulomb of charge passes a point every second. In a single loop there is nowhere for the charge to pile up or leak away, so the current is the same everywhere in that loop.

#### Worked example

A current of 0.25 A flows through a lamp for 2 minutes. Calculate the charge that flows.

1. t = 2 × 60 = 120 s.
2. Q = 0.25 × 120 = 30 C.

How long does it take for 600 C to flow at 4.0 A?

1. t = Q ÷ I = 600 ÷ 4.0 = 150 s.

#### Common mistakes

- Forgetting to convert minutes to seconds.
- Thinking current is "used up" by components. The current going into a lamp equals the current coming out.

### 4.2.1.3 Current, resistance and potential difference

#### What you need to know

- The current through a component depends on both the resistance of the component and the potential difference across the component. The greater the resistance, the smaller the current for a given potential difference.

```latex
V = I R
```

- Potential difference V in volts (V), current I in amperes (A), resistance R in ohms (Ω).

#### Explained

Potential difference is the push that drives charge round; resistance is how hard it is for the charge to get through. With a bigger push you get more current; with more resistance you get less.

#### Worked example

A resistor has a potential difference of 6.0 V across it and a current of 0.30 A through it. Calculate its resistance.

1. R = V ÷ I = 6.0 ÷ 0.30 = 20 Ω.

A 12 V supply is connected to a 48 Ω heater. Calculate the current.

1. I = V ÷ R = 12 ÷ 48 = 0.25 A.

#### Common mistakes

- Using milliamps without converting. 250 mA = 0.25 A.

#### Practical

Required practical 3: investigating factors that affect resistance.

Part 1, length of a wire:

1. Tape a length of thin constantan (resistance) wire along a metre ruler. Connect a crocodile clip at the zero end.
2. Build a circuit with a cell or low-voltage supply, an ammeter in series and a switch. Connect a voltmeter across the length of wire being tested.
3. Attach the second crocodile clip at 10 cm. Close the switch, read the current and potential difference, then open the switch.
4. Repeat at 20 cm, 30 cm and so on up to 100 cm.
5. Calculate R = V ÷ I for each length. Plot resistance (y-axis) against length (x-axis).

Expected result: a straight line through the origin (or close to it), so resistance is directly proportional to length.

Keep the temperature constant by keeping the current small and opening the switch between readings, because a hot wire has a higher resistance. A line that does not go through the origin suggests a zero error, such as the clip not being exactly at 0 cm or resistance in the clips.

Part 2, resistors in series and parallel:

1. Measure the current and potential difference for one resistor and calculate its resistance.
2. Add a second identical resistor in series and repeat. The total resistance is the sum, so it doubles.
3. Put the two resistors in parallel and repeat. The total resistance is less than either one.

Safety: the wire can get hot at short lengths; use a low potential difference and do not touch it while the current flows.

### 4.2.1.4 Resistors

#### What you need to know

- For some resistors at constant temperature, the current is directly proportional to the potential difference across the resistor. The resistance stays the same as the current changes. These are called ohmic conductors.
- The resistance of components such as lamps, diodes, thermistors and LDRs is not constant; it changes with the current through the component.
- The resistance of a filament lamp increases as the temperature of the filament increases.
- The current through a diode flows in one direction only. The diode has a very high resistance in the reverse direction.
- The resistance of a thermistor decreases as the temperature increases. Thermistors are used in thermostats and temperature sensors.
- The resistance of an LDR decreases as light intensity increases. LDRs are used in circuits that switch lights on when it gets dark.
- You should be able to explain the design and use of a circuit to measure the resistance of a component by measuring the current through, and potential difference across, the component.
- You should be able to draw an appropriate circuit diagram using correct symbols and use graphs to explore whether circuit elements are linear or non-linear, and relate the curves to function and properties.

#### Explained

The I–V graph (current on the y-axis, potential difference on the x-axis) shows how a component behaves.

| Component | Shape of I–V graph | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resistor at constant temperature | Straight line through the origin, in both directions | Resistance is constant |
| Filament lamp | S-shaped curve that gets flatter at higher p.d., the same in both directions | As current rises the filament gets hotter, the ions vibrate more and get in the way of the electrons, so resistance rises |
| Diode | Zero current for negative p.d.; current rises steeply once a small positive p.d. (about 0.6 V) is reached | Very high resistance one way, low the other |

Thermistor: as it warms, resistance falls. Used in fire alarms, fridges and car engine sensors. LDR: in bright light resistance is low; in the dark it is high. Used in street lights and security lights.

#### Common mistakes

- Reading resistance from the gradient of an I–V graph. Resistance = V ÷ I at a point, not the gradient. For a straight line through the origin, the steeper the line, the lower the resistance.
- Saying a thermistor's resistance increases with temperature. For the thermistors at GCSE it decreases.

#### Practical

Required practical 4: investigating I–V characteristics.

1. Set up a circuit with a power supply, a variable resistor, an ammeter in series with the component, and a voltmeter in parallel with the component.
2. Use the variable resistor to change the potential difference across the component in steps. Record current and potential difference each time.
3. Reverse the connections to the component (or the supply) and repeat to get negative values.
4. Repeat for a fixed resistor, a filament lamp and a diode (a diode needs a protective resistor in series).
5. Plot current (y-axis) against potential difference (x-axis) for each, including negative values.

Keep the fixed resistor at a constant temperature by keeping currents low and switching off between readings.

Safety: the lamp gets hot; a diode can be damaged by a large current, so always include its protective resistor.

### 4.2.2 Series and parallel circuits

#### What you need to know

- There are two ways of joining electrical components: in series and in parallel. Some circuits include both.
- For components in series:
  - there is the same current through each component
  - the total potential difference of the power supply is shared between the components
  - the total resistance of two components is the sum of the resistance of each component.

```latex
R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2
```

- For components in parallel:
  - the potential difference across each branch is the same
  - the total current through the whole circuit is the sum of the currents through the separate branches
  - the total resistance of two resistors is less than the resistance of the smaller individual resistor.
- You should be able to use circuit diagrams to construct and check series and parallel circuits that include a variety of common components, and solve problems for circuits with resistors in series using the idea of equivalent resistance.
- You should be able to explain qualitatively why adding resistors in series increases the total resistance whilst adding resistors in parallel decreases the total resistance.
- You should be able to calculate the currents, potential differences and resistances in d.c. series circuits.

#### Explained

In series there is one path, so charge has to go through every resistor in turn: more resistors means more opposition. In parallel there are extra paths, so charge has more routes to take: the total current from the supply goes up for the same potential difference, which means the total resistance has gone down.

#### Worked example

A 9.0 V battery is connected to a 10 Ω resistor and a 20 Ω resistor in series. Calculate the current and the potential difference across each resistor.

1. R_total = 10 + 20 = 30 Ω.
2. I = V ÷ R = 9.0 ÷ 30 = 0.30 A (the same through both).
3. V across 10 Ω = 0.30 × 10 = 3.0 V.
4. V across 20 Ω = 0.30 × 20 = 6.0 V.
5. Check: 3.0 + 6.0 = 9.0 V, the supply potential difference.

Two lamps are in parallel across a 6.0 V supply. One takes 0.20 A and the other 0.50 A. What is the supply current? 0.20 + 0.50 = 0.70 A. The potential difference across each lamp is 6.0 V.

#### Common mistakes

- Adding resistances in parallel. At GCSE you only need to say the total is less than the smallest one.
- Saying the current is "shared" in series. It is the potential difference that is shared in series; current is shared in parallel.

### 4.2.3.1 Direct and alternating potential difference

#### What you need to know

- Mains electricity is an a.c. supply. In the UK the domestic supply has a frequency of 50 Hz and is about 230 V.
- You should be able to explain the difference between direct and alternating potential difference.

#### Explained

A direct potential difference (d.c.), such as from a cell, always pushes charge the same way, so the current flows in one direction. An alternating potential difference (a.c.) keeps reversing direction, so the current flows back and forth. UK mains changes direction 50 times each way every second (50 cycles per second).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying mains is 50 V. It is about 230 V at 50 Hz.

### 4.2.3.2 Mains electricity

#### What you need to know

- Most electrical appliances are connected to the mains using three-core cable.
- The insulation covering each wire is colour coded:
  - live wire: brown
  - neutral wire: blue
  - earth wire: green and yellow stripes.
- The live wire carries the alternating potential difference from the supply. The neutral wire completes the circuit. The earth wire is a safety wire to stop the appliance becoming live.
- The potential difference between the live wire and earth (0 V) is about 230 V. The neutral wire is at, or close to, earth potential (0 V). The earth wire is at 0 V; it only carries a current if there is a fault.
- You should be able to explain that a live wire may be dangerous even when a switch in the mains circuit is open, and explain the danger of providing any connection between the live wire and earth.

#### Explained

Your body is at about 0 V. If you touch the live wire, there is a potential difference of about 230 V across you, and a current flows through you to earth. That can cause a fatal shock.

A switch that is off still leaves the live wire at 230 V on the supply side of the switch, so it is still dangerous to touch. Any connection between the live wire and earth, such as damaged insulation touching a metal case or a person, gives a low-resistance path; a large current flows, which can cause a shock or a fire. In a metal-cased appliance, the earth wire gives that current a safe path; the large current melts the fuse and cuts off the supply.

#### Common mistakes

- Thinking the earth wire carries current all the time. It only does when there is a fault.
- Mixing up the colours. Live is brown, neutral is blue, earth is green and yellow.

### 4.2.4.1 Power

#### What you need to know

- The power transfer in any circuit device is related to the potential difference across it and the current through it, and to the energy changes over time.

```latex
P = V I \qquad P = I^2 R
```

- Power P in watts (W), potential difference V in volts (V), current I in amperes (A), resistance R in ohms (Ω).

#### Worked example

A hairdryer runs on 230 V and draws a current of 5.0 A. Calculate its power.

1. P = 230 × 5.0 = 1150 W.

A current of 3.0 A flows through a 40 Ω heating element. Calculate the power.

1. P = I²R = 3.0² × 40 = 9 × 40 = 360 W.

#### Common mistakes

- Forgetting to square the current in P = I²R.

### 4.2.4.2 Energy transfers in everyday appliances

#### What you need to know

- Everyday electrical appliances are designed to bring about energy transfers.
- The amount of energy an appliance transfers depends on how long it is switched on for and its power.
- You should be able to describe how different domestic appliances transfer energy from batteries or a.c. mains to the kinetic energy of electric motors or the energy of heating devices.
- Work is done when charge flows in a circuit.

```latex
E = P t \qquad E = Q V
```

- Energy E in joules (J), power P in watts (W), time t in seconds (s), charge flow Q in coulombs (C), potential difference V in volts (V).
- You should be able to explain how the power of a circuit device is related to the potential difference across it, the current through it, and the energy transferred over a given time.
- You should be able to describe, with examples, the relationship between the power ratings for domestic electrical appliances and the changes in stored energy when they are in use.

#### Explained

A fan transfers energy electrically from the mains to the kinetic store of the motor and blades. A kettle transfers energy electrically to the thermal store of the heating element and then by heating to the water. A higher power rating means more energy transferred each second: a 3 kW kettle boils water faster than a 2 kW one.

#### Worked example

A 9.0 V battery drives 450 C of charge through a toy motor. How much energy is transferred?

1. E = QV = 450 × 9.0 = 4050 J.

A 60 W lamp is left on for 8 hours. How much energy does it transfer in joules?

1. t = 8 × 3600 = 28 800 s.
2. E = 60 × 28 800 = 1 728 000 J (1.73 MJ).

#### Common mistakes

- Using hours with watts. In E = Pt, power in W and time in s give joules.

### 4.2.4.3 The National Grid

#### What you need to know

- The National Grid is a system of cables and transformers linking power stations to consumers.
- Electrical power is transferred from power stations to consumers using the National Grid.
- Step-up transformers increase the potential difference from the power station to the transmission cables; step-down transformers decrease it to a much lower value for domestic use.
- You should be able to explain why the National Grid system is an efficient way to transfer energy.

#### Explained

For a given power, P = VI. If the potential difference is made very high (hundreds of thousands of volts), the current is very low. The heating in the cables depends on I²R, so a low current means much less energy is wasted heating the cables. Step-down transformers near homes then bring the potential difference down to a safe 230 V.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying high voltage "makes the electricity travel faster". The point is a lower current, so less heating of the cables.

### 4.2.5.1 Static charge (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- When certain insulating materials are rubbed against each other they become electrically charged. Negatively charged electrons are rubbed off one material and onto the other.
- The material that gains electrons becomes negatively charged. The material that loses electrons is left with an equal positive charge.
- When two electrically charged objects are brought close together they exert a force on each other. Two objects that carry the same type of charge repel. Two objects that carry different types of charge attract.
- Attraction and repulsion between two charged objects are examples of non-contact force.
- You should be able to describe the production of static electricity, and sparking, by rubbing surfaces, and describe evidence that charged objects exert forces of attraction or repulsion on one another when not in contact.
- You should be able to explain how the transfer of electrons between objects can explain the phenomena of static electricity.

#### Explained

Only electrons move. Positive charges (protons) stay in the nuclei. Rub a polythene rod with a cloth: electrons move from the cloth to the rod, so the rod becomes negative and the cloth positive. Rub an acetate rod and electrons move from the rod to the cloth, so the rod becomes positive.

Evidence of the forces: hang one charged rod on a thread and bring another near; same charges swing apart, opposite charges swing together. A charged balloon sticks to a wall.

Sparking: if enough charge builds up, the potential difference between the object and something nearby (such as an earthed object) becomes large enough for electrons to jump across the air gap, making a spark.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying positive charges move. At GCSE only electrons are transferred.

### 4.2.5.2 Electric fields (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- A charged object creates an electric field around itself. The electric field is strongest close to the charged object; the further away, the weaker it is.
- A second charged object placed in the field experiences a force. The force gets stronger as the distance between the objects decreases.
- You should be able to draw the electric field pattern for an isolated charged sphere.
- You should be able to explain the concept of an electric field, and how it helps explain non-contact forces between charged objects and sparking.

#### Explained

Field lines for an isolated sphere are straight lines that go out radially, like spokes. For a positive sphere the arrows point outwards (the direction a positive charge would be pushed). For a negative sphere the arrows point inwards. Lines are closer together near the sphere, showing the field is stronger there.

The field explains a non-contact force: one charge does not need to touch the other because each sits in the other's field. Sparking happens when the field is strong enough to pull electrons from air molecules, so the air conducts and charge flows across the gap.

#### Common mistakes

- Drawing field lines that curve or cross for a single sphere. They are straight, radial and never cross.

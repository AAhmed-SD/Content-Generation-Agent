# Pearson Edexcel GCSE Astronomy 1AS0: spec map

Every spec point of Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9–1) in Astronomy (1AS0), in the board's order, with the notes file that covers it.

- Specification: Issue 3, December 2024 (first teaching 2017, first certification 2019). Qualification code 1AS0, confirmed from the spec PDF. Not tiered.
- The spec numbers its content by topic: 1.1 to 16.12. Sub-items are lettered (a, b, c ...); this map lists the point and the file covers every lettered item.
- Students do not have to recall equations. The six formulae (4.4, 8.6, 11.23, 13.3, 16.3, 16.5) and a data sheet (planet data, distances, constants including H₀ = 68 km/s/Mpc) are given in the exam.
- Each student must complete at least one unaided and one aided observation task (from A1 to A10 and B1 to B12, not both from the same row). Observations are not marked, but the written papers test observational techniques; they are covered in file 17.
- Existing notes elsewhere (AQA GCSE Physics 4.8 and the Edexcel GCSE Physics astronomy file) cover a small part of Topics 14 and 16 at a lower depth. The 1AS0 points need more detail, so every point has its own coverage here, with those files linked as background.

## Topic 1 Planet Earth (Paper 1)

Every observation we make is made from a spinning, slightly squashed planet, through a layer of air. This topic covers the Earth's shape and size, its internal layers, the latitude and longitude system, the main reference lines on its surface, and how the atmosphere affects what we see in the sky.

### 1.1 and 1.2 Shape and size of the Earth

#### What you need to know

- The Earth is an oblate spheroid: a sphere slightly flattened at the poles and bulging at the equator.
- The mean diameter of the Earth is about 13 000 km (given on the data sheet). Use it in calculations of scale.

#### Explained

The Earth spins once a day. Material at the equator moves fastest and tends to bulge outwards, so the equatorial diameter is about 43 km more than the pole-to-pole diameter. That difference is tiny (about 0.3%), so from space the Earth looks round; but it is measurable and matters for precise mapping and for satellite orbits. It is also why the equatorial bulge can be pulled on by the Sun and Moon, causing precession (Topic 3).

Evidence that the Earth is round: the curved shadow on the Moon during a lunar eclipse; ships disappearing hull first over the horizon; different stars being visible from different latitudes; the Sun's noon altitude changing with latitude (used by Eratosthenes, Topic 3).

#### Worked example

How many Earth diameters fit into the mean Earth-Moon distance of 380 000 km? 380 000 ÷ 13 000 ≈ 29. So the Moon is roughly 30 Earth diameters away.

Circumference of the Earth = π × d = 3.14 × 13 000 ≈ 41 000 km.

#### Common mistakes

- Describing the Earth as a perfect sphere, or as "egg-shaped". Oblate spheroid means flattened at the poles.
- Getting the bulge the wrong way round: it bulges at the equator.

### 1.3 The Earth's internal divisions

#### What you need to know

- The four main layers and their features: crust, mantle, outer core and inner core.

#### Explained

| Layer | Depth | State | Composition and features |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Crust | Surface to about 5 to 10 km under oceans, 30 to 70 km under continents | Solid | Rocks rich in silicon and oxygen; thinnest layer; broken into tectonic plates |
| Mantle | To about 2900 km | Solid rock, but hot enough to flow very slowly over millions of years | Silicate rocks rich in iron and magnesium; convection currents drive plate movement |
| Outer core | About 2900 to 5100 km | Liquid | Mainly molten iron and nickel; moving liquid metal generates the Earth's magnetic field |
| Inner core | About 5100 km to the centre (about 6400 km) | Solid | Iron and nickel; hottest part (around 5000 to 6000 °C) but solid because of the enormous pressure |

Seismic waves from earthquakes give the evidence: S-waves cannot travel through liquid, and they do not pass through the outer core, so it must be liquid. The magnetic field generated in the outer core produces the magnetosphere, which protects the Earth from the solar wind (Topic 10).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the mantle is liquid magma. It is mostly solid rock that flows extremely slowly.
- Saying the inner core is liquid because it is hottest. Pressure keeps it solid.

### 1.4 and 1.5 Latitude, longitude and surface reference points

#### What you need to know

- Use latitude and longitude to give positions on Earth.
- Use these reference lines and points: the Equator, Tropic of Cancer, Tropic of Capricorn, Arctic Circle, Antarctic Circle, Prime Meridian, North Pole and South Pole.

#### Explained

Latitude is the angle north or south of the Equator, measured from the centre of the Earth: 0° at the Equator to 90° N at the North Pole and 90° S at the South Pole. Lines of equal latitude (parallels) are circles parallel to the Equator.

Longitude is the angle east or west of the Prime Meridian (which passes through Greenwich, London): 0° to 180° E or W. Lines of equal longitude (meridians) run from pole to pole.

A position is written latitude first, for example London ≈ 51.5° N, 0.1° W. Angles can be split into minutes (1° = 60′).

| Reference | Latitude or longitude | Astronomical meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equator | 0° latitude | Directly below the celestial equator; the Sun is overhead at noon at the equinoxes |
| Tropic of Cancer | About 23.5° N | Furthest north the Sun is ever overhead (June solstice) |
| Tropic of Capricorn | About 23.5° S | Furthest south the Sun is ever overhead (December solstice) |
| Arctic Circle | About 66.5° N | North of here there is at least one day a year when the Sun does not set, and one when it does not rise |
| Antarctic Circle | About 66.5° S | The same for the southern hemisphere |
| Prime Meridian | 0° longitude | Reference for longitude and for Greenwich Mean Time (Topic 4) |
| North Pole | 90° N | Directly below the north celestial pole; Polaris almost overhead |
| South Pole | 90° S | Directly below the south celestial pole |

The tropics and polar circles exist because the Earth's axis is tilted by about 23.5° to the perpendicular of its orbit. 23.5° + 66.5° = 90°.

#### Worked example

Place A is at 52° N, 4° W and place B at 52° N, 11° E. They are on the same parallel; the difference in longitude is 4 + 11 = 15°. Since the Earth turns 360° in 24 hours, 15° is 1 hour of time (Topic 4).

#### Common mistakes

- Swapping latitude and longitude. Latitude goes north-south (think "ladder rungs" that run across); longitude lines run from pole to pole.
- Adding longitudes on the same side of the meridian. Two places at 10° W and 4° W differ by 6°, not 14°.

### 1.6 Effects of the atmosphere on observations

#### What you need to know

- How the Earth's atmosphere affects sky colour, causes skyglow (light pollution) and causes twinkling (seeing).

#### Explained

Sky colour: sunlight is white, a mix of all colours. Air molecules scatter short-wavelength (blue) light much more than long-wavelength (red) light. Scattered blue light reaches our eyes from every direction, so the daytime sky looks blue. At sunrise and sunset, light passes through much more air; most blue has been scattered out of the direct beam, so the Sun and the sky near it look orange and red. On the Moon, with no atmosphere, the sky is black even in daytime. Scattering is also why stars cannot be seen in the daytime: the bright scattered sunlight drowns them out.

Skyglow (light pollution): artificial light from streetlights, buildings and cars shines upwards and is scattered by the air, dust and water droplets, making the night sky glow orange or grey. This reduces contrast, so faint stars, the Milky Way, galaxies and nebulae disappear. In a city only a few dozen stars may be visible; at a dark site, thousands. (Causes and effects are developed in 6.6.)

Twinkling (seeing): the atmosphere has moving pockets of air at different temperatures and densities, which refract light by slightly different amounts. Starlight crossing them is bent this way and that, so stars appear to flicker and jump. "Seeing" describes how steady the air is: good seeing means little twinkling and sharp telescope images. Planets twinkle less than stars because they show a tiny disc rather than a point, so the effects average out. Twinkling is worst near the horizon, where light passes through the most air.

Other effects: clouds and humidity block or dim light; the atmosphere absorbs most wavelengths outside visible and radio (Topic 13); refraction lifts objects near the horizon slightly.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the sky is blue because it reflects the oceans.
- Saying stars twinkle because they flicker in brightness themselves. The effect is caused by our atmosphere.
- Calling twinkling "light pollution". Light pollution is artificial light; seeing is turbulence.

## Topic 2 The lunar disc (Paper 1)

The Moon is the easiest object to study without a telescope. This topic covers its shape and size, the main kinds of surface feature and how they formed, seven named features you must find on the disc, its rotation and orbit, why it keeps one face towards us, and libration, which lets us see a little more than half of it.

### 2.1 and 2.2 Shape and size of the Moon

#### What you need to know

- The Moon is (very nearly) a sphere.
- Its mean diameter is about 3500 km (data sheet). Use it in scale and ratio calculations.

#### Explained

The Moon is large enough that its own gravity has pulled it into a spherical shape (see 12.2). It rotates slowly (once a month), so it has almost no equatorial bulge. Its diameter is a little over a quarter of the Earth's.

#### Worked example

Ratio of diameters: Earth ÷ Moon = 13 000 ÷ 3500 ≈ 3.7. So about 3.7 Moons would fit across the Earth.

Angular size check: at 380 000 km, a 3500 km Moon subtends an angle of about (3500 ÷ 380 000) × 57.3° ≈ 0.53°, about half a degree, the same as the Sun (Topic 3).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Moon is a quarter of the Earth's size by volume. The diameter ratio is about 1 : 3.7; the volume ratio is about 1 : 50.

### 2.3 and 2.4 Lunar surface formations: appearance, structure and origin

#### What you need to know

- Recognise craters, maria, terrae, mountains and valleys on the Moon's surface.
- Understand their structure and how each formed.

#### Explained

| Feature | Appearance | Structure and origin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Craters | Circular hollows with raised rims; large ones have central peaks, terraced walls and rays of bright material spreading out (ray craters such as Tycho) | Formed by the impact of asteroids, meteoroids and comets. The impact releases enormous energy, blasting out a bowl far wider than the impactor; ejected material falls back as rays and smaller secondary craters. Most formed early in the Solar System's history; with no air, water or plate movement on the Moon, they are not worn away |
| Maria (singular mare, "seas") | Large, dark, smooth, fairly flat areas, mainly on the near side; few craters | Huge impact basins later flooded by runny basaltic lava that rose from the interior about 3 to 4 billion years ago and set into dark rock. Fewer craters show they are younger than the highlands |
| Terrae (highlands) | Bright, rough, heavily cratered areas | The original crust of lighter-coloured rock (anorthosite) that formed as the early molten Moon cooled. The oldest surfaces, saturated with craters |
| Mountains | Ranges of peaks, often at the edges of maria; best seen near the terminator where they cast long shadows | Mostly the uplifted rims of giant impact basins (for example the Apennines on the edge of the Sea of Showers basin); some are central peaks of craters. Not formed by plate collisions as on Earth |
| Valleys (rilles) | Long narrow troughs, straight or winding | Some are collapsed lava tubes or channels cut by flowing lava (sinuous rilles); straight ones are where the crust has cracked and dropped between faults; some are chains of secondary craters from impacts |

Features are easiest to see along the terminator (the line between the lit and dark parts), where the Sun is low and shadows are long. At full Moon, shadows vanish and the disc looks flat, though bright ray systems stand out.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying maria are water. They are dark solidified lava plains; early observers thought they were seas.
- Saying most lunar craters are volcanic. Almost all are impact craters.
- Saying the full Moon is the best time to see craters. Shadows are longest near the terminator at other phases.

### 2.5 Identifying named features

#### What you need to know

- Locate on the lunar disc: Sea of Tranquility, Ocean of Storms, Sea of Crises, Tycho, Copernicus, Kepler, the Apennine mountain range.

#### Explained

Positions are described as seen with the naked eye from the northern hemisphere, with north at the top (left and right swap when viewed from the southern hemisphere, and many telescopes invert the image).

| Feature | Type | Where to find it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sea of Tranquility (Mare Tranquillitatis) | Mare | Right of centre, slightly above the middle; site of the first crewed landing (Apollo 11) |
| Sea of Crises (Mare Crisium) | Mare | Small, separate, oval dark patch near the upper right (eastern) edge |
| Ocean of Storms (Oceanus Procellarum) | Largest mare | Huge dark area covering much of the left (western) side |
| Tycho | Young ray crater | In the bright southern highlands, lower centre; its bright rays stretch across much of the disc at full Moon |
| Copernicus | Large ray crater with terraced walls | Left of centre, just above the middle, in the dark mare area south of the Sea of Showers |
| Kepler | Smaller bright ray crater | Further left (west) of Copernicus, within the Ocean of Storms |
| Apennine mountains (Montes Apenninus) | Mountain range | Curving arc above centre, along the south-eastern edge of the Sea of Showers (Mare Imbrium) |

A good way to learn them: the dark maria on the right form a chain from the Sea of Crises inwards to the Sea of Tranquility; the bright craters Copernicus and Kepler sit in the grey region on the left; Tycho is the bright spot low down that all the rays come from.

#### Common mistakes

- Placing Tycho in a mare. It is in the bright southern highlands.
- Forgetting that a telescope may flip the image when using a map.

### 2.6 and 2.7 Rotation, revolution and synchronous rotation

#### What you need to know

- Use the Moon's rotation period and its revolution (orbital) period.
- Understand that the Moon's rotation is synchronous with its orbit.

#### Explained

- Revolution (orbital) period: the Moon orbits the Earth once in about 27.3 days relative to the stars (the sidereal month, Topic 4).
- Rotation period: the Moon spins once on its axis in the same 27.3 days.

Because the two periods are equal, the Moon keeps the same face (the near side) towards the Earth. This is synchronous rotation, also called tidal locking. Imagine walking round a chair while always facing it: you also turn round once yourself.

It happened because the Earth's gravity raised tidal bulges in the young Moon. When the Moon spun faster than it orbited, the Earth's pull on the bulges acted as a brake until the spin matched the orbit. Many moons in the Solar System are tidally locked to their planets.

The far side is not permanently dark; it gets the same amount of sunlight. At new Moon the far side is fully lit. ("Dark side" is a misleading name for the side we cannot see.)

#### Worked example

A day on the Moon (sunrise to sunrise at a fixed point) lasts one synodic month, about 29.5 Earth days, so each lunar location has about 14.75 days of daylight then 14.75 days of night.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Moon does not rotate. It rotates once per orbit.
- Saying the far side is always dark.

### 2.8 Lunar libration

#### What you need to know

- The causes of libration and how it lets us see more of the lunar surface.

#### Explained

Although the Moon keeps one face towards us, small apparent "wobbles" called libration let us see about 59% of its surface over time, not just 50%. Libration means a rocking motion.

- Libration in longitude: the Moon's orbit is an ellipse, so its orbital speed varies (faster near perigee, slower near apogee: Kepler's second law, Topic 8). Its rotation rate is steady. So sometimes the rotation gets slightly ahead of the orbit and sometimes behind, letting us see a little round the east and west edges.
- Libration in latitude: the Moon's axis is tilted slightly relative to its orbit around the Earth (about 6.7°), so during each orbit we see a little over the north pole and then a little over the south pole.
- Diurnal libration: as the Earth rotates, an observer is carried from one side of the Earth to the other, so they view the Moon from slightly different positions at moonrise and moonset, seeing a little more round each edge.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Moon physically wobbles. Most libration is an effect of viewing geometry, not a real rocking.
- Saying we see 100% of the Moon over time. About 41% is never visible from Earth.

## Topic 3 The Earth-Moon-Sun system (Paper 1)

The Earth, Moon and Sun affect each other through gravity and light. This topic covers their relative sizes and distances, how ancient Greek astronomers measured them, tides, the slow precession of the Earth's axis, and solar and lunar eclipses.

Data (given on the data sheet): Earth diameter 13 000 km; Moon diameter 3500 km; Sun diameter 1.4 × 10⁶ km; mean Earth-Moon distance 380 000 km; 1 AU (mean Earth-Sun distance) = 1.5 × 10⁸ km.

### 3.1, 3.2 and 3.4 Relative sizes and distances

#### What you need to know

- Use the relative sizes of the Earth, Moon and Sun, including the Sun's mean diameter of 1.4 × 10⁶ km.
- Use the relative distances between them.

#### Explained

| Ratio | Calculation | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sun diameter ÷ Earth diameter | 1 400 000 ÷ 13 000 | ≈ 108 |
| Earth diameter ÷ Moon diameter | 13 000 ÷ 3500 | ≈ 3.7 |
| Sun diameter ÷ Moon diameter | 1 400 000 ÷ 3500 | 400 |
| Sun distance ÷ Moon distance | 150 000 000 ÷ 380 000 | ≈ 395 (about 400) |

The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and also about 400 times further away. So both subtend almost the same angle in our sky, about 0.5°. This coincidence is why the Moon can just cover the Sun in a total solar eclipse.

A scale model: if the Earth were a 13 mm marble, the Moon would be a 3.5 mm bead 38 cm away, and the Sun a ball 1.4 m wide about 150 m away.

#### Worked example

How many Earths would fit side by side across the Sun? 1.4 × 10⁶ ÷ 1.3 × 10⁴ ≈ 108.

The ratio of volumes Sun : Earth = 108³ ≈ 1.3 × 10⁶, so over a million Earths would fit inside the Sun.

#### Common mistakes

- Cubing when a question asks for diameter ratios, or forgetting to cube when it asks about volume.
- Mixing km and m in standard form.

### 3.3 Eratosthenes and Aristarchus

#### What you need to know

- How Eratosthenes and Aristarchus used observations of the Sun and Moon to find, in order: the diameter of the Earth, the diameter of the Moon, the distance to the Moon, the distance to the Sun, the diameter of the Sun.

#### Explained

1. Diameter of the Earth (Eratosthenes, about 240 BCE). At Syene in southern Egypt, at noon on the summer solstice, the Sun was directly overhead (it lit the bottom of a deep well; vertical objects cast no shadow). At the same time in Alexandria, about 800 km (roughly 5000 stadia) due north, a vertical stick cast a shadow showing the Sun was about 7.2° from the vertical. Because the Sun is so far away its rays are parallel, so this angle equals the angle at the Earth's centre between the two cities. 7.2° is 1/50 of 360°, so the circumference is 50 × 800 km = 40 000 km, and the diameter = circumference ÷ π ≈ 12 700 km.

2. Diameter of the Moon (Aristarchus, about 270 BCE). During a lunar eclipse, the Moon passes through the Earth's shadow. By timing how long the Moon took to cross the shadow compared with how long it took to move its own width, Aristarchus estimated that the Earth's shadow at the Moon's distance was a few Moon diameters wide. With the Earth's size known, this gives the Moon's diameter (roughly a third to a quarter of the Earth's; modern value about 0.27).

3. Distance to the Moon. The Moon's angular diameter is about 0.5°. Knowing its real diameter (D) and its angular size (θ), distance = D ÷ θ (with θ in radians), or distance ≈ D × (360 ÷ 2π) ÷ 0.5 ≈ 115 × D. With D = 3500 km this gives about 400 000 km.

4. Distance to the Sun. At exactly first or last quarter, the Moon is half lit, so the angle Earth-Moon-Sun is 90°. Aristarchus measured the angle between the Moon and the Sun as seen from Earth at that moment; he got 87°. With a right-angled triangle, the Sun's distance ÷ Moon's distance = 1 ÷ cos 87° ≈ 19. The true angle is about 89.85°, giving about 390; his method was sound but the angle is very hard to measure, and timing exact quarter is difficult.

5. Diameter of the Sun. Since the Sun and Moon look the same angular size, Sun diameter ÷ Moon diameter = Sun distance ÷ Moon distance. So once the distances are known, the Sun's diameter follows.

The importance: these were the first measurements of the size of the cosmos using geometry and careful observation rather than guesswork. Each result built on the one before.

#### Worked example

Eratosthenes-style: two places on the same meridian are 1100 km apart. At local noon on the same day, the Sun's angle from the vertical is 3.0° at one and 12.9° at the other. Difference = 9.9°. Circumference = 1100 × (360 ÷ 9.9) ≈ 40 000 km. Diameter = 40 000 ÷ π ≈ 12 700 km.

Aristarchus-style: if the Moon-Sun angle at quarter Moon were 89.5°, the Sun would be 1 ÷ cos 89.5° ≈ 115 times further than the Moon.

#### Common mistakes

- Using the angle at the stick, rather than the difference between the two places' angles, when neither place has the Sun overhead.
- Forgetting that Eratosthenes assumed the Sun's rays are parallel.
- Saying Aristarchus got the Sun's distance right. His method was correct but his measured angle gave a value about 20 times too small.

### 3.5 Tides

#### What you need to know

- The relative effects of the Sun and the Moon in producing high and low tides, and spring and neap tides.

#### Explained

The Moon's gravity pulls more strongly on the side of the Earth nearest to it than on the Earth's centre, and more strongly on the centre than on the far side. These differences stretch the oceans into two bulges: one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side. As the Earth rotates under the bulges, a coastline passes through two high tides and two low tides in about 24 hours 50 minutes (the extra 50 minutes is because the Moon moves along its orbit during the day). So high tides are roughly 12 hours 25 minutes apart and are about 50 minutes later each day.

The Sun also raises tides, but its tidal effect is only about half the Moon's, because although the Sun's pull is much stronger overall, it is so far away that the difference in its pull across the Earth is smaller.

- Spring tides: at new Moon and full Moon, the Sun, Moon and Earth are in line, so their tidal effects add. High tides are highest and low tides lowest (the largest tidal range). They happen about every two weeks, nothing to do with the season spring.
- Neap tides: at first and last quarter, the Sun and Moon pull at right angles; their effects partly cancel. The tidal range is smallest.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying there is one high tide a day. There are usually two.
- Linking spring tides to the season of spring.
- Saying the Sun has no tidal effect.

### 3.6 and 3.7 Precession of the Earth's axis

#### What you need to know

- How the gradual precession of the Earth's axis changes the appearance of the Sun, Moon and stars from Earth, and its use in archaeoastronomy.
- Use data on the rate of precession.

#### Explained

The Earth's axis is tilted at about 23.5°. Because the Earth bulges at the equator, the Sun's and Moon's gravity pulls on the bulge and tries to straighten the tilt. Since the Earth is spinning, the result is that the axis slowly traces out a cone in space, like a wobbling spinning top. This is precession. One full circle takes about 26 000 years, so the rate is 360° ÷ 26 000 ≈ 0.014° per year, or about 50 arcseconds per year (1° about every 72 years).

Effects:

- The celestial poles move. Today the north celestial pole is close to Polaris, but about 4500 years ago it was near Thuban (in Draco), and in about 12 000 years it will be near Vega. Polaris is a temporary pole star.
- The equinox points (First Point of Aries, Topic 5) slide westward along the ecliptic: this is the "precession of the equinoxes". The Sun at the March equinox is now in Pisces, not Aries, and is moving towards Aquarius.
- Star coordinates (RA and dec) change slowly, so star catalogues are given for a stated date (epoch), for example J2000.
- Which stars are visible from a given latitude changes over thousands of years: some southern stars that are now never seen from Britain were once visible, and the reverse.
- The direction of sunrise at the solstices barely changes with precession; but the rising and setting points of particular stars on the horizon change noticeably.

Archaeoastronomy studies how ancient peoples observed the sky. If a monument is aligned with a star's rising point, the alignment will have drifted because of precession. Working backwards with the known rate, archaeoastronomers can estimate when the alignment was accurate and so date the structure (see 7.2).

#### Worked example

How far has the north celestial pole's direction moved round its 26 000-year circle in 2000 years? 2000 ÷ 26 000 × 360° ≈ 28°.

How long for the equinox point to move 30° (one zodiac constellation's width, roughly)? 30 ÷ 0.014 ≈ 2200 years.

#### Common mistakes

- Confusing precession (the direction of the axis changes over 26 000 years) with the tilt angle changing. The tilt stays about 23.5°.
- Saying Polaris will always be the pole star.

### 3.8, 3.9 and 3.10 Eclipses

#### What you need to know

- The appearance of the Sun in partial, total and annular solar eclipses, including first, second, third and fourth contact.
- The appearance of the Moon in partial and total lunar eclipses, including first to fourth umbral contact.
- The causes of solar and lunar eclipses.

#### Explained

Shadows: an object lit by a large source like the Sun casts two kinds of shadow. The umbra is the central region of full shadow, where the whole Sun is blocked. The penumbra is the outer region of part shadow, where only part of the Sun is blocked.

Solar eclipse: the Moon passes between the Sun and the Earth, so it can only happen at new Moon. The Moon's shadow falls on the Earth.

- Total: observers in the small umbra (a track usually less than 300 km wide) see the Sun completely covered. The sky darkens, bright stars and planets appear, and the pearly corona and pink prominences become visible around the black disc. Totality lasts at most about 7 minutes.
- Partial: observers in the penumbra see part of the Sun covered, like a bite taken out of it.
- Annular: when the Moon is near apogee (furthest from Earth) it appears slightly smaller than the Sun, so even when centred it cannot cover the whole Sun. A bright ring (annulus) of the Sun's photosphere is left. The umbra does not reach the ground (the region beyond the umbra's tip is the antumbra).

Contacts in a total solar eclipse:

1. First contact: the Moon's disc first touches the Sun's edge; the partial phase begins.
2. Second contact: the Moon's disc fully covers the Sun; totality begins. Just before, Baily's beads (sunlight through lunar valleys) and the diamond ring appear.
3. Third contact: the Moon's trailing edge uncovers the Sun; totality ends.
4. Fourth contact: the Moon leaves the Sun's disc; the eclipse ends.

Lunar eclipse: the Earth passes between the Sun and Moon, so it can only happen at full Moon. The Moon moves into the Earth's shadow. It is visible from the whole night side of the Earth at once, and lasts hours.

- Penumbral phase: a slight dimming that is hard to notice.
- Partial: part of the Moon is in the umbra; a dark curved "bite" appears.
- Total: the whole Moon is in the umbra. It does not vanish but turns a coppery red, because the Earth's atmosphere refracts some sunlight into the shadow, and scatters out the blue (as at sunset), so red light reaches the Moon. Totality can last over an hour.

Umbral contacts in a total lunar eclipse: first contact (U1), the Moon's edge first enters the umbra; second (U2), the Moon is wholly inside the umbra and totality begins; third (U3), the Moon's leading edge starts to leave the umbra, totality ends; fourth (U4), the Moon leaves the umbra completely.

Why eclipses do not happen every month: the Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5° to the ecliptic. At most new and full Moons the Moon passes above or below the Sun-Earth line. Eclipses only happen when a new or full Moon falls close to a node (where the Moon's orbit crosses the ecliptic). This happens in "eclipse seasons" about every 6 months.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a solar eclipse happens at full Moon. It happens at new Moon; lunar eclipses happen at full Moon.
- Saying the Moon disappears in a total lunar eclipse. It glows red.
- Saying solar eclipses happen every month. The 5° tilt of the Moon's orbit prevents this.
- Looking at a partial solar eclipse with the naked eye: it is never safe, apart from during totality itself.

## Topic 4 Time and the Earth-Moon-Sun cycles (Paper 1)

Our units of time come from the sky: the day from the Earth's spin, the month from the Moon, the year from the Earth's orbit. This topic covers the difference between days measured against the stars and against the Sun, solar time and the Equation of Time, shadow sticks and sundials, lunar phases and months, the seasons' effect on sunrise and sunset, time zones, and how longitude is found from time.

Equation (given): Equation of Time = Apparent Solar Time (AST) − Mean Solar Time (MST).
Data: sidereal day = 23 h 56 min; synodic day = 24 h 00 min.

### 4.1 and 4.14 Sidereal and synodic days

#### What you need to know

- The difference between a sidereal day and a synodic (solar) day.
- The relationship between sidereal time and synodic (solar) time.

#### Explained

- A sidereal day is the time for the Earth to rotate once relative to the distant stars: 23 h 56 min (to the nearest minute; more precisely 23 h 56 min 4 s).
- A synodic (solar) day is the time between two successive passages of the Sun across the meridian (two local noons): on average 24 h 00 min.

Why they differ: during one day the Earth moves about 1/365 of the way round its orbit, so about 1° along it. To bring the Sun back to the meridian, the Earth must turn about 1° extra beyond a full rotation. At 360° in 24 hours, 1° takes 4 minutes. So the solar day is about 4 minutes longer than the sidereal day.

Consequences: a given star rises, crosses the meridian and sets about 4 minutes earlier each night (by solar clock time). Over a month that is 2 hours; over a year, 24 hours, so a year contains 366 sidereal days but only 365 solar days. That is why the constellations visible in the evening change with the seasons.

Sidereal time is time kept by the stars. Local sidereal time (LST) equals the right ascension of stars on the meridian (6.11). A sidereal clock gains about 3 min 56 s per solar day.

#### Worked example

A star crosses the meridian at 22:00 tonight. Thirty days later it crosses at about 22:00 − 30 × 4 min = 22:00 − 2 h = 20:00.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Earth rotates once in 24 hours. 24 hours is the solar day; one rotation relative to the stars takes 23 h 56 min.
- Adding the 4 minutes instead of subtracting: stars arrive earlier each night.

### 4.2, 4.3 and 4.18 Apparent Solar Time, Mean Solar Time and GMT

#### What you need to know

- The Sun's role in Apparent Solar Time (AST).
- The Mean Sun's role in Mean Solar Time (MST) and Local Mean Time (LMT).
- Mean time along the Prime Meridian is Greenwich Mean Time (GMT), the same as Universal Time (UT).

#### Explained

Apparent Solar Time is time measured by the real Sun's position: it is 12:00 AST when the Sun crosses the observer's meridian (local noon). A sundial shows AST.

The real Sun is not a steady clock: the length of the solar day varies slightly through the year (4.6). So astronomers invented the Mean Sun: an imaginary Sun that moves at a steady rate along the celestial equator, crossing the meridian exactly every 24 hours. Mean Solar Time is time kept by this Mean Sun; clocks keep mean time.

Local Mean Time (LMT) is mean solar time for your own longitude. Before railways, every town kept its own LMT, so noon in Bristol was about 10 minutes later than in London.

Greenwich Mean Time (GMT) is the Local Mean Time on the Prime Meridian (0° longitude). It is the same as Universal Time (UT), the world's reference time for astronomy and navigation.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a sundial shows clock time. It shows apparent solar time.
- Thinking the Mean Sun is real. It is a calculating device.

### 4.4, 4.5 and 4.6 The Equation of Time

#### What you need to know

- Use: Equation of Time (EoT) = AST − MST.
- The annual variation of the Equation of Time and its causes.

#### Explained

The Equation of Time is the difference between sundial time and mean (clock) time on a given day. It varies through the year between about +16 minutes (early November, sundial ahead of clock) and about −14 minutes (mid February, sundial behind clock). It is zero on about four dates (around mid April, mid June, early September and late December). A graph of EoT against date is a wavy curve with two peaks and two troughs.

Two causes, which add together:

1. The Earth's elliptical orbit. The Earth moves fastest at perihelion (early January) and slowest at aphelion (early July) (Kepler's second law, Topic 8). When it moves faster along its orbit, the Earth must turn further to bring the Sun back to the meridian, so the solar day is longer than average. This effect alone gives a variation with a period of one year (about ±7.5 minutes).
2. The tilt of the Earth's axis (obliquity). The Sun moves along the ecliptic, which is tilted at 23.5° to the celestial equator. Near the equinoxes the Sun's motion along the ecliptic is partly north-south, so its east-west progress is slower; near the solstices it moves parallel to the equator and further from the poles, so its east-west progress is faster. This effect alone gives a variation with a period of half a year (about ±10 minutes).

#### Worked example

On a day when EoT = −12 min, a sundial reads 11:40. MST = AST − EoT = 11:40 − (−12 min) = 11:52.

On a day when EoT = +15 min, local noon by the sundial (12:00 AST) occurs at MST = 12:00 − 0:15 = 11:45 mean time.

#### Common mistakes

- Getting the sign wrong. Rearrange carefully: MST = AST − EoT, and AST = MST + EoT.
- Giving only one cause.

### 4.7 and 4.8 Shadow sticks and sundials

#### What you need to know

- How to find the time of local noon using shadows, including a shadow stick (gnomon).
- The structure and use of sundials.

#### Explained

A shadow stick is a vertical stick on level ground. Its shadow is longest in the morning and evening and shortest when the Sun is highest. Local noon is when the shadow is shortest, and at that moment it points due north (in the northern hemisphere, outside the tropics).

Method: mark the tip of the shadow every few minutes from about an hour before to an hour after expected noon, recording the clock time of each mark. Plot shadow length against time; the minimum of the curve gives the time of local noon. A more accurate way uses symmetry: draw a circle centred on the stick; mark where the shadow tip crosses the circle in the morning and again in the afternoon; local noon is halfway between the two times, and the line midway between the two marks points north.

A sundial has:

- A gnomon: the part that casts the shadow. Its edge (the style) must be parallel to the Earth's axis, so it points at the celestial pole and makes an angle with the horizontal equal to the latitude.
- A dial plate marked with hour lines.

Types include horizontal dials (on a flat surface; hour lines unevenly spaced), vertical dials on walls, and equatorial dials (the plate is parallel to the equator; hour lines are evenly spaced at 15°). A sundial shows local apparent solar time. To get clock time you must correct for (a) the Equation of Time, (b) the difference in longitude from the time-zone meridian (4 minutes per degree), and (c) summer time.

#### Common mistakes

- Using the time of the shortest shadow as 12:00 clock time. It is 12:00 AST.
- Setting a gnomon vertical. It must be tilted at the latitude angle and point to the celestial pole.

### 4.9 and 4.10 Lunar phases and months

#### What you need to know

- The lunar phase cycle.
- The difference between sidereal and synodic months.

#### Explained

The Moon is always half lit by the Sun. The phase we see depends on how much of the lit half faces us, which depends on the angle between the Moon and the Sun as seen from Earth.

| Phase | Position | Rises and sets (approximately) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New Moon | Between Earth and Sun; dark side faces us | Rises and sets with the Sun; not visible |
| Waxing crescent | Small angle east of the Sun | Seen in the evening western sky after sunset |
| First quarter | 90° east of Sun; right half lit (northern hemisphere) | Rises about noon, sets about midnight |
| Waxing gibbous | Between 90° and 180° | Most of the evening |
| Full Moon | Opposite the Sun (opposition) | Rises at sunset, sets at sunrise; visible all night |
| Waning gibbous | | Rises in the late evening |
| Last (third) quarter | 90° west of Sun; left half lit | Rises about midnight, visible in the morning |
| Waning crescent | | Seen in the eastern sky before dawn |

"Waxing" means growing; "waning" means shrinking. In the northern hemisphere a waxing Moon is lit on the right.

Months:

- Sidereal month: the time for the Moon to orbit once relative to the stars, about 27.3 days.
- Synodic month: the time from one new Moon to the next (one cycle of phases), about 29.5 days.

The synodic month is longer because, during one orbit, the Earth has moved about 27° round the Sun, so the Moon has to travel about 2.2 days further to return to the same position relative to the Sun.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying phases are caused by the Earth's shadow. Phases are about viewing angle; the Earth's shadow causes lunar eclipses.
- Swapping the two month lengths.

### 4.11, 4.12 and 4.13 Sunrise, sunset, equinoxes and solstices

#### What you need to know

- The annual variation in the times of sunrise and sunset.
- The astronomical significance of equinoxes and solstices.
- The variation in the Sun's apparent path through the year, especially at the equinoxes and solstices.

#### Explained

The Earth's axis is tilted 23.5° and keeps pointing the same direction in space as the Earth orbits. So for half the year the northern hemisphere leans towards the Sun.

| Event | Date (approx.) | Sun's declination | In the UK (northern hemisphere) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| March (vernal) equinox | 20 March | 0°, crossing the celestial equator northwards | Day and night about equal; Sun rises due east, sets due west |
| June solstice | 21 June | +23.5° (furthest north) | Longest day; Sun rises north-east, sets north-west; highest noon altitude |
| September (autumnal) equinox | 22 September | 0°, crossing southwards | Day and night about equal; due east and due west |
| December solstice | 21 December | −23.5° (furthest south) | Shortest day; rises south-east, sets south-west; lowest noon altitude |

The Sun's noon altitude = 90° − latitude + Sun's declination. At latitude 52° N: at the June solstice 90 − 52 + 23.5 = 61.5°; at the equinoxes 38°; at the December solstice 14.5°.

Sunrise and sunset times change through the year: in the UK, sunrise is earliest in June (before 5 am GMT) and latest in December (around 8 am), and day length ranges from about 16.5 hours to about 7.5 hours. The rate of change is fastest near the equinoxes and slowest near the solstices ("solstice" means "Sun stands still" because the Sun's noon height hardly changes for several days). The earliest sunset is a little before the December solstice and the latest sunrise a little after, because of the Equation of Time.

The higher the latitude, the bigger the seasonal variation; at the equator, day and night are always about 12 hours.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying seasons are caused by the Earth being closer to the Sun in summer. The Earth is closest in early January, in the northern winter. Seasons are caused by the tilt.
- Saying the Sun always rises due east.

### 4.15, 4.16, 4.17 and 4.18 Local time, time zones and GMT

#### What you need to know

- Local time differs for observers at different longitudes.
- The use of time zones; use time-zone data.
- GMT is mean time on the Prime Meridian, the same as UT.

#### Explained

The Earth turns 360° in 24 hours: 15° per hour, 1° per 4 minutes, 1 arcminute of longitude per 4 seconds of time. The Sun crosses meridians further east earlier. So a place 15° east of Greenwich has local noon 1 hour before Greenwich; a place 15° west has noon 1 hour after.

If everyone kept local time, every town would have a different clock time. Railways and telegraphs in the 1800s needed a common time, so standard time zones were introduced: regions keep the mean time of a standard meridian, usually a multiple of 15° from Greenwich, so neighbouring zones usually differ by whole hours. The UK keeps GMT (UT + 0) in winter and British Summer Time (UT + 1) in summer. Zone boundaries follow national borders, so they are irregular, and some countries use half-hour offsets.

#### Worked example

1. It is 12:00 GMT. What is the Local Mean Time at 75° W? 75 ÷ 15 = 5 hours behind: 07:00.
2. A city at 3° W keeps GMT. Its local mean noon occurs at 12:00 + 3 × 4 min = 12:12 GMT.
3. A flight leaves London (UT + 0) at 10:00 and lands in a city on UT + 4 after 6 hours. Landing time in UT is 16:00, local time 20:00.

#### Common mistakes

- Adding time for places west of Greenwich. West is behind (earlier); east is ahead.
- Mixing up 4 minutes per degree with 4 degrees per minute.

### 4.19 Longitude from a shadow stick and the Equation of Time

#### What you need to know

- Use shadow-stick data and the Equation of Time to find the observer's longitude.

#### Explained

Method:

1. Use a shadow stick to find the time of local apparent noon, using a clock set to GMT (UT).
2. Correct for the Equation of Time: at local apparent noon, AST = 12:00, so your local mean time at that instant is MST = 12:00 − EoT.
3. Compare this local mean time with the GMT on the clock at the same instant.
4. Each 4 minutes of difference between local mean time and GMT is 1° of longitude: later than 12:00 GMT means west, earlier means east.

#### Worked example

On a day when EoT = +10 min (sundial ahead of clock), the shortest shadow occurs at 12:22 GMT.

- At that instant AST = 12:00, so local MST = AST − EoT = 12:00 − 0:10 = 11:50.
- GMT is 12:22, so local mean time is 32 minutes behind GMT.
- 32 ÷ 4 = 8°. Behind GMT means west: longitude 8° W.

Check: on a day when EoT = −8 min, local apparent noon is seen at 11:52 GMT. Local MST = 12:00 − (−0:08) = 12:08. Local time is 16 minutes ahead of GMT, so longitude is 4° E.

#### Common mistakes

- Forgetting the Equation of Time correction, which can cause an error of up to 4° (16 min).
- Getting east and west the wrong way round.

### 4.20 and 4.21 Finding longitude at sea

#### What you need to know

- The principles of astronomical methods for finding longitude, including the lunar distance method.
- The principle of the horological method (Harrison's marine chronometer). The inner workings of chronometers are not needed.

#### Explained

Latitude is easy to find at sea (from the altitude of Polaris or the noon Sun, 6.18). Longitude is hard, because it requires knowing the time at a reference place (Greenwich) at the same moment as your local time. Errors caused shipwrecks, so in 1714 the British government offered a large prize for a practical method.

The principle of all methods: longitude difference = local time difference × 15° per hour. Local time comes from the Sun or stars (local noon). The problem is knowing Greenwich time.

Lunar distance method (astronomical): the Moon moves across the background stars by about its own diameter every hour, so it acts as a clock hand on the sky. Astronomers at Greenwich published tables (the Nautical Almanac from 1767) predicting the angle between the Moon and selected bright stars (or the Sun) at every few hours of Greenwich time. A navigator measured that angle with a sextant, corrected it, and looked up the Greenwich time it corresponded to. Comparing it with local time gave longitude. It worked but needed clear skies, careful measurement and long calculations; accuracy was about half a degree.

Other astronomical methods: timing the eclipses of Jupiter's moons (works on land but impractical on a moving ship).

Horological method: carry a clock set to Greenwich time that keeps accurate time on a rolling ship through temperature and humidity changes. John Harrison built a series of marine chronometers in the 1700s; his H4 kept time to within seconds a day on long voyages. At local noon, the navigator reads the chronometer for Greenwich time; the difference gives longitude directly. Chronometers became the standard method as they became cheaper.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the problem was finding latitude. Latitude was easy; longitude needs accurate time.
- Saying the lunar distance method measures the Moon's distance from the Earth. It measures the angle between the Moon and a star.

## Topic 5 Solar System observation (Paper 1)

This topic covers how to look at the Sun safely without a telescope, the Sun's yearly path across the sky, how the planets move against the stars (including their backwards loops), the reference points on the ecliptic, meteors and meteor showers, and the words used for planet positions.

### 5.1 Observing the Sun safely by pinhole projection

#### What you need to know

- How pinhole projection gives a safe image of the Sun.

#### Explained

Never look directly at the Sun, with or without binoculars or a telescope. Its intense visible and invisible (infrared and ultraviolet) radiation can permanently damage the retina in seconds, and the retina has no pain receptors, so you do not feel it happening. Sunglasses, smoked glass and exposed film are not safe.

Pinhole projection: make a small hole (about 1 mm) in a piece of card. Stand with your back to the Sun and let sunlight pass through the hole onto a second, white card held in the shadow of the first. An inverted image of the Sun appears. The further away the screen, the larger (but dimmer) the image: its diameter is about 1/110 of the distance from pinhole to screen (because the Sun subtends about 0.5°). A long box with a pinhole at one end and white paper at the other makes a pinhole camera that shades the image and improves contrast.

Uses: watching the progress of a partial solar eclipse; spotting large sunspots; it is the method for unaided task A6 (sunspot drawings).

#### Common mistakes

- Looking at the Sun through the pinhole. You look at the screen, never through the hole.
- Making the hole large to get a brighter image. A large hole gives a blurred image.

### 5.2, 5.4 and 5.6 The ecliptic, the Zodiacal Band and the First Points

#### What you need to know

- The Sun's apparent annual path against the stars is called the ecliptic.
- The planets' observed motion is within a narrow Zodiacal Band.
- The terms First Point of Aries and First Point of Libra.

#### Explained

As the Earth orbits the Sun, the Sun appears to move against the background stars, travelling once round the sky each year (about 1° per day, eastwards). We cannot see stars behind the Sun in daylight, but we can tell where it is from which stars are on the opposite side at midnight. The Sun's path is the ecliptic, a great circle on the celestial sphere. It is the plane of the Earth's orbit projected onto the sky. Because the Earth's axis is tilted by 23.5°, the ecliptic is tilted by 23.5° to the celestial equator.

The other planets orbit in planes close to the Earth's (within a few degrees), and the Moon's orbit is tilted about 5°. So the Moon and planets are always found within a band about 8° either side of the ecliptic: the Zodiacal Band (or zodiac). The constellations along it are the zodiac constellations (Aries, Taurus, Gemini, Cancer, Leo, Virgo, Libra, Scorpius, Sagittarius, Capricornus, Aquarius, Pisces, and also Ophiuchus, which the ecliptic crosses).

The ecliptic crosses the celestial equator at two points:

- First Point of Aries: where the Sun crosses the celestial equator going north, at the March equinox. It is the zero point of right ascension (6.8).
- First Point of Libra: where the Sun crosses going south, at the September equinox (RA 12 h).

The names come from the constellations these points lay in about 2000 years ago. Because of precession (3.6), the First Point of Aries is now in Pisces and the First Point of Libra in Virgo; the names are kept.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the ecliptic is the celestial equator. They are tilted at 23.5° to each other and meet only at the two First Points.
- Saying the First Point of Aries is still in Aries.

### 5.3 and 5.5 Changing positions of the planets and retrograde motion

#### What you need to know

- The positions of the planets in the night sky change.
- The observed retrograde motion of planets.

#### Explained

The word planet means "wanderer": over days and weeks a planet moves against the fixed pattern of stars. Its motion is usually eastwards (prograde, the same direction as the Sun and Moon move against the stars). Inner planets (Mercury and Venus) are always seen fairly close to the Sun, in the evening or morning sky; outer planets can be seen at any angle from the Sun, including all night at opposition.

Retrograde motion: from time to time, an outer planet such as Mars appears to slow down, stop, move westwards (backwards) for a few weeks, stop again, and then resume its eastward motion, tracing a loop or zigzag on the sky.

Explanation in the Sun-centred (heliocentric) model: the Earth moves faster round its orbit than an outer planet. When the Earth overtakes the planet, which happens around opposition, our line of sight to the planet swings backwards relative to the distant stars, just as a slower car seems to move backwards when you overtake it on a motorway. Mars retrogrades for about 2 months every 26 months; Jupiter for about 4 months every 13 months. The loop shape (rather than a straight back-and-forth) is because the planets' orbits are tilted slightly to each other.

Inner planets also show retrograde motion, when they overtake the Earth around inferior conjunction.

Explaining retrograde motion was the main challenge for Earth-centred models, which needed epicycles (Topic 7). The heliocentric model explains it simply.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the planet really moves backwards in its orbit. It is an effect of our changing viewpoint.
- Saying retrograde motion happens at conjunction for an outer planet. It is centred on opposition.

### 5.7 Meteors and meteor showers

#### What you need to know

- The appearance and cause of meteors and meteor showers, including finding the radiant.

#### Explained

A meteor ("shooting star") is a brief streak of light, usually lasting less than a second, as a small particle (a meteoroid, usually sand-grain to pebble size) enters the Earth's atmosphere at tens of kilometres per second (about 11 to 72 km/s). Friction with the air heats it and the air around it until they glow, at heights of about 80 to 120 km. Most burn up completely. A very bright meteor is a fireball. A piece that survives to the ground is a meteorite (11.10).

Sporadic meteors can be seen on any night, a few per hour. A meteor shower is a period, on the same dates each year, when many meteors are seen. A shower happens when the Earth passes through a stream of debris left along the orbit of a comet (or occasionally an asteroid). Examples: the Perseids (peak around 12 August, from comet Swift-Tuttle), the Geminids (around 14 December, from an asteroid), the Leonids (around 17 November). The zenithal hourly rate (ZHR) is the number an observer would see per hour under ideal dark skies with the radiant overhead.

The radiant: the meteors in a shower travel along parallel paths. Seen from the ground, parallel lines appear to spread out from a single point in the distance (perspective, like railway tracks). That point is the radiant. The shower is named after the constellation containing it (Perseids from Perseus).

Determining the radiant (tasks A2 and B2): plot the paths of several shower meteors on a star chart, or photograph them; extend each trail backwards; the lines meet (approximately) at the radiant. Meteors whose trails do not point back to the radiant are sporadic. The best time to observe is after midnight, when your side of the Earth faces forwards along its orbit and sweeps up more meteoroids, and when the radiant is high.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying meteors are stars. They are small particles burning in our atmosphere.
- Saying meteors come from the radiant's stars. The radiant is a perspective effect; the meteoroids are in the Solar System.
- Confusing meteoroid (in space), meteor (the light), meteorite (on the ground).

### 5.8 Planetary positions: conjunction, opposition, elongation, transit, occultation

#### What you need to know

- The terms: conjunction (superior and inferior), opposition, elongation, transit, occultation.

#### Explained

- Conjunction: two bodies appear close together in the sky (same RA or ecliptic longitude). For a planet, conjunction means it is in the same direction as the Sun.
  - Inferior conjunction: an inner planet (Mercury or Venus) is between the Earth and the Sun.
  - Superior conjunction: a planet is on the far side of the Sun from the Earth. Inner and outer planets both have superior conjunctions; outer planets have only this kind. Planets near conjunction are lost in the Sun's glare.
- Opposition: an outer planet is directly opposite the Sun in our sky (Sun, Earth and planet in line with the Earth in the middle). The planet is closest to Earth, brightest, and visible all night, rising at sunset and on the meridian at midnight. Best time to observe outer planets. Inner planets never reach opposition.
- Elongation: the angle between a planet and the Sun as seen from Earth. Greatest elongation is the maximum angle an inner planet reaches: about 28° for Mercury, about 47° for Venus. Eastern elongation: evening sky; western elongation: morning sky. These are the best times to see Mercury and Venus.
- Transit: a small body passes in front of a larger one, for example Mercury or Venus crossing the Sun's disc (only possible at inferior conjunction, and only when the planet is near a node of its orbit), or a moon crossing Jupiter's disc. Transits of Venus were used to measure the AU (11.12).
- Occultation: a large body passes in front of a smaller-looking one and hides it, for example the Moon occulting a star or planet. The star disappears instantly because it is a point source.

The difference between transit and occultation: in a transit the near object looks smaller than the far one; in an occultation the near object looks larger and hides the far one. A total solar eclipse is a kind of occultation.

#### Worked example

Venus's orbit radius is 0.72 AU. At greatest elongation the Sun-Venus-Earth angle is 90°, so sin(elongation) = 0.72 ÷ 1.0, giving elongation ≈ 46°. (This is how greatest elongation can be used to find an inner planet's orbit radius in AU.)

#### Common mistakes

- Saying Venus can be at opposition. Only outer planets can.
- Mixing up transit and occultation.

## Topic 6 Celestial observation (Paper 1)

This topic is about finding your way round the night sky with the naked eye: what you can see, the main constellations and how to use them as signposts, star charts, light pollution, the two coordinate systems (equatorial and horizon) and how they link through latitude, the daily turning of the sky, circumpolar stars, finding latitude from Polaris, naked-eye techniques and what affects visibility.

### 6.1 Naked-eye phenomena

#### What you need to know

- Recognise the Sun, Moon, stars (including double stars, constellations and asterisms), star clusters, galaxies and nebulae, planets, comets, meteors, aurorae and supernovae; and artificial satellites and aircraft.

#### Explained

| Object | How to recognise it with the naked eye |
| --- | --- |
| Sun | Never look; observe by projection only |
| Moon | Bright disc or crescent, changing phase nightly, moves about 13° a night against the stars |
| Stars | Points of light that twinkle; fixed patterns; colours (reddish Betelgeuse, bluish Rigel) |
| Double stars | Two stars very close together, e.g. Mizar and Alcor in the Plough's handle |
| Constellations | Official regions of sky (88 of them) containing star patterns, e.g. Orion |
| Asterisms | Recognisable patterns that are not whole official constellations, e.g. the Plough (part of Ursa Major), the Summer Triangle |
| Star clusters | Small fuzzy patch or tight group, e.g. the Pleiades (M45) in Taurus |
| Galaxies and nebulae | Faint fuzzy patches from a dark site: Andromeda Galaxy (M31), Orion Nebula (M42) in the Sword |
| Planets | Bright, steady (twinkle less), near the ecliptic; move slowly against the stars over weeks; Venus is the brightest point after the Moon |
| Comets | Fuzzy head (coma), sometimes a tail pointing away from the Sun; move over days |
| Meteors | Brief streaks lasting under a second |
| Aurorae | Glowing green or red curtains or arcs, moving slowly; usually in the north for UK observers, more common near solar maximum |
| Supernovae | A "new" star that suddenly appears very bright and fades over months (rare in our Galaxy; last naked-eye one, SN 1987A in the LMC) |
| Artificial satellites | Steady points moving smoothly across the sky in a few minutes, no flashing lights; may fade suddenly as they enter the Earth's shadow; the ISS is very bright |
| Aircraft | Flashing red, green and white lights; often audible; may change direction |

#### Common mistakes

- Mistaking a satellite for a meteor (a meteor lasts under a second) or for an aircraft (an aircraft flashes).
- Saying planets do not move. They move against the stars over days and weeks.

### 6.2 and 6.3 Constellations, asterisms and pointers

#### What you need to know

- Recognise and draw Cassiopeia, Cygnus, Orion, the Plough, the Southern Cross, the Summer Triangle and the Square of Pegasus, with their most prominent stars.
- Use asterisms as pointers: Arcturus and Polaris from the Plough; Sirius, Aldebaran and the Pleiades from Orion's Belt; Fomalhaut and the Andromeda Galaxy from the Square of Pegasus.

#### Explained

| Pattern | Shape | Prominent stars | Season (evening, UK) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Plough (in Ursa Major) | Saucepan of seven stars: four in the bowl, three in the curved handle | Dubhe and Merak (the "pointers" at the end of the bowl), Alioth, Mizar (with Alcor) | Circumpolar; high in spring |
| Cassiopeia | W or M shape | Schedar, Caph | Circumpolar; high in autumn |
| Cygnus (Northern Cross) | Large cross lying along the Milky Way | Deneb (tail), Albireo (beak; a coloured double in a telescope) | Summer and autumn |
| Summer Triangle | Three bright stars in three constellations | Vega (Lyra), Deneb (Cygnus), Altair (Aquila) | Summer and early autumn |
| Square of Pegasus | Large square of four similar stars | Markab, Scheat, Algenib, and Alpheratz (which belongs to Andromeda) | Autumn |
| Orion | Hourglass figure with three belt stars in a line and a sword below | Betelgeuse (red, top left), Rigel (blue-white, bottom right), Bellatrix, the belt (Alnitak, Alnilam, Mintaka) | Winter |
| Southern Cross (Crux) | Small kite-shaped cross | Acrux, Mimosa, Gacrux | Southern sky; never visible from the UK |

Pointers (star-hopping):

- From the Plough to Polaris: follow the line from Merak through Dubhe (the two stars at the end of the bowl, away from the handle) about five times their separation to reach Polaris, the pole star.
- From the Plough to Arcturus: follow the curve of the handle away from the bowl ("arc to Arcturus") to the bright orange star Arcturus in Boötes.
- Orion's Belt to Sirius: extend the belt downwards to the left (south-east) to Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, in Canis Major.
- Orion's Belt to Aldebaran and the Pleiades: extend the belt upwards to the right (north-west) to orange Aldebaran in Taurus, and further on to the Pleiades cluster.
- Square of Pegasus to Fomalhaut: extend the western (right) side of the square southwards a long way to Fomalhaut, a lone bright star low in the south.
- Square of Pegasus to the Andromeda Galaxy: from Alpheratz, follow the chain of stars of Andromeda north-east; hop up from the second bright star (Mirach) through two fainter stars to the fuzzy patch of M31.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Plough is a constellation. It is an asterism in Ursa Major.
- Saying Polaris is the brightest star. It is only moderately bright; Sirius is the brightest.
- Saying the Southern Cross is visible from Britain.

### 6.4 and 6.5 Star names across cultures and finding objects

#### What you need to know

- Why constellation, asterism and star names vary between cultures.
- Use star charts, planispheres, computer programs and apps to identify objects.

#### Explained

Constellations are patterns imagined by people; the stars in a pattern are usually at very different distances and not physically linked. Different cultures saw different shapes and told their own stories: the Plough is the Big Dipper in North America and has other names elsewhere; many bright star names used today are Arabic (Aldebaran, Betelgeuse, Altair, Deneb) because astronomers in the Islamic world preserved and extended Greek astronomy; Chinese astronomy divided the sky into many smaller asterisms; Aboriginal Australian traditions include patterns made by dark dust lanes in the Milky Way. The International Astronomical Union fixed 88 official constellations with agreed boundaries in the 1920s so astronomers worldwide could communicate clearly.

Finding objects:

- Star chart: a map of the sky showing stars (larger dots for brighter stars), constellation lines, and RA and dec grid. Hold it so the direction you face is at the bottom.
- Planisphere: a rotating star map; set the date and time on the edge, and the window shows the sky visible at that moment. Made for a particular latitude.
- Computer programs and apps: show the sky for any time and place; can use phone sensors to point at objects. Use a red-light mode to protect dark adaptation.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the stars of a constellation are close together in space.
- Using a planisphere made for a very different latitude.

### 6.6 Light pollution

#### What you need to know

- The causes and effects of light pollution on observations.

#### Explained

Causes: artificial lighting that shines upwards or sideways rather than only where it is needed (streetlights, security lights, floodlit buildings, sports grounds, illuminated signs), scattered by air molecules, dust and water droplets in the atmosphere into a skyglow over towns. Also glare from nearby lights shining into the observer's eyes, and light trespass.

Effects: reduced contrast between sky and faint objects, so fewer stars are visible (the limiting magnitude may be 3 or 4 in a city, compared with about 6.5 at a dark site); the Milky Way, faint galaxies and nebulae, faint meteors, aurorae and the zodiacal light become invisible; dark adaptation is spoiled; long-exposure photographs are fogged with an orange background.

Reducing it: shielded "full cut-off" lights pointing downwards, lower-intensity and warmer lights, switching off when not needed, timers and motion sensors; Dark Sky Reserves and Parks. Observers can travel to dark sites, observe objects high in the sky (away from the town glow at the horizon), and use filters for some nebulae. Tasks A5 and B5 survey light pollution by finding the faintest star visible.

#### Common mistakes

- Blaming moonlight as "light pollution". It is natural sky brightness, although it also reduces visibility.

### 6.7 to 6.11 Celestial sphere and coordinates

#### What you need to know

- The celestial sphere, celestial poles and celestial equator.
- The equatorial coordinate system (right ascension and declination).
- The horizon coordinate system (altitude and azimuth).
- How the observer's latitude links the two systems for objects on the meridian.
- How the observer's meridian defines local sidereal time and an object's hour angle.

#### Explained

The celestial sphere is an imaginary sphere of very large radius centred on the Earth, onto which all celestial objects appear to be projected. It looks as if the sphere turns once a day around us.

- Celestial poles: the points where the Earth's axis, extended, meets the celestial sphere. The north celestial pole (NCP) is within 1° of Polaris.
- Celestial equator: the projection of the Earth's equator onto the celestial sphere, 90° from both celestial poles.

Equatorial coordinates (fixed to the stars, the same for every observer):

- Declination (dec, δ): the angle north (+) or south (−) of the celestial equator, from +90° at the NCP to −90° at the south celestial pole. Like latitude. Measured in degrees, arcminutes and arcseconds.
- Right ascension (RA, α): the angle eastwards along the celestial equator from the First Point of Aries, measured in hours, minutes and seconds of time, from 0 h to 24 h (1 h = 15°). Like longitude.

Horizon coordinates (depend on the observer's location and time):

- Altitude: the angle above the horizon, from 0° at the horizon to 90° at the zenith (directly overhead).
- Azimuth: the angle measured from north, eastwards (clockwise seen from above) round the horizon: north 0°, east 90°, south 180°, west 270°.

The meridian is the great circle running from the north point of the horizon, through the zenith, to the south point. It is the line from due north to due south through the point overhead.

Linking the systems (6.10): for an observer at latitude φ (north):

- The altitude of the north celestial pole = φ.
- The celestial equator crosses the meridian at altitude 90° − φ, in the south.
- So a star of declination δ, when on the meridian south of the zenith, has altitude = 90° − φ + δ (the culmination altitude).

Local sidereal time and hour angle (6.11): as the Earth turns, stars of increasing RA cross the meridian. Local sidereal time (LST) is the RA of the stars currently on the observer's meridian. An object's hour angle (HA) is the time since it crossed the meridian: HA = LST − RA. HA = 0 means the object is on the meridian (best placed); positive HA means it is west of the meridian (has already culminated).

#### Worked example

1. An observer is at 52° N. What is the altitude of a star of dec +20° when it culminates? 90 − 52 + 20 = 58°, due south.
2. Sirius has dec −16.7°. From 52° N its maximum altitude is 90 − 52 − 16.7 = 21.3°.
3. LST is 5 h 30 min. Betelgeuse has RA 5 h 55 min. HA = 5:30 − 5:55 = −25 min, so it will be on the meridian in 25 sidereal minutes.

#### Common mistakes

- Measuring RA in degrees in answers. It is in hours.
- Confusing altitude (angle above the horizon) with height above sea level.
- Using latitude where 90° − latitude is needed.

### 6.12 and 6.17 Planning: best times and best objects

#### What you need to know

- Use equatorial and horizon coordinates to find the best time to observe an object and the best objects to observe at a given time.
- Use rising and setting times to predict an object's approximate position in the sky.

#### Explained

An object is best placed when it is highest, on the meridian (culminating), because its light then passes through the least atmosphere (less dimming, steadier seeing, less light pollution near the horizon). That happens when LST = the object's RA.

- Best time for an object: find the date and time when LST equals its RA during darkness. Objects with RA about 12 hours from the Sun's RA culminate around midnight: they are at opposition and well placed. The Sun's RA is 0 h at the March equinox, 6 h in June, 12 h in September and 18 h in December. So objects at RA 12 h are best seen around midnight in late March, and objects at RA 6 h (Orion) around midnight in December.
- Best objects at a given time: work out the LST, then choose objects with RA close to it and with declinations giving a good altitude from your latitude (dec greater than φ − 90° to rise at all; ideally high).

Rising and setting: a star rises in the east half of the sky and sets in the west. A star on the celestial equator rises due east and sets due west and is above the horizon for 12 hours; it culminates halfway between rising and setting. Stars north of the equator (UK view) rise north of east and are up for more than 12 hours; southern stars rise south of east and are up for less. So if a star rises at 20:00 and sets at 06:00, it will be on the meridian (due south) at about 01:00; at 22:30 it will be roughly in the south-east, halfway up to its maximum.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the best time is just after rising. Low objects are dimmed and blurred.
- Forgetting that the object must culminate during darkness.

### 6.13 to 6.16 Diurnal motion and circumpolar stars

#### What you need to know

- The terms cardinal points, culmination, meridian, zenith, circumpolarity.
- Diurnal (daily) motion of the sky due to the Earth's rotation.
- Use a star's declination to determine whether it is circumpolar from a given latitude.
- The motion of circumpolar stars, including upper transit (culmination) and lower transit.

#### Explained

- Cardinal points: north, east, south and west on the horizon.
- Zenith: the point directly overhead (altitude 90°).
- Meridian: the north-south line through the zenith.
- Culmination: an object reaching its highest point, as it crosses the meridian (upper transit).
- Circumpolarity: a circumpolar star never sets; it circles the celestial pole and stays above the horizon all the time.

Diurnal motion: the Earth rotates from west to east, so the whole sky appears to rotate from east to west once per sidereal day, about the celestial poles. From the UK, stars rise in the east, climb to culminate in the south, and set in the west; stars near the pole circle it anticlockwise (looking north). Long-exposure photographs pointed at the pole show circular star trails; each trail covers 15° of arc per hour of exposure.

Circumpolar condition: from northern latitude φ, a star is circumpolar if its declination δ > 90° − φ. At 52° N, any star with dec greater than +38° never sets (Capella, Deneb, the Plough, Cassiopeia). Stars with dec less than −(90° − φ), i.e. below −38° from 52° N, never rise (the Southern Cross).

A circumpolar star crosses the meridian twice a day:

- Upper transit (culmination): above the pole, at altitude 90° − φ + δ (it may be north of the zenith if δ > φ).
- Lower transit: below the pole, at altitude φ − (90° − δ) = φ + δ − 90°. For a circumpolar star this is above 0°.

#### Worked example

From latitude 55° N, is Vega (dec +38.8°) circumpolar? 90 − 55 = 35. 38.8 > 35, so yes. Its lower transit altitude = 55 + 38.8 − 90 = 3.8°, just above the northern horizon. Its upper transit altitude = 90 − 55 + 38.8 = 73.8°, high in the south.

#### Common mistakes

- Using dec > φ as the test. The test is dec > 90° − φ.
- Saying circumpolar stars do not move. They circle the pole.

### 6.18 Latitude from Polaris

#### What you need to know

- Find the observer's latitude using Polaris.

#### Explained

Polaris lies within about 0.7° of the north celestial pole, and the altitude of the celestial pole equals the observer's latitude. So measuring the altitude of Polaris gives the latitude, to within a degree. At the North Pole Polaris is overhead (90°); at the equator it is on the horizon (0°). It can be measured with a quadrant, sextant, protractor with a plumb line, or roughly by hand: a fist at arm's length covers about 10°.

For better accuracy, measure its altitude at upper and lower transit and take the mean, or apply a correction from tables, because Polaris circles the pole in a small circle.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying Polaris is exactly at the pole.
- Trying this from the southern hemisphere, where Polaris is below the horizon.

### 6.19 Naked-eye techniques

#### What you need to know

- Dark adaptation and averted vision.

#### Explained

Dark adaptation: in darkness the pupil widens (in seconds) and, more importantly, the rod cells in the retina become much more sensitive as a chemical (rhodopsin) builds up, over about 20 to 30 minutes. A single glance at a white light undoes this. So observers wait 20 to 30 minutes in the dark before observing faint objects and use dim red torches, because red light affects the rods least.

Averted vision: the centre of the retina (fovea) has mostly cone cells, good for colour and detail in bright light but poor in dim light. Rods, which are more sensitive, are concentrated away from the centre. Looking slightly to one side of a faint object (towards the nose) places its light on rod-rich areas, so it becomes visible. Used for faint stars, nebulae, galaxies and fine detail on planets.

#### Common mistakes

- Using a white torch to read a chart.
- Saying colours are seen best in dim light. Rods see no colour, so faint objects appear grey.

### 6.20 and 6.21 Factors affecting visibility and the naked-eye Milky Way

#### What you need to know

- Factors affecting visibility: rising and setting, seeing conditions, weather conditions, landscape.
- The appearance of the Milky Way from Earth with the naked eye.

#### Explained

- Rising and setting: an object must be above the horizon during darkness; near the horizon it is dimmed (extinction) and blurred. Some objects are only up in daytime at certain seasons, or never rise from your latitude.
- Seeing conditions: how steady the atmosphere is (1.6). Poor seeing causes twinkling and blurs detail. Transparency (clearness of the air: haze, dust, humidity) is also important.
- Weather: cloud blocks observations; rain, wind (shakes instruments), dew forming on optics; clear nights after a cold front often have excellent transparency.
- Landscape: hills, trees and buildings block the horizon; local lights; observing site altitude (higher sites are above some haze and cloud).
- Others: moonlight brightens the sky; light pollution; twilight.

The Milky Way to the naked eye, from a dark site, is a faint, irregular, misty band of light stretching right across the sky. It is the combined light of billions of distant stars in the disc of our own Galaxy, seen edge-on from inside. It has dark lanes and patches, such as the Great Rift through Cygnus, caused by clouds of dust blocking light. It is brightest towards Sagittarius (the direction of the Galactic centre, low in the south from the UK in summer) and runs through Cygnus and Cassiopeia (high in autumn). It cannot be seen from light-polluted towns. Through binoculars it breaks into countless individual stars (15.1).

#### Common mistakes

- Calling the Milky Way a cloud or nebula. It is the light of many stars in our Galaxy's disc.
- Saying it is the same brightness all the way round. It is brightest towards the Galactic centre.

## Topic 7 Early models of the Solar System (Paper 1)

People have watched the sky for thousands of years to keep time, farm and mark sacred days. This topic covers how ancient civilisations used solar and lunar cycles, why the alignments of ancient monuments have shifted, Earth-centred models and Ptolemy's epicycles, and the scale of the Solar System with the units AU, light year and parsec.

### 7.1 Ancient uses of solar and lunar cycles

#### What you need to know

- How detailed observations of the Sun and Moon were used by ancient civilisations around the world for agricultural systems, religious systems, time and calendar systems, and alignments of ancient monuments.

#### Explained

- Agriculture: farmers needed to know when to sow and harvest. In ancient Egypt, the first dawn rising of Sirius (its heliacal rising, the first morning it is seen after being hidden near the Sun) came just before the annual Nile flood that watered the fields. In many cultures, the rising of the Pleiades marked the start of planting or harvest seasons. Solstices and equinoxes marked the turning of the seasons.
- Religion: many festivals were fixed by the Sun or Moon: midwinter festivals at the December solstice when the Sun "returns"; festivals at full or new Moons; in Islam the months begin with the sighting of the new crescent Moon, so Ramadan moves through the solar year; Easter is set by the first full Moon after the March equinox. Some cultures associated gods with the Sun, Moon and planets, and priests kept records of eclipses and planetary positions (Babylonian clay tablets).
- Time and calendars: the day (Sun), the month (lunar phases, about 29.5 days) and the year (seasons, about 365.25 days) do not divide into each other neatly. Lunar calendars (12 months of 29 or 30 days, 354 days) drift against the seasons; solar calendars (Egyptian 365-day year; the Julian calendar adding a leap day every 4 years; the Gregorian reform of 1582) keep in step with the seasons; lunisolar calendars (Babylonian, Hebrew, Chinese) add an extra month about every three years to stay in line. The Maya kept extremely accurate cycles of Venus.
- Monument alignments: many ancient structures are aligned with sunrise or sunset at the solstices: Stonehenge (midsummer sunrise along the axis, midwinter sunset opposite), Newgrange in Ireland (a light-box lets the midwinter sunrise shine down a passage into the inner chamber), Chichen Itza (a serpent-shaped shadow on the pyramid steps at the equinoxes), the Egyptian pyramids (sides aligned to the cardinal points with great precision, probably using stars).

#### Common mistakes

- Treating astrology and astronomy as the same. Ancient sky-watching often had both purposes, but the exam asks about the observations and their practical uses.
- Assuming a lunar calendar keeps in step with the seasons.

### 7.2 Alignments changed by precession

#### What you need to know

- The current alignment of ancient monuments with the sky differs from the original alignment, because of the precession of the Earth's axis.

#### Explained

Precession (3.6) changes the positions of the stars relative to the celestial poles and equator over thousands of years: the rising and setting points of a given star on the horizon shift. So a passage built to point at the rising of a particular star 4500 years ago no longer points at it. (Alignments with the solstice Sun change only very slightly over this time, because they depend on the tilt of the axis, which varies little, not on precession.)

Archaeoastronomers use this: if a structure seems to be aligned with a star, they calculate in which century that star rose or set in the right direction. That date can be checked against archaeological dating. For example, narrow shafts in the Great Pyramid of Giza have been suggested to point at stars that were in those positions around 2500 BCE; a passage once pointing to the pole star then (Thuban) now does not, as the pole has moved towards Polaris.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying monuments have moved. The monuments are fixed; the sky has shifted due to precession.

### 7.3 and 7.4 Geocentric models and epicycles

#### What you need to know

- Early geocentric (Earth-centred) models of the Solar System.
- The advantage of adding epicycles, as described by Ptolemy.

#### Explained

Geocentric model: the Earth is fixed at the centre of the Universe; the Moon, Mercury, Venus, the Sun, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn move round it, each on its own sphere or circle, with the sphere of the fixed stars outermost. Aristotle (4th century BCE) taught this. It seemed natural: we do not feel the Earth moving; objects fall towards the Earth; the stars show no parallax shift (which would be expected if the Earth moved, but the stars are too far for the naked eye to detect it). Circles were thought to be perfect motion for heavenly bodies.

Problems: simple circular motion around the Earth cannot explain (a) the retrograde loops of the planets (5.5), (b) the changing brightness of planets such as Mars (suggesting their distance changes), or (c) the unequal lengths of the seasons.

Ptolemy's model (about 150 CE, in his book the Almagest):

- Each planet moves on a small circle called an epicycle, whose centre moves round the Earth on a larger circle called the deferent.
- When the planet's motion on the epicycle is opposite to the epicycle's motion along the deferent, the planet appears to move backwards: this reproduces retrograde motion. It also explains the change in brightness, because the planet's distance from the Earth varies as it goes round the epicycle.
- Ptolemy also offset the centre of the deferents from the Earth and used an "equant" point about which motion was uniform, to match the planets' changing speeds.

Advantage: the model predicted planetary positions well enough for calendars, navigation and astrology, and it lasted about 1400 years. Disadvantages: it was very complicated, and added more circles as observations improved; it did not explain why the inner planets always stay near the Sun, except by special rules. The heliocentric model (Topic 8) explained the same observations more simply.

Aristarchus had proposed a Sun-centred model as early as the 3rd century BCE, but it was not widely accepted.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying Ptolemy's model was inaccurate. It predicted positions well; it was rejected because a simpler, physically correct model replaced it.
- Saying epicycles were used to explain the phases of the Moon.

### 7.5, 7.6 and 11.9 The scale of the Solar System and distance units

#### What you need to know

- Use information about the scale of the Solar System.
- Use the astronomical unit (1 AU = 1.5 × 10⁸ km), the light year (l.y.) and the parsec (pc). (Point 11.9 in Paper 2 repeats this.)

#### Explained

- Astronomical unit (AU): the mean distance between the Earth and the Sun, 1.5 × 10⁸ km. Used for distances within the Solar System. Jupiter is 5.2 AU from the Sun, Neptune 30 AU.
- Light year (l.y.): the distance light travels in one year, 9.5 × 10¹² km (about 63 000 AU). Used for distances to stars and galaxies. Light from the Sun takes about 8.3 minutes to reach us; from the nearest star (Proxima Centauri), 4.2 years.
- Parsec (pc): the distance at which a star would show a parallax angle of one arcsecond (13.11), 3.1 × 10¹³ km = 3.26 l.y. Professional astronomers use parsecs, kiloparsecs (kpc) and megaparsecs (Mpc).

Scale: the planets' mean distances from the Sun (data sheet): Mercury 0.38 AU, Venus 0.72, Earth 1.0, Mars 1.5, Jupiter 5.2, Saturn 9.5, Uranus 19.1, Neptune 30.0; dwarf planets Ceres 2.8 (in the asteroid belt), Pluto 39.5, Eris 67.8. The inner planets are crowded close to the Sun; the outer planets are spread far apart. The Oort Cloud may extend to about 100 000 AU (around 1.5 l.y.), a third of the way to the nearest star.

#### Worked example

1. Convert Saturn's distance to km: 9.5 × 1.5 × 10⁸ = 1.4 × 10⁹ km.
2. How long does light take to travel from the Sun to Neptune? Distance = 30 × 1.5 × 10¹¹ m = 4.5 × 10¹² m. Time = 4.5 × 10¹² ÷ 3.0 × 10⁸ = 15 000 s ≈ 4.2 hours.
3. How many AU in one light year? 9.5 × 10¹² ÷ 1.5 × 10⁸ ≈ 63 000 AU.
4. A star is 8.6 l.y. away. In parsecs: 8.6 ÷ 3.26 = 2.6 pc.

#### Common mistakes

- Thinking a light year is a time. It is a distance.
- Converting km to m incorrectly in light-time calculations (multiply km by 1000).
- Confusing which is bigger: 1 pc = 3.26 l.y., so the parsec is larger.

## Topic 8 Planetary motion and gravity (Paper 1)

Between the 1540s and the 1680s, astronomers moved from an Earth-centred to a Sun-centred Solar System and then explained why planets move as they do. This topic covers the roles of Copernicus, Brahe and Kepler, Kepler's three laws, the names for the near and far points of orbits, using Kepler's third law, and Newton's law of gravitation in words.

Equation (given): Kepler's third law, T² ÷ r³ = a constant, where T is the orbital period and r the mean orbital radius.

### 8.1 and 8.2 From geocentric to heliocentric: Copernicus, Brahe and Kepler

#### What you need to know

- How the observations of Tycho Brahe contributed to the change from a geocentric to a heliocentric model.
- How the mathematical modelling of Copernicus and Kepler contributed.

#### Explained

Nicolaus Copernicus (published 1543) proposed a heliocentric model: the Sun at the centre, with the Earth as a planet orbiting it and spinning daily on its axis. This explained retrograde motion naturally (the Earth overtaking outer planets, 5.5), explained why Mercury and Venus never stray far from the Sun (their orbits are inside ours), and gave the order and relative distances of the planets from their periods. But Copernicus kept perfectly circular orbits, so his model still needed some small epicycles to match observations and was not more accurate than Ptolemy's.

Tycho Brahe (1546 to 1601) built large, precise naked-eye instruments (before telescopes) and measured planetary and star positions for over 20 years with an accuracy of about 1 to 2 arcminutes, far better than anyone before. He observed a "new star" (supernova, 1572) and a comet (1577) and showed they were beyond the Moon, so the heavens were not unchanging. He proposed his own hybrid model (the Sun and Moon orbit the Earth, the other planets orbit the Sun). His most important contribution was his data.

Johannes Kepler (1571 to 1630), Brahe's assistant, used Brahe's accurate data on Mars. No circular orbit fitted the observations: the error was about 8 arcminutes, too big to ignore given Brahe's accuracy. Kepler found that an ellipse with the Sun at one focus fitted perfectly. He published his three laws (1609 and 1619). The heliocentric model with elliptical orbits was simpler and far more accurate than any model before.

(Galileo's telescope observations, which also supported heliocentrism, are covered in 11.24.)

#### Common mistakes

- Saying Copernicus discovered elliptical orbits. He used circles; Kepler discovered ellipses.
- Saying Brahe supported the heliocentric model. He had his own model; his data made Kepler's work possible.

### 8.3 Gravity and stable elliptical orbits

#### What you need to know

- The role of gravity in creating stable elliptical orbits.

#### Explained

A planet moving through space would travel in a straight line if no force acted (Newton's first law). The Sun's gravity pulls it towards the Sun all the time. The combination of the planet's sideways motion and the inward pull bends its path into an orbit. If the planet's speed is just right for its distance, the orbit is a circle; otherwise it is an ellipse.

On an elliptical orbit, as the planet moves towards the Sun, gravity speeds it up; as it moves away, gravity slows it down. At the closest point it is moving fastest and swings round and out again; at the furthest point it is slowest and falls back in. The orbit repeats, so it is stable. If the planet were too slow it would spiral in; if it were moving faster than escape speed it would leave on an open path. (The planets' gentle pulls on each other cause only small, slow changes, 12.1.)

#### Common mistakes

- Saying there is no gravity in orbit. Gravity is what keeps the planet in orbit.
- Saying the Sun is at the centre of the ellipse. It is at one focus.

### 8.4 and 8.5 Kepler's laws and orbit terms

#### What you need to know

- Kepler's three laws of planetary motion.
- Aphelion and perihelion (orbits around the Sun); apogee and perigee (orbits around the Earth).

#### Explained

1. First law: each planet moves in an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. An ellipse has two foci; the more stretched the ellipse, the further apart they are. Most planetary orbits are nearly circular.
2. Second law: a line joining the planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times. So the planet moves fastest when nearest the Sun and slowest when furthest away.
3. Third law: the square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the mean distance from the Sun: T² ∝ r³, or T² ÷ r³ = constant for all the planets of the same star.

Orbit terms:

- Perihelion: the point of an orbit around the Sun closest to the Sun. Aphelion: the furthest point. (The Earth is at perihelion in early January, about 147 million km, and aphelion in early July, about 152 million km.)
- Perigee: the point of an orbit around the Earth closest to the Earth (for the Moon or artificial satellites). Apogee: the furthest point.

#### Common mistakes

- Thinking the second law says equal distances in equal times. It is equal areas.
- Mixing up perihelion (Sun) and perigee (Earth).

### 8.6 and 8.7 Using Kepler's third law

#### What you need to know

- Use T² ÷ r³ = a constant.
- The constant depends inversely on the mass of the central body.

#### Explained

If T is measured in Earth years and r in AU, then for the Earth T = 1 and r = 1, so the constant is 1 for everything orbiting the Sun: T² = r³. This makes calculations simple.

The same law applies to moons orbiting a planet, or satellites orbiting the Earth, but with a different constant. The constant is inversely proportional to the mass of the central body: a more massive central body pulls harder, so objects at the same distance orbit faster (shorter T), giving a smaller T² ÷ r³. This allows astronomers to find the mass of a planet by timing its moons, or the mass of a star from its planets.

#### Worked example

1. Mars has r = 1.5 AU. T² = 1.5³ = 3.375, so T = √3.375 = 1.84 years (data sheet: 1.9).
2. A comet has a period of 76 years. r³ = 76² = 5776, so r = ∛5776 ≈ 17.9 AU.
3. A moon of Jupiter has T = 1.77 days at r = 422 000 km. Another moon has r = 1 070 000 km. Since T² ÷ r³ is the same for both: T₂ = T₁ × (r₂ ÷ r₁)^1.5 = 1.77 × (2.54)^1.5 = 1.77 × 4.05 ≈ 7.2 days.
4. A planet orbits a star 4 times the mass of the Sun at 1 AU. The constant is 1/4 of the Sun's, so T² = r³ ÷ 4 = 0.25 and T = 0.5 years.

#### Common mistakes

- Squaring r and cubing T. It is T squared and r cubed.
- Using the solar-system constant for satellites of the Earth or moons of Jupiter.
- Forgetting to take the square root (or cube root) at the end.

### 8.8 and 8.9 Newton's law of universal gravitation

#### What you need to know

- Newton explained Kepler's laws using his law of universal gravitation.
- The gravitational force between two bodies is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of their separation. (The algebraic form is not needed.)

#### Explained

Isaac Newton (published 1687) proposed that every mass attracts every other mass. The force:

- Is proportional to the product of the two masses: double one mass, double the force; double both, four times the force.
- Is inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres: double the distance, the force falls to 1/4; triple it, 1/9; halve it, 4 times bigger.

Newton showed mathematically that a force like this, together with his laws of motion, makes planets move in ellipses (first law), speed up near the Sun (second law) and obey T² ∝ r³ (third law), with the constant depending on the Sun's mass. So Kepler's laws, which only described the motion, were explained by one physical law. The same force makes an apple fall and holds the Moon in orbit: the law is universal, applying on Earth and throughout the heavens. It later predicted the return of Halley's comet and led to the discovery of Neptune.

#### Worked example

A satellite is moved from 2 Earth radii from the Earth's centre to 6 Earth radii. The distance triples, so the gravitational force becomes 1/3² = 1/9 of its original value.

Two stars of masses M and 3M are at distance d. If the separation halves, the force becomes 2² = 4 times larger.

#### Common mistakes

- Using inverse proportion (1/d) instead of inverse square (1/d²).
- Adding the masses instead of multiplying.

## Topic 9 Exploring the Moon (Paper 2)

Telescopes, spacecraft and astronauts have told us much more about the Moon than the naked eye can. This topic covers the Moon's interior compared with the Earth's, the differences between its near and far sides, how the far side was explored, why getting to the Moon needs rockets, and the theories of the Moon's origin.

### 9.1 The Moon's internal structure compared with the Earth's

#### What you need to know

- The Moon's major internal divisions, in comparison with those of the Earth.

#### Explained

Evidence comes from seismometers left by Apollo astronauts (which recorded moonquakes and impacts), from measurements of the Moon's gravity by orbiting spacecraft, from its average density, and from how it wobbles as it rotates.

| | Earth | Moon |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crust | Thin: 5 to 70 km; granite (continents) and basalt (oceans) | Thicker relative to size: about 30 to 50 km, thicker on the far side; anorthosite highlands with basalt in the maria |
| Mantle | About 2900 km thick, solid but flowing slowly (convection drives plate tectonics) | Most of the Moon's volume; rigid and cold in the upper part; a partly molten zone near the base |
| Core | Large: about 55% of Earth's radius; liquid outer core of iron and nickel generates a strong magnetic field; solid inner core | Very small: about 20% of the Moon's radius; iron-rich, probably with a solid inner core and a fluid outer layer; no global magnetic field today |
| Mean density | About 5.5 g/cm³ | About 3.3 g/cm³ (so the Moon has little iron) |
| Activity | Active: plate tectonics, volcanoes, earthquakes | Geologically almost "dead": no plates, no active volcanoes; weak moonquakes |

The Moon is small, so it lost its internal heat much faster than the Earth. Its low density and small core are important evidence for the Giant Impact Hypothesis (9.5).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Moon has no core.
- Saying the Moon has a magnetic field like Earth's. It has only weak patches of magnetised rock.

### 9.2 and 9.3 The near and far sides

#### What you need to know

- The major differences in appearance between the near and far sides of the Moon.
- How information has been gathered about the far side.

#### Explained

| Near side | Far side |
| --- | --- |
| About 31% covered by dark maria | Very few maria (about 1 to 2%) |
| Large smooth lava plains | Heavily cratered bright highlands almost everywhere |
| Thinner crust | Thicker crust, which made it harder for lava to reach the surface |
| | Contains the South Pole-Aitken basin, one of the largest impact basins in the Solar System |

The reason for the difference is linked to the crust: lava from the interior could break through the thinner near-side crust and flood the large impact basins, but rarely reached the surface through the thicker far-side crust.

Because the Moon's rotation is synchronous (2.7), the far side can never be seen from Earth (except the small extra areas shown by libration, 2.8). It was first seen in 1959 when the Soviet probe Luna 3 flew round the Moon and sent back photographs. Since then:

- Crewed Apollo missions in lunar orbit (from Apollo 8, 1968) photographed it directly; astronauts were the first humans to see it.
- Orbiters such as Lunar Orbiter, Clementine, Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) and GRAIL mapped the whole surface in detail, measured heights with lasers, and mapped variations in gravity to find the crust's thickness.
- Landers and rovers: China's Chang'e 4 made the first soft landing on the far side (2019), using a relay satellite positioned beyond the Moon to send signals to Earth, since the Moon blocks direct radio contact. Chang'e 6 returned the first far-side samples (2024).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the far side cannot be photographed. It has been mapped completely by spacecraft.
- Explaining the lack of maria by the far side "facing away from the Earth's gravity" without mentioning crust thickness.

### 9.4 Escape velocity and rockets

#### What you need to know

- A spacecraft travelling to the Moon must reach the Earth's escape velocity (or nearly so), and the energy needed can only be provided by rockets.

#### Explained

Escape velocity is the minimum speed an object needs at the surface to escape a body's gravity completely without further propulsion. For the Earth it is about 11.2 km/s (about 40 000 km/h). A spacecraft going to the Moon must reach nearly this speed: it does not need to escape the Earth's gravity entirely (the Moon is still within the Earth's gravitational influence), but it needs almost the same energy.

Why rockets: a rocket carries its own fuel and oxidiser, so it works in the near-vacuum of space and does not need air (jet engines need oxygen from the air). It works by pushing hot exhaust gases out of the back at high speed; by Newton's third law, the gases push the rocket forwards. Enormous amounts of energy are needed to lift the spacecraft's mass to that speed, so most of a launch rocket's mass is propellant. Multi-stage rockets drop empty fuel tanks and engines as they go, so less mass needs to be accelerated. Launching eastwards, closer to the equator, uses the Earth's rotation to give a little extra speed.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying spacecraft need rockets because there is no gravity in space.
- Giving escape velocity in m/s without converting correctly: 11.2 km/s = 11 200 m/s.

### 9.5 Theories of the Moon's origin

#### What you need to know

- The Giant Impact Hypothesis, and alternatives including Capture Theory and Co-accretion Theory.

#### Explained

Giant Impact Hypothesis (the most widely accepted): about 4.5 billion years ago, soon after the Earth formed, a Mars-sized body (sometimes called Theia) struck the young Earth a glancing blow. The impact melted and vaporised much of the impactor and the Earth's outer layers. A disc of debris, mostly from the mantles, was thrown into orbit around the Earth and gathered together (accreted) to form the Moon. The iron cores of the two bodies mostly merged with the Earth.

Evidence for it:

- The Moon has a small iron core and low density, matching formation from mantle-rock debris.
- Moon rocks have almost identical oxygen isotope ratios to Earth rocks, suggesting a shared source.
- Moon rocks are depleted in volatile elements (easily evaporated substances such as water), as expected after extreme heating.
- Evidence of a global "magma ocean" early in the Moon's history, consistent with fast formation from hot debris.
- Computer simulations show such an impact can produce a moon of the right size and the Earth-Moon system's angular momentum (spin and orbit).

Alternatives:

- Capture Theory: the Moon formed elsewhere in the Solar System and was captured by the Earth's gravity as it passed. Problems: capture of such a large body needs a very special situation, such as a third body or the Earth's early atmosphere to slow it down; and it would not explain the matching isotopes.
- Co-accretion Theory: the Earth and Moon formed together, side by side, from the same cloud of material orbiting the Sun. Problems: it would give the Moon an iron core and density similar to the Earth's, which it does not have; and it does not easily explain the system's angular momentum.
- (Fission Theory, in which a fast-spinning Earth flung off the Moon, is an older idea, also rejected.)

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Giant Impact Hypothesis has been proved. It is the best-supported theory, but some questions remain (for example, why the Moon and Earth are so similar if much of the Moon should come from Theia).
- Mixing up co-accretion (formed together) and capture (formed elsewhere).

## Topic 10 Solar astronomy (Paper 2)

The Sun is the only star close enough to study in detail. This topic covers safe telescopic observation, the Sun's internal layers and how energy is made and carried out, the proton-proton cycle, the solar atmosphere, sunspots and the solar cycle, finding the Sun's rotation period, the Sun at different wavelengths, the solar wind and its effects, and the Earth's magnetosphere.

Data: temperature of the photosphere 5800 K.

### 10.1 Safe solar observing with telescopes

#### What you need to know

- Methods of safe observation: telescopic projection and the H-alpha filter.

#### Explained

A telescope or binoculars concentrate sunlight, so looking at the Sun through one would cause instant, permanent blindness. Finder scopes must be capped.

- Telescopic projection: point the telescope at the Sun without looking through it (align it by its shadow). Hold a white card some distance behind the eyepiece. A bright, magnified image of the Sun forms on the card, showing sunspots and limb darkening. Use a simple, cheap eyepiece (heat can damage cemented eyepieces) and never leave the telescope unattended. Moving the card further away gives a larger, dimmer image. A shade around the tube improves contrast.
- Full-aperture white-light filter: a certified filter fitted securely over the front of the telescope, blocking almost all the light before it enters. Shows the photosphere and sunspots.
- H-alpha filter: a special narrow-band filter that only lets through a very narrow range of red light at 656.3 nm, emitted by hydrogen. It shows the chromosphere: prominences at the limb, filaments on the disc, flares and fine structure, which are invisible in white light. H-alpha telescopes are expensive specialist instruments.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying sunglasses or a filter that screws into the eyepiece make telescope viewing safe. Eyepiece filters can crack from the heat.
- Saying H-alpha shows the photosphere. It shows the chromosphere.

### 10.2, 10.3 and 10.4 Inside the Sun and the proton-proton cycle

#### What you need to know

- The location and relative temperatures of the Sun's core, radiative zone, convective zone and photosphere.
- The role of each in energy production and transfer.
- The proton-proton cycle, the main fusion process in the Sun.

#### Explained

| Layer | Location | Temperature | Role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Core | Centre to about 0.25 of the radius | About 15 million K | Nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium releases energy. Extremely high temperature and density are needed |
| Radiative zone | About 0.25 to 0.7 of the radius | About 7 million K falling to 2 million K | Energy is carried outwards as radiation (photons), which are absorbed and re-emitted countless times in random directions, so it takes tens of thousands to over 100 000 years to cross |
| Convective zone | About 0.7 of the radius to the surface | About 2 million K falling to about 6000 K | Energy is carried by convection: hot gas rises, cools at the top and sinks. The tops of convection cells are seen as granulation on the photosphere |
| Photosphere | The visible surface, a few hundred km thick | About 5800 K | Emits most of the visible light that leaves the Sun; where sunspots and granules are seen |

The proton-proton cycle: hydrogen nuclei (protons) are fused into helium in steps:

1. Two protons fuse to form a deuterium nucleus (heavy hydrogen: one proton and one neutron). One proton turns into a neutron, giving off a positron and a neutrino.
2. The deuterium nucleus fuses with another proton to form helium-3 (two protons, one neutron), giving off a gamma-ray photon.
3. Two helium-3 nuclei fuse to form helium-4 (two protons, two neutrons), releasing two protons.

Overall: four hydrogen nuclei become one helium-4 nucleus. The helium-4 nucleus has slightly less mass than the four protons; this missing mass is released as energy (E = mc²), carried by gamma rays, positrons (which annihilate with electrons, giving more gamma rays) and neutrinos. The Sun converts about 4 million tonnes of mass into energy every second.

Fusion requires very high temperature (so nuclei move fast enough to overcome the electrostatic repulsion between positive charges) and high density (so collisions are frequent).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Sun burns hydrogen. It is nuclear fusion, not chemical burning.
- Saying the photosphere is the hottest layer. The core is hottest; the corona above is also far hotter than the photosphere.
- Saying light from the core reaches us in 8 minutes. That is the time from the surface; energy takes a very long time to cross the radiative zone.

### 10.5 The solar atmosphere

#### What you need to know

- The location, temperature and relative density of the chromosphere and corona.

#### Explained

- Chromosphere: a thin layer (about 2000 km) just above the photosphere. Temperature rises from about 4000 K to about 20 000 K going outwards. Much lower density than the photosphere. It glows pinkish-red (from hydrogen-alpha light), seen briefly as a red ring at the start and end of totality in a solar eclipse, or with an H-alpha filter. Shows spicules (jets), prominences and flares.
- Corona: the Sun's outer atmosphere, extending millions of km into space and merging into the solar wind. Temperature 1 to 2 million K or more, far hotter than the photosphere, although why it is so hot is still being researched (magnetic fields are thought to carry energy into it). Extremely low density, so although very hot it emits little visible light: it is only seen as a pearly white halo during a total eclipse, or with a coronagraph, or in X-rays and ultraviolet.

Order of density: photosphere > chromosphere > corona.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the corona is cooler because it is further from the core.
- Saying the corona is bright. It is very faint compared with the photosphere.

### 10.6, 10.7 and 10.8 Sunspots, solar rotation and the solar cycle

#### What you need to know

- The structure, origin and evolution of sunspots.
- Use sunspot data to determine the mean solar rotation period.
- Use sunspot data related to the solar cycle.

#### Explained

Structure: a sunspot appears as a dark central umbra surrounded by a lighter, streaky penumbra. Sunspots are about 1500 to 2000 K cooler than the surrounding photosphere (around 4000 K in the umbra). They are not actually dark; they look dark only by contrast with the brighter surroundings. Sizes range from about 1000 km to over 100 000 km (many times the Earth's diameter).

Origin: the Sun rotates faster at its equator (about 25 days) than near its poles (about 35 days): differential rotation. This winds up magnetic field lines inside the Sun until loops of strong field burst up through the photosphere. Where a loop emerges and re-enters, the strong magnetic field slows down convection, so less heat reaches the surface there, giving a cooler, darker region. Sunspots usually appear in pairs or groups of opposite magnetic polarity.

Evolution: a spot group grows over days, may last from hours to months (usually a week or two), then decays and disappears. Over a solar cycle, the first spots appear at high latitudes (about 30 to 40° north and south), and as the cycle progresses new spots appear closer and closer to the equator. A plot of spot latitude against time therefore looks like a series of "butterfly wings" (the butterfly diagram).

Solar cycle: the number of sunspots rises and falls over about 11 years, from solar minimum (few or no spots) to solar maximum (many spots, more flares and coronal mass ejections, more aurorae), and back. The magnetic polarity of the Sun reverses each cycle, so the full magnetic cycle is about 22 years. Using sunspot data: from a graph of sunspot number against year, read off the times of successive maxima (or minima) and find the mean interval.

Solar rotation period (task A6 and B6): track a sunspot across the disc on daily projections or photographs. Measure how far it moves across the disc each day. A spot near the centre of the disc moves about 13° of solar longitude per day (14° at the equator). Either:

- Time how long a spot takes to cross from one edge to the other (about 13.5 days, half a rotation) and double it; or
- Find the angle moved per day and divide 360° by it.

This gives the synodic rotation period (about 27 days for mid-latitude spots seen from Earth). Because the Earth moves along its orbit in the same direction, the true (sidereal) rotation period relative to the stars is shorter, about 25 days at the equator.

#### Worked example

A sunspot near the solar equator moves 52° of solar longitude in 4 days. Rate = 13° per day. Rotation period = 360 ÷ 13 ≈ 27.7 days (as seen from Earth).

Sunspot maxima occurred in 1989, 2000, 2014 and 2024 (approximately). Intervals: 11, 14 and 10 years. Mean = 35 ÷ 3 ≈ 11.7 years.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying sunspots are holes or are cold. They are about 4000 K, cooler only than their surroundings.
- Saying the Sun rotates as a solid body.
- Forgetting that a spot near the limb appears to move slowly and look squashed because of the Sun's curvature; use spots near the centre for measurements.

### 10.9 The Sun at different wavelengths

#### What you need to know

- How the Sun looks when observed in radiation from different parts of the electromagnetic spectrum.

#### Explained

Different wavelengths come from different layers at different temperatures, so each shows different structures.

| Radiation | What is seen |
| --- | --- |
| Radio | The corona and chromosphere; radio bursts from flares |
| Infrared | Slightly below and at the photosphere; cooler features |
| Visible (white light) | The photosphere: sunspots, granulation, faculae, limb darkening |
| H-alpha (visible red) | The chromosphere: prominences, filaments, flares, plage |
| Ultraviolet | The upper chromosphere and lower corona: bright active regions, loops of hot gas following magnetic fields |
| X-ray | The corona: very hot gas over active regions, flares, dark coronal holes (where the solar wind escapes) |

Ultraviolet and X-ray observations must be made from space (the atmosphere absorbs them, 13.22), by observatories such as SOHO and the Solar Dynamics Observatory.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying sunspots are seen in X-rays in the same way. In X-rays, active regions above spots look bright.

### 10.10, 10.11 and 10.12 The solar wind, its effects and the magnetosphere

#### What you need to know

- The nature, composition and origin of the solar wind.
- Its main effects: aurorae, comet tails, geomagnetic storms, and effects on satellites, aircraft and crewed missions.
- The shape and position of the Earth's magnetosphere, including the Van Allen Belts.

#### Explained

The solar wind is a continuous stream of charged particles (a plasma) flowing out from the Sun in all directions at about 400 to 800 km/s. It is mainly electrons and protons, with some helium nuclei (alpha particles) and traces of heavier ions. It originates in the corona: the corona is so hot that particles move fast enough to escape the Sun's gravity, especially from coronal holes where magnetic field lines open out into space. Coronal mass ejections (CMEs) are huge bursts of extra material, more frequent at solar maximum.

Effects:

- Aurorae: when solar wind particles reach the Earth, some are channelled by the magnetic field down towards the polar regions. They collide with oxygen and nitrogen atoms in the upper atmosphere (about 100 to 300 km up), which then emit light: green and red from oxygen, blue and purple from nitrogen. Aurorae form ovals around the magnetic poles, and are seen further from the poles after strong solar activity.
- Comet tails: the solar wind pushes the ionised gas from a comet into a straight blue ion (plasma) tail that always points directly away from the Sun, whichever way the comet is moving (11.2).
- Geomagnetic storms: a CME hitting the magnetosphere causes rapid changes in the Earth's magnetic field. These can induce large currents in long power lines and pipelines, causing blackouts (as in Quebec in 1989); disrupt radio communications and GPS; and make compasses unreliable.
- Satellites: charged particles can damage electronics and solar panels; the heated, expanded upper atmosphere increases drag on low-orbit satellites, making them lose height.
- Aircraft: high-altitude polar flights receive increased radiation doses, and may be rerouted during storms; radio communication can black out.
- Crewed missions: astronauts outside the protection of the magnetosphere (for example going to the Moon or Mars) risk dangerous radiation doses from solar particle events, so they need shelters and warnings.

The magnetosphere is the region of space around the Earth controlled by the Earth's magnetic field. The solar wind compresses it on the day side (the boundary, the magnetopause, is about 10 Earth radii towards the Sun, with a bow shock in front where the solar wind slows suddenly) and stretches it into a long magnetotail on the night side, extending hundreds of Earth radii away from the Sun. So it is shaped like a teardrop or comet. It deflects most solar wind particles around the Earth, protecting the atmosphere and life.

The Van Allen Belts are two doughnut-shaped zones within the magnetosphere, around the Earth's equator, where charged particles are trapped by the magnetic field: an inner belt (mainly protons, about 1000 to 6000 km up) and an outer belt (mainly electrons, about 13 000 to 60 000 km up). Spacecraft must be shielded or pass through quickly.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying aurorae are caused by sunlight reflecting off ice.
- Saying the magnetosphere is a symmetrical sphere. It is squashed on the Sun side and has a long tail.
- Saying the solar wind is made of neutral atoms. It is charged particles.

### 11.1, 11.6, 11.8, 11.9 and 11.11 Bodies of the Solar System

#### What you need to know

- Use data about the names and relative locations of planets, dwarf planets and Small Solar System Objects (asteroids, meteoroids and comets).
- The principal characteristics of the planets: relative size, relative mass, surface temperature, atmospheric composition, presence of moons and ring systems.
- Use information about the size of the Solar System; use the AU, light year and parsec (see also Topic 7).

#### Explained

Definitions (International Astronomical Union):

- Small Solar System Objects (SSSOs): everything else orbiting the Sun: asteroids (rocky or metallic bodies, mostly in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter), comets (icy bodies) and meteoroids (small fragments).

Patterns: the four inner terrestrial planets are small, dense and rocky with few or no moons and no rings; the four outer giant planets are large, low-density, mostly gas and ice, with many moons and ring systems. Temperature generally falls with distance, except Venus, which is hotter than Mercury because of its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere.

Size: Neptune is 30 AU from the Sun; the Kuiper Belt extends to about 50 AU; the heliosphere's edge is over 100 AU; the Oort Cloud perhaps 2000 to 100 000 AU. Light takes about 4 hours to reach Neptune and over a year to reach the outer Oort Cloud.

Most planets, the asteroid belt and the Kuiper Belt objects orbit close to the plane of the ecliptic (within a few degrees; Mercury about 7°, Pluto about 17°), and all planets orbit in the same direction. This flatness is evidence that they formed from a flat, spinning disc (12.3). The Oort Cloud is an exception: it is a spherical shell, so long-period comets arrive from all directions.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling Pluto a planet. It is a dwarf planet (since 2006).
- Saying Mercury is the hottest planet. Venus is, because of its greenhouse atmosphere.
- Saying only Saturn has rings. All four giant planets do.

### 11.2, 11.3, 11.4 and 11.5 Comets, the Kuiper Belt, the Oort Cloud and the heliosphere

#### What you need to know

- The structure of comets: nucleus, coma and tails.
- Orbits of short-period comets and their origin in the Kuiper Belt.
- Orbits of long-period comets and their origin in the Oort Cloud.
- The location and nature of the Kuiper Belt, Oort Cloud and heliosphere.

#### Explained

Structure of a comet:

- Nucleus: a solid body a few km across of ice (water, carbon dioxide, ammonia, methane) mixed with dust and rock: a "dirty snowball". Very dark surface.
- Coma: as the comet approaches the Sun (within a few AU), heat makes the ices sublime (turn straight to gas), releasing gas and dust into a huge glowing cloud around the nucleus, which can be larger than the Sun.
- Tails: two kinds. The dust tail is curved, yellowish-white, made of dust pushed away gently by radiation pressure of sunlight, and lags behind along the orbit. The ion (gas or plasma) tail is straight and bluish, made of ionised gas swept out by the solar wind; it points directly away from the Sun. Tails can be millions of km long. Because both are pushed by the Sun, a comet moving away from the Sun travels tail first.

Orbits:

- Short-period comets have periods of less than 200 years, orbit fairly close to the ecliptic, mostly in the same direction as the planets, and have elliptical orbits reaching out to around the outer planets or the Kuiper Belt. Most come from the Kuiper Belt (and the scattered disc beyond it), deflected inwards by Neptune's gravity. Example: Halley's comet (76 years), 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko (6.4 years).
- Long-period comets have periods of thousands to millions of years, very elongated orbits, and come from all directions and at any angle to the ecliptic. They come from the Oort Cloud, nudged inwards by passing stars or by the Galaxy's tidal pull.

Regions:

- Kuiper Belt: a flat, doughnut-shaped belt of icy bodies beyond Neptune, from about 30 to 50 AU, close to the plane of the ecliptic. Contains dwarf planets such as Pluto and Makemake and many smaller objects, leftovers from the formation of the Solar System.
- Oort Cloud: a hypothetical, roughly spherical cloud of trillions of icy bodies surrounding the Solar System at roughly 2000 to 100 000 AU. Never directly observed: its existence is inferred from the orbits of long-period comets.
- Heliosphere: the huge bubble in interstellar space blown by the solar wind. The termination shock is where the solar wind slows sharply (about 90 AU); the heliopause, the boundary where the solar wind meets the interstellar medium, is about 120 AU from the Sun. Voyager 1 crossed it in 2012 and Voyager 2 in 2018.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a comet's tail points behind it. It points away from the Sun.
- Saying the Oort Cloud has been photographed.

### 11.7 Formation and current position of the gas giants

#### What you need to know

- The main theories for how the gas giant planets in our Solar System formed and how they reached their current positions.

#### Explained

Formation: beyond the "snow line" (about 3 to 5 AU from the young Sun), it was cold enough for water and other compounds to freeze into ice, so there was much more solid material. Rocky and icy planetesimals collected quickly into large cores of about 10 Earth masses. Their gravity was then strong enough to pull in huge envelopes of hydrogen and helium gas from the surrounding disc before the young Sun blew the gas away. This is the core accretion model (12.3 describes it and the alternative, disc instability).

Current positions: the giant planets probably did not form exactly where they are now. Models (such as the "Nice model") suggest that gravitational interactions with the remaining planetesimals, and with each other, made the giants migrate: Jupiter moved slightly inward, while Saturn, Uranus and Neptune moved outwards. Uranus and Neptune may have swapped places. This migration scattered many small bodies, perhaps causing the Late Heavy Bombardment of the inner planets and shaping the Kuiper Belt. The "Grand Tack" idea suggests Jupiter moved inwards towards Mars's orbit and then back out again, which would explain why Mars is small and the asteroid belt has little mass.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying giant planets formed close to the Sun and then moved out. Their cores formed beyond the snow line where ices were available.

### 11.10 Meteoroids and meteorites

#### What you need to know

- The origin and structure of meteoroids and meteorites.

#### Explained

Meteoroids are small rocky or metallic particles in space, from dust grains up to about a metre across (larger bodies are asteroids). Most come from comets (dust released as the ice evaporates, forming meteor streams, 5.7) or from collisions between asteroids, and a few are chipped off the Moon or Mars by impacts.

A meteorite is a meteoroid that survives its passage through the atmosphere and reaches the ground. Main types:

- Stony meteorites (most common, over 90% of falls): mainly silicate rock. Chondrites contain small round grains called chondrules and are some of the oldest, least changed material in the Solar System (about 4.6 billion years old); some carbonaceous chondrites contain water and organic compounds.
- Iron meteorites: mostly iron-nickel metal, from the cores of asteroids that were broken apart. Show crystal patterns when cut and etched.
- Stony-iron meteorites: a mixture of metal and rock, from the boundary between core and mantle of a broken asteroid.

They are studied because they show what the early Solar System was made of and how old it is.

#### Common mistakes

- Confusing the three terms: meteoroid (in space), meteor (light in the sky), meteorite (on the ground).

### 11.12 Transits of Venus and the size of the AU

#### What you need to know

- How transits of Venus, as proposed by Halley, were used to find the size of the astronomical unit and so the absolute size of the Solar System.

#### Explained

Kepler's third law gave the relative distances of all the planets in AU, but nobody knew how big an AU was in km. Edmond Halley proposed in 1716 that timing a transit of Venus across the Sun from widely separated places on Earth would give the answer.

The principle is parallax: observers at different latitudes see Venus cross the Sun along slightly different paths (chords), because they view it from slightly different positions. The separation between the paths depends on the distance to Venus and the Sun. Each observer times the transit from start to end; the duration tells them the length of their chord, and hence where it lies on the Sun's disc. Knowing the distance between the observers on Earth (in km), the angular difference between the paths, and Venus's distance relative to the Earth's from Kepler's third law (0.72 AU), the Earth-Sun distance in km can be calculated.

Transits of Venus are rare: they come in pairs 8 years apart, more than a century apart (1761 and 1769; 1874 and 1882; 2004 and 2012; next 2117). Expeditions were sent all over the world in 1761 and 1769 (including Captain Cook's voyage to Tahiti). Results were affected by the "black drop effect", which made exact contact times hard to judge, but gave an AU of about 150 million km, within a few per cent of today's value (now measured accurately by radar).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a transit of Venus can happen at every inferior conjunction. Venus's orbit is tilted, so it usually passes above or below the Sun.

### 11.13 The origin of water on Earth

#### What you need to know

- The main theories for the origin of Earth's water.

#### Explained

The early Earth formed inside the snow line, where it was too hot for ice to be included in large amounts, and it was very hot after formation and the Giant Impact. So where did its oceans come from? Main theories:

- Delivered by comets: icy comets striking the young Earth. But measurements of the ratio of deuterium (heavy hydrogen) to normal hydrogen in most comets (for example comet 67P measured by Rosetta) show it is higher than in Earth's oceans, so comets cannot have supplied most of the water.
- Delivered by asteroids: carbonaceous chondrite asteroids from the outer asteroid belt contain water in their minerals with a deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio close to that of Earth's oceans. This is the favoured source of much of Earth's water.
- From within the Earth (outgassing): water was locked in minerals in the rocks that formed the Earth and was released by volcanoes as the planet cooled, condensing as rain to fill the oceans.

Most scientists think the water came from a mixture of these sources.

#### Common mistakes

- Stating that comets definitely brought Earth's water. Isotope evidence suggests they were not the main source.

### 11.14 to 11.17 The eye, objectives and simple telescopes

#### What you need to know

- The human eye is limited in astronomy by its small aperture and limited sensitivity in low light.
- The objective of a telescope captures and focuses light so the image can be magnified by an eyepiece.
- Convex (converging) lenses and concave (converging) mirrors can collect and focus light.
- Simple telescopes combine an objective (lens or mirror) with an eyepiece.

#### Explained

The eye's pupil is at most about 7 mm across in the dark, so it collects very little light; and the eye cannot store light over a long exposure, as a camera can. So many faint objects are invisible to the eye, and fine detail cannot be resolved.

A telescope has two optical parts:

- The objective (or primary): a large converging lens or concave mirror that collects light from a distant object and brings it to a focus, forming a real image at its focal point. The bigger it is, the more light it collects.
- The eyepiece: a small, short-focal-length converging lens (or set of lenses) that magnifies this image for the eye, like a magnifying glass.

A convex (converging) lens refracts parallel light rays from a distant object so they meet at the focal point. A concave mirror reflects parallel rays to a focus. The distance from the lens or mirror to the focus is its focal length.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the main purpose of a telescope is magnification. Collecting more light (and resolving detail) is more important.
- Calling a concave mirror diverging. A concave mirror converges light.

### 11.18 and 11.25 Telescope designs; reflectors versus refractors

#### What you need to know

- The basic designs of the Galilean refractor, Keplerian refractor, Newtonian reflector and Cassegrain reflector. Detailed ray diagrams are not needed.
- The advantages of reflecting telescopes compared with refractors, in terms of chromatic aberration, very long focal lengths, large apertures and multiple mirrors.

#### Explained

| Design | Objective | Eyepiece and layout | Image |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Galilean refractor | Convex lens at the front of a tube | Concave (diverging) lens, placed before the focus | Upright; narrow field of view; used by Galileo |
| Keplerian refractor | Convex lens | Convex (converging) lens, placed after the focus | Inverted; wider field; the basis of modern refractors |
| Newtonian reflector | Concave (parabolic) primary mirror at the bottom of an open tube | A small flat secondary mirror at 45° near the top reflects light out to an eyepiece in the side of the tube | Inverted; simple and cheap; popular with amateurs |
| Cassegrain reflector | Concave primary mirror with a central hole | A small convex secondary mirror reflects light back down through the hole to an eyepiece or camera behind the primary | Compact tube with long effective focal length; used in most large professional telescopes |

Advantages of reflectors over refractors:

- Chromatic aberration: a lens refracts different colours by different amounts, so they focus at slightly different points, giving coloured fringes round bright objects. Mirrors reflect all colours the same way, so reflectors have no chromatic aberration.
- Very long focal lengths: high magnification needs a long focal length; a refractor with a long focal length needs a very long, awkward tube. Reflector designs such as the Cassegrain fold the light path, so a long focal length fits in a short tube.
- Large apertures: a large lens is very heavy, can only be supported at its edge, and sags under its own weight, distorting the image; the glass must be flawless all the way through. A mirror can be supported across its whole back, only its front surface must be accurate, and it can be made thin and light. The largest refractor is about 1 m; reflectors are over 10 m.
- Multiple mirrors: very large primaries can be built from many smaller hexagonal segments (for example the Keck telescopes, JWST), which is impossible with lenses.

Disadvantages of reflectors: the secondary mirror blocks some light and causes diffraction spikes; open tubes let in dust and air currents; mirrors need recoating and aligning (collimation).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying reflectors are better because they magnify more. Magnification depends on focal lengths.
- Saying the Galilean telescope uses two convex lenses. Its eyepiece is concave.

### 11.19 to 11.23 Telescope properties

#### What you need to know

- Light grasp is directly proportional to the area of the objective, so to the square of its diameter.
- Aperture is related to the diameter of the objective.
- Field of view is the circle of sky visible through the eyepiece, in degrees or arcminutes.
- Resolution is better (finer) for a larger objective, and worse at longer wavelengths.
- Use magnification = fₒ ÷ fₑ.

#### Explained

- Aperture: the diameter of the objective lens or mirror (the "size" of a telescope).
- Light grasp (light-gathering power): how much light the telescope collects, proportional to the area of the objective, so to the diameter squared. Doubling the diameter collects 4 times as much light, showing fainter objects.
- Resolution (resolving power): the ability to show fine detail and separate close objects (such as a close double star). The smallest angle that can be resolved is proportional to wavelength ÷ diameter, so a larger aperture gives better resolution, and observing at longer wavelengths (such as radio) gives worse resolution for the same size of dish (13.25). Atmospheric seeing often limits ground-based resolution.
- Field of view: the circle of sky seen through the eyepiece. Higher magnification gives a smaller field of view. Wide fields suit large objects (the Pleiades, the Andromeda Galaxy); narrow fields suit planets and double stars.
- Magnification = focal length of objective ÷ focal length of eyepiece. A long-focal-length objective and short eyepiece give high magnification. Changing the eyepiece changes the magnification. Too much magnification just gives a larger, dimmer, blurrier image ("empty magnification").

#### Worked example

1. A telescope has fₒ = 1200 mm. With a 25 mm eyepiece, magnification = 1200 ÷ 25 = 48×. With a 6 mm eyepiece, 200×.
2. Light grasp: a 200 mm telescope compared with the eye's 7 mm pupil: (200 ÷ 7)² ≈ 816 times as much light.
3. A 300 mm telescope compared with a 100 mm telescope: (300 ÷ 100)² = 9 times the light grasp, and 3 times better resolution.
4. A student wants 150× with an eyepiece of 8 mm. fₒ = 150 × 8 = 1200 mm.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying light grasp is proportional to diameter. It is proportional to diameter squared.
- Dividing fₑ by fₒ.
- Saying a larger aperture gives a larger field of view.

### 11.24 Galileo's early observations

#### What you need to know

- The importance of Galileo's early telescope observations in establishing the heliocentric model.

#### Explained

From 1609 Galileo Galilei used his own small refracting telescopes (about 20× to 30× magnification) and saw:

- Four moons orbiting Jupiter (now called the Galilean moons). This showed that not everything orbits the Earth, and that a moving planet can keep its moons (an objection to a moving Earth had been that it would leave the Moon behind).
- The phases of Venus: Venus shows a full set of phases, from crescent to nearly full, and appears much larger as a crescent than when gibbous. This is only possible if Venus orbits the Sun. In the Ptolemaic model, Venus always lies between the Earth and the Sun and could only ever show crescent phases. This was strong evidence against the Ptolemaic model.
- Mountains and craters on the Moon, and spots on the Sun that moved (showing the Sun rotates): heavenly bodies were not perfect, unchanging spheres.
- Many more stars than can be seen with the naked eye, and that the Milky Way is made of countless stars.

These observations supported the heliocentric model of Copernicus and Kepler, though they did not by themselves rule out Brahe's hybrid model. Galileo's support for heliocentrism brought him into conflict with the Church authorities of the time.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying Galileo invented the telescope. He improved it and was among the first to use it for astronomy.
- Saying the moons of Jupiter proved the Earth goes round the Sun. They showed not everything orbits the Earth; the phases of Venus were the stronger evidence.

### 11.26, 11.27 and 11.28 Space probes

#### What you need to know

- Advantages and disadvantages of fly-by, orbiter, impactor and lander probes.
- An example of each with its target and major discoveries: fly-by New Horizons (outer Solar System); orbiter Juno (Jupiter) or Dawn (Vesta and Ceres); impactor Deep Impact (comet Tempel 1); lander Philae (comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko).
- A space probe must reach (close to) the Earth's escape velocity, which needs rockets (see 9.4).

#### Explained

| Type | What it does | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fly-by | Passes close to a body once and continues on its way; may visit several bodies using gravity assists | Cheaper; can visit several targets; needs no fuel to slow down | Only a brief look; only one side may be seen; no long-term monitoring |
| Orbiter | Fires rockets to slow down and go into orbit | Studies the whole body over months or years; maps the surface; observes changes and seasons | Needs extra fuel to brake into orbit, so heavier and costlier; cannot sample the surface directly |
| Impactor | Deliberately crashes into a body | Reveals material beneath the surface; can test methods of deflecting asteroids | One-off; the impactor is destroyed; needs another craft to observe |
| Lander | Lands on the surface (some carry rovers) | Direct measurements, chemical analysis, close-up images, can drill or sample | Very complex and risky landing; studies only one small area (unless a rover); most expensive |

Examples:

- New Horizons (fly-by): launched 2006, flew past Pluto in 2015, giving the first detailed images: a young, smooth plain of nitrogen ice (Sputnik Planitia), mountains of water ice, and a thin hazy atmosphere. In 2019 it flew past the Kuiper Belt object Arrokoth, a "contact binary" of two lobes stuck together.
- Juno (orbiter): in orbit around Jupiter since 2016 in a polar orbit. Discovered huge cyclone clusters at the poles, measured Jupiter's strong and irregular magnetic field, and found that its core is "fuzzy", spread out rather than compact.
- Dawn (orbiter): orbited the asteroid Vesta (2011 to 2012) and the dwarf planet Ceres (2015 to 2018), the first craft to orbit two bodies, using ion engines. Found that Vesta is a layered protoplanet with an iron core, and found bright salt deposits (sodium carbonate) in Occator crater on Ceres, evidence of briny water below the surface.
- Deep Impact (impactor): in 2005 it released a 370 kg copper impactor into comet Tempel 1 while the main craft watched. The cloud of ejected material showed the nucleus is fluffy, porous and covered in fine dust, with water ice and organic material underneath.
- Philae (lander): carried by the Rosetta orbiter, Philae landed on comet 67P in 2014, the first soft landing on a comet. Its harpoons failed and it bounced into a shadowed crack, so its batteries ran out after about 60 hours, but it detected organic molecules on the surface and found it was harder than expected. Rosetta showed the comet's water has a different deuterium ratio from Earth's oceans (11.13).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying all probes land. Most are fly-bys or orbiters.
- Mixing up Rosetta (orbiter) and Philae (lander).

### 11.29 and 11.30 Crewed exploration and Apollo

#### What you need to know

- Advantages and disadvantages of direct observation by crewed missions.
- The main features of the Apollo programme to land astronauts on the Moon.

#### Explained

Crewed missions: advantages: humans can make decisions on the spot, adapt to the unexpected, choose the most interesting samples, repair equipment, and do complex experiments; they inspire the public. Disadvantages: very much more expensive (life support, food, water, air, shielding, safe return); risk to life (radiation, accidents); limited by human endurance; the spacecraft must return to Earth, so it carries much more mass. Robotic probes can go further, for longer, and to places too hostile for people.

The Apollo programme (USA, NASA):

- Aim: to land astronauts on the Moon and return them safely, set by President Kennedy in 1961, in a "space race" with the Soviet Union.
- Hardware: the Saturn V three-stage rocket (the most powerful ever flown at the time) launched the Command and Service Module (CSM, which stayed in lunar orbit with one astronaut) and the Lunar Module (LM, which took two astronauts to the surface and back up to the CSM). Only the small Command Module returned to Earth, splashing down in the ocean.
- Key missions: Apollo 1 (1967, a fire killed three astronauts in a ground test); Apollo 8 (1968, first humans to orbit the Moon and see the far side); Apollo 11 (July 1969, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin made the first landing, in the Sea of Tranquility); Apollo 13 (1970, an oxygen tank exploded, landing cancelled, crew returned safely); Apollo 15 to 17 used the Lunar Roving Vehicle; Apollo 17 (1972) was the last.
- Results: six successful landings, twelve astronauts walked on the Moon; about 382 kg of rock and soil returned, used to date the lunar surface and to support the Giant Impact Hypothesis; seismometers and laser reflectors left on the surface (the reflectors are still used to measure the Earth-Moon distance precisely).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying all three astronauts landed. One stayed in orbit in the Command Module.
- Saying Apollo 13 landed on the Moon.

## Topic 12 Formation of planetary systems (Paper 2)

Gravity, tides, collisions and the solar wind have shaped our Solar System. This topic covers how these forces act on planets and moons, what decides whether a body is round, broken up or has an atmosphere, how giant planets form, how we find planets around other stars, and the search for life elsewhere.

### 12.1 Forces at work in our Solar System

#### What you need to know

Identify these in our Solar System:

- Gravitational attraction producing regular motion: the orbits of planets and moons.
- Tidal gravitational forces producing ring systems, asteroid belts and internal heating.
- Gravitational interactions of several bodies: gradual shifts in orbits, chaotic motion, resonances, and Lagrangian points (no detailed maths).
- Accidental collisions causing impact craters, changes to orbits or planetary orientations.
- The solar wind affecting comets, planetary atmospheres and the heliosphere.

#### Explained

- Regular motion: gravity keeps planets in elliptical orbits around the Sun and moons around planets (Topic 8), giving predictable periods.
- Tidal forces: a body's gravity pulls more strongly on the near side of a neighbour than on its far side. This difference stretches the neighbour.
  - Ring systems: inside a certain distance of a giant planet (the Roche limit, 12.2), tidal forces stop particles gathering into a moon, or tear apart a moon that strays too close. Saturn's rings lie mainly inside its Roche limit.
  - Asteroid belt: Jupiter's strong gravity disturbed the planetesimals between Mars and Jupiter, so they never gathered into a planet.
  - Internal heating: a moon on a slightly elliptical orbit is squeezed and flexed by changing tides, which heats its interior by friction. Io, Jupiter's innermost large moon, is the most volcanically active body in the Solar System because of this; Europa and Enceladus are heated enough to keep liquid water oceans under their ice.
- Multiple-body interactions:
  - Gradual shifts: the planets' small pulls on each other slowly change the shapes and orientations of orbits over thousands of years. Irregularities in Uranus's orbit led to the prediction and discovery of Neptune (1846).
  - Chaotic motion: some small bodies, such as some asteroids and comets, have orbits that are very sensitive to small changes, so their long-term paths cannot be predicted. Hyperion, a moon of Saturn, tumbles chaotically.
  - Resonances: when the orbital periods of two bodies are in a simple ratio, their regular pulls add up over time. Io, Europa and Ganymede are in a 1 : 2 : 4 resonance, which keeps their orbits elliptical (driving tidal heating). Pluto orbits twice for every three Neptune orbits. Resonances with Jupiter clear gaps in the asteroid belt (Kirkwood gaps); moons cause gaps in Saturn's rings (Cassini Division).
  - Lagrangian points: five places in the orbit system of two large bodies (such as the Sun and Earth) where a small object can keep the same position relative to both. L1 (between Sun and Earth) is used by solar observatories such as SOHO; L2 (beyond Earth) is used by JWST; at L4 and L5, 60° ahead and behind a planet, Trojan asteroids collect (Jupiter has thousands).
- Accidental collisions: impacts produced craters on almost all solid bodies; a giant impact probably formed the Moon (9.5); collisions may have tilted Uranus onto its side (axis tilt about 98°) and could explain Venus's slow backwards rotation; the impact 66 million years ago that contributed to the extinction of the dinosaurs.
- Solar wind: pushes comets' ion tails away from the Sun (11.2); can strip away the atmospheres of planets without a protective magnetic field (Mars lost much of its atmosphere this way); inflates the heliosphere (11.5).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying Io is volcanic because it is close to the Sun. Its heat comes from tidal flexing by Jupiter.
- Saying Lagrangian points have no gravity. The pulls of the two bodies and the orbital motion balance there.

### 12.2 Forces in forming planets and moons

#### What you need to know

- The interaction between tidal gravitational forces and elastic forces determines whether a body is broken apart (the Roche limit).
- The interaction between gravitational and elastic forces determines whether a body is spherical or irregular.
- The interaction between gravitational and thermal factors determines whether a body has an atmosphere.

#### Explained

- Roche limit: the distance from a planet inside which its tidal forces are stronger than the forces holding a moon together (its own gravity plus the strength of its material). Inside this limit, a loosely bound moon is pulled apart, and particles cannot clump together, so rings form. Small solid bodies held together by rock strength (such as small moons and spacecraft) can survive inside it.
- Shape: a small body's rock is strong enough (elastic and rigid forces) to resist its weak gravity, so it can stay lumpy and irregular, like most asteroids and the small moons Phobos and Deimos. Above a few hundred km across, gravity becomes strong enough to overcome the material's strength and pull the body into a sphere (hydrostatic equilibrium). This is part of the definition of a planet and dwarf planet.
- Atmosphere: gas molecules move faster at higher temperatures. A body keeps an atmosphere if its gravity (escape velocity) is strong enough compared with the speed of its gas molecules. Large, cold bodies keep thick atmospheres (Jupiter, Titan); small or hot bodies lose theirs (Mercury, the Moon). Light gases such as hydrogen escape more easily than heavy ones such as carbon dioxide.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a body is round because it spins.
- Saying the Moon has no atmosphere because there is no air in space. It is because its gravity is too weak to hold one at its temperature.

### 12.3 Formation of gas giants in planetary systems

#### What you need to know

- The main theories for the formation of gas giant planets in planetary systems.

#### Explained

All planetary systems form from a protoplanetary disc: a flat, spinning disc of gas and dust around a young star, left over from the collapse of a cloud (nebula). Dust grains stick together into larger and larger bodies (planetesimals, then protoplanets).

1. Core accretion model (most widely accepted): beyond the snow line, ice adds to the solid material, so a rocky-icy core can grow to about 10 Earth masses relatively quickly. Its gravity then pulls in a huge envelope of hydrogen and helium from the disc, quickly building a gas giant. This must happen within a few million years, before radiation from the young star blows away the gas. It explains why giant planets have dense cores and why they are more common around stars rich in heavy elements.
2. Disc instability model: in a massive, cold disc, regions can become unstable and collapse directly under their own gravity into giant planets, very quickly (in thousands of years), without a solid core first. It may explain giant planets found very far from their stars, where core accretion would be too slow.

Many exoplanet systems contain "hot Jupiters", giant planets orbiting very close to their stars. They cannot have formed there (too hot for ices), so they are thought to have migrated inwards through interactions with the disc. This supports the idea that planets can move after they form (11.7).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying gas giants formed close to their stars and moved outwards.

### 12.4 Discovering exoplanets

#### What you need to know

- Methods for discovering exoplanet systems: the transit method, astrometry and radial velocity.

#### Explained

Exoplanets are planets orbiting stars other than the Sun. They are very faint and lost in their star's glare, so most are found indirectly.

- Transit method: if a planet's orbit is edge-on to us, it passes in front of its star once each orbit and blocks a tiny fraction of the light. The star's brightness dips regularly. The period of the dips gives the orbital period (and, with Kepler's third law, the distance from the star); the depth of the dip gives the planet's size relative to the star (a bigger planet blocks more light). Used by the Kepler and TESS space telescopes to find thousands of planets. Limitation: only works for planets whose orbits are lined up with our line of sight; needs at least three transits to confirm.
- Radial velocity (Doppler wobble): a planet and its star orbit their common centre of mass, so the star wobbles slightly. When it moves towards us, its spectral lines are blueshifted; when away, redshifted. The period of the shift gives the planet's orbital period; the size of the shift gives a minimum mass for the planet. Best for massive planets close to their stars (which cause bigger, faster wobbles). The first planet around a Sun-like star (51 Pegasi b, 1995) was found this way.
- Astrometry: very precise measurement of the star's position on the sky, which shows its tiny side-to-side wobble. Hard because the movement is extremely small; works best for massive planets far from nearby stars. The Gaia mission can detect such wobbles.

(Direct imaging, blocking the star's light, and gravitational microlensing are also used, but are not named in this spec.)

#### Worked example

A star's brightness dips by 1% (0.01) during a transit. The fraction of light blocked = (planet radius ÷ star radius)². So planet radius ÷ star radius = √0.01 = 0.1: the planet's radius is a tenth of the star's (roughly Jupiter-sized for a Sun-like star).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the transit method shows the planet. It shows only the drop in the star's light.
- Saying radial velocity measures the planet's speed. It measures the star's motion towards and away from us.

### 12.5 and 12.6 Life elsewhere and Goldilocks zones

#### What you need to know

- The requirements for life and the possibility of life on Titan, Europa, Enceladus and outside our Solar System.
- The relevance of Goldilocks (habitable) zones.

#### Explained

Life as we know it needs: liquid water (as a solvent for chemical reactions); a source of energy (sunlight or chemical energy); the elements for organic molecules (carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, sulfur); and enough time and stable conditions.

- Titan (Saturn's largest moon): has a thick nitrogen atmosphere with complex organic chemistry and lakes and rivers of liquid methane and ethane, studied by the Cassini-Huygens mission. It is far too cold for liquid surface water (about −180 °C), but it may have an ocean under its icy crust. Any life would have to be very different, perhaps using methane as a solvent.
- Europa (Jupiter's moon): an icy crust over a global salty liquid water ocean, kept warm by tidal heating. Possible hydrothermal vents on the ocean floor could supply energy and chemicals, as at vents on Earth's sea floor that support life. Missions such as Europa Clipper and JUICE are investigating.
- Enceladus (Saturn's moon): Cassini flew through plumes of water vapour and ice jetting from cracks near its south pole and detected salts, silica and organic molecules and hydrogen, suggesting a subsurface ocean in contact with a rocky core and hydrothermal activity.
- Outside the Solar System: thousands of exoplanets are known, some rocky and Earth-sized. Astronomers try to detect gases in their atmospheres (biosignatures) such as oxygen, methane or water vapour, using telescopes like JWST.

The Goldilocks (habitable) zone is the range of distances from a star where a planet's surface temperature could allow liquid water to exist: not too hot (water boils away) and not too cold (water freezes). Its distance depends on the star's luminosity: hotter, brighter stars have wider zones further out; cool red dwarfs have narrow zones very close in. Earth is in the Sun's habitable zone; Venus is too close and Mars on the outer edge. It is used to choose which exoplanets to study first. But being in the zone does not guarantee life (atmosphere and other factors matter), and moons outside it (Europa, Enceladus) may have liquid water heated by tides.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying Titan has lakes of water. Its lakes are liquid methane and ethane.
- Saying planets in the habitable zone definitely have life.

### 12.7 The Drake equation

#### What you need to know

- How the factors in the Drake equation can be used to estimate the number of civilisations in our Galaxy.

#### Explained

Frank Drake (1961) suggested estimating the number of civilisations in the Milky Way that we could detect (N) by multiplying a chain of factors:

N = R* × fp × nₑ × fl × fi × fc × L

| Factor | Meaning | How well known |
| --- | --- | --- |
| R* | Rate of star formation in the Galaxy (stars per year) | Fairly well known (a few per year) |
| fp | Fraction of stars with planets | Now known from exoplanet surveys: most stars |
| nₑ | Number of planets per system with suitable environments for life | Becoming known (habitable-zone planets) |
| fl | Fraction of those where life actually develops | Unknown |
| fi | Fraction of those where intelligent life develops | Unknown |
| fc | Fraction that develop technology that releases detectable signals (such as radio) | Unknown |
| L | Length of time such civilisations release signals (years) | Unknown |

The equation does not give a definite answer, because the last four factors are unknown: estimates range from less than one to millions. Its value is that it breaks a huge question into smaller parts, shows which parts science can measure, and guides research. Astronomy has made progress on the early factors (fp, nₑ) through exoplanet discoveries.

#### Worked example

Take R* = 2 per year, fp = 0.5, nₑ = 1, fl = 0.1, fi = 0.01, fc = 0.1, L = 10 000 years.
N = 2 × 0.5 × 1 × 0.1 × 0.01 × 0.1 × 10 000 = 1. With these estimates, about one detectable civilisation (ours). Increasing L to 1 million years would give N = 100.

#### Common mistakes

- Treating the Drake equation as proof that aliens exist. It is a framework for estimation.

### 12.8 SETI

#### What you need to know

- The search for extra-terrestrial intelligence (SETI) by receiving radio waves, including the benefits and dangers of discovering extra-terrestrial life.

#### Explained

SETI uses large radio telescopes to listen for artificial signals from other civilisations. Radio is used because radio waves pass easily through the Earth's atmosphere and through gas and dust in space, travel at the speed of light, and are cheap to produce; a technological civilisation would probably use them. Searches focus on narrow-band signals (natural sources emit over a wide range of frequencies), on "quiet" frequencies such as near the 21 cm hydrogen line (the "water hole"), and on stars with known habitable-zone planets. Projects such as Breakthrough Listen use telescopes around the world. No confirmed signal has been found (the 1977 "Wow!" signal was never repeated).

Benefits of discovering extra-terrestrial life: it would answer one of the biggest questions in science; we could learn about biology, technology and history from another civilisation; it might unite humanity; the search itself develops technology for signal processing.

Dangers: an advanced civilisation might be hostile or exploit us; replying could reveal our location (a debate about whether to send messages, called active SETI or METI); social, religious and cultural disruption; risk of panic or misinformation. Because signals take many years to travel, any "conversation" would be extremely slow.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying SETI sends signals. SETI listens; sending messages is a separate, debated activity.

## Topic 13 Exploring starlight (Paper 2)

Almost everything we know about stars comes from their light. This topic covers brightness and the magnitude scale, the distance modulus, spectra and spectral types, the Hertzsprung-Russell (HR) diagram, measuring distance with parallax, HR diagrams and Cepheids, variable and binary stars, star clusters, star trails, digital detectors, and astronomy across the electromagnetic spectrum from the ground and from space.

Equation (given): distance modulus M = m + 5 − 5 log d, with d in parsecs.

### 13.1, 13.2 and 13.3 Magnitudes and the distance modulus

#### What you need to know

- The astronomical magnitude scale and how apparent magnitude relates to brightness as seen from Earth.
- The term absolute magnitude.
- Use M = m + 5 − 5 log d to find the absolute (M) or apparent (m) magnitude, given distance d in parsecs.

#### Explained

The magnitude scale was started by Hipparchus, who called the brightest stars "first magnitude" and the faintest visible "sixth magnitude". Features of the modern scale:

- It runs backwards: smaller (or more negative) numbers mean brighter. Sirius is −1.5, Vega about 0, Polaris 2.0; the naked-eye limit at a dark site is about +6.
- It is logarithmic: a difference of 5 magnitudes is exactly a factor of 100 in brightness. So 1 magnitude is a factor of about 2.5 (2.512, the fifth root of 100).
- Very bright objects have negative magnitudes: Venus up to about −4.6, the full Moon −12.7, the Sun −26.7.

Apparent magnitude (m) is how bright a star looks from Earth. It depends on both the star's actual luminosity and its distance.

Absolute magnitude (M) is the apparent magnitude the star would have if it were at a standard distance of 10 parsecs. It allows the true brightness (luminosity) of stars to be compared fairly. The Sun's absolute magnitude is about +4.8: from 10 pc away it would be a faint naked-eye star.

The distance modulus relates them: M = m + 5 − 5 log d. If d = 10 pc, log 10 = 1, so M = m. Stars further than 10 pc have m > M (look fainter than they would at 10 pc).

#### Worked example

1. A star has m = 6.0 at d = 100 pc. M = 6.0 + 5 − 5 log 100 = 6.0 + 5 − 10 = 1.0.
2. A star with M = −2.0 is at 250 pc. Rearrange: m = M − 5 + 5 log d = −2.0 − 5 + 5 × 2.398 = −2.0 − 5 + 11.99 = 5.0. Just visible to the naked eye.
3. Star A has m = 1; star B has m = 6. B is 5 magnitudes fainter, so A appears 100 times brighter.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a larger magnitude means brighter.
- Using log to base e (ln) instead of log₁₀.
- Saying absolute magnitude is how bright a star looks from Earth.

### 13.4, 13.5, 13.6 and 13.21 Stellar spectra and spectral types

#### What you need to know

- The information a stellar spectrum gives: chemical composition, temperature and radial velocity.
- Stars can be classified by spectral type.
- A star's colour and spectral type are related to its surface temperature.
- How astronomers obtain and study the pattern of spectral lines from astronomical objects.

#### Explained

Obtaining spectra (13.21): a spectroscope or spectrograph on a telescope spreads the light into its component wavelengths using a prism or, more often, a diffraction grating. The spectrum is recorded on a digital sensor. A star's spectrum is a continuous rainbow crossed by dark absorption lines, where cooler gases in the star's outer layers absorb particular wavelengths. Hot thin gas clouds (nebulae) give emission lines (bright lines on a dark background). The pattern is compared with spectra of elements measured in the laboratory.

Information from a spectrum:

- Chemical composition: each element absorbs a unique set of wavelengths, like a fingerprint. Matching the lines identifies the elements present. Helium was discovered in the Sun's spectrum before it was found on Earth. Most stars are about 73% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass.
- Temperature: hotter objects emit more strongly at shorter wavelengths, so the peak of the continuous spectrum (and the colour) shows the surface temperature. The strengths of certain lines also depend on temperature.
- Radial velocity: if the star moves towards or away from us, all its lines are shifted to shorter (blueshift) or longer (redshift) wavelengths by the Doppler effect. The size of the shift gives the speed along the line of sight. Used in binary stars and exoplanet detection (12.4) and cosmology (16.3).

Spectral types: stars are classified by the lines in their spectra into types O, B, A, F, G, K, M, in order of decreasing temperature (each type subdivided 0 to 9, so the Sun is G2).

| Type | Colour | Surface temperature (approx.) | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| O | Blue | Above 30 000 K | Stars in Orion's Belt (Alnitak) |
| B | Blue-white | 10 000 to 30 000 K | Rigel |
| A | White | 7500 to 10 000 K | Sirius, Vega |
| F | Yellow-white | 6000 to 7500 K | Procyon |
| G | Yellow | 5200 to 6000 K | The Sun (5800 K) |
| K | Orange | 3700 to 5200 K | Arcturus, Aldebaran |
| M | Red | Below 3700 K | Betelgeuse, Proxima Centauri |

#### Common mistakes

- Saying red stars are hottest. Red stars are the coolest; blue the hottest.
- Saying dark lines are caused by gaps in the star. They are absorption by cooler gas.

### 13.7, 13.8 and 13.13 The Hertzsprung-Russell diagram

#### What you need to know

- Sketch a simple HR diagram with labelled axes, showing main sequence stars, the Sun, red and blue giants, white dwarfs and supergiants.
- How a star's life cycle relates to its position on the HR diagram, for stars of about the Sun's mass and much more massive stars.
- Use an HR diagram to determine distances to stars.

#### Explained

The HR diagram plots stars' luminosity (or absolute magnitude) on the vertical axis against surface temperature (or spectral type, or colour) on the horizontal axis. Conventions:

- Vertical: luminosity increases upwards (absolute magnitude becomes more negative upwards), often in units of the Sun's luminosity on a logarithmic scale (10⁻⁴ to 10⁶).
- Horizontal: temperature decreases from left to right (hot O and B stars on the left, about 30 000 K or more; cool M stars on the right, about 3000 K).

Regions:

- Main sequence: a diagonal band from top left (hot, bright, massive blue stars) to bottom right (cool, dim, low-mass red dwarfs). About 90% of stars are here, fusing hydrogen in their cores.
- The Sun: on the main sequence, in the middle (G2, 5800 K, luminosity 1, M = +4.8).
- Red giants: upper right of the main sequence: cool but very luminous, because they are huge.
- Blue giants: upper left, hot and very luminous.
- Supergiants: across the very top (red supergiants such as Betelgeuse at top right; blue supergiants such as Rigel at top left).
- White dwarfs: lower left: hot but very dim, because they are tiny.

Life cycles on the diagram (14.9 and 14.10):

- Sun-like star: a protostar moves onto the main sequence, where it stays about 10 billion years; it then moves up and to the right to become a red giant; after losing its outer layers (planetary nebula) its core moves to the lower left as a white dwarf, then slowly cools and fades towards the bottom right (black dwarf).
- Much more massive star: joins the main sequence near the top left, stays only millions of years, then moves to the right across the top as a red supergiant, before exploding as a supernova; the remnant (neutron star or black hole) is not on the normal diagram.

Distances from the HR diagram (spectroscopic parallax): take a main sequence star's spectrum to find its spectral type; read its absolute magnitude M from the main sequence on the HR diagram; measure its apparent magnitude m; then use the distance modulus to find d. This works for stars too far for parallax. Similarly, for a whole cluster, fitting its main sequence to the standard one gives the cluster's distance.

#### Worked example

A main sequence star has spectral type A0, which on the HR diagram corresponds to M = +0.6. Its apparent magnitude is 10.6. m − M = 10.0 = 5 log d − 5, so log d = 3, d = 1000 pc.

#### Common mistakes

- Drawing temperature increasing to the right.
- Putting white dwarfs at the top. They are hot but faint, so bottom left.
- Saying stars move along the main sequence as they age. They stay in roughly one place on it during hydrogen burning.

### 13.9 The inverse square law

#### What you need to know

- The inverse square relationship between distance and brightness (intensity).

#### Explained

Light from a star spreads out in all directions over the surface of an ever-larger sphere. The area of a sphere is proportional to r². So the brightness (intensity, power received per square metre) falls as 1 ÷ distance²: twice as far means 1/4 as bright; three times as far, 1/9; ten times as far, 1/100.

This is why apparent magnitude depends on distance, and why a factor of 10 in distance changes the magnitude by 5 (a factor of 100 in brightness).

#### Worked example

A star is moved (in imagination) from 10 pc to 40 pc. Distance × 4, so brightness × 1/16. 16 is about 2.5³, so it would be about 3 magnitudes fainter.

#### Common mistakes

- Halving the brightness when the distance doubles.

### 13.10, 13.11 and 13.12 Angles, parsecs and parallax

#### What you need to know

- One degree is 60 arcminutes (60′) and one arcminute is 60 arcseconds (60″).
- The term parsec.
- Determine astronomical distances using heliocentric parallax.

#### Explained

Small angles: 1° = 60′, 1′ = 60″, so 1° = 3600″. The full Moon is about 30′ (half a degree) across; the eye's resolution is about 1′; good ground-based telescopes resolve about 1″.

Heliocentric parallax: as the Earth orbits the Sun, a nearby star appears to shift its position against much more distant background stars. Photograph the star six months apart, when the Earth is on opposite sides of its orbit (a baseline of 2 AU). The parallax angle p is defined as half the total apparent shift (the angle corresponding to a 1 AU baseline).

The parsec ("parallax second") is the distance at which a star has a parallax of exactly 1 arcsecond. 1 pc = 3.26 light years = 3.1 × 10¹³ km.

Distance in parsecs = 1 ÷ parallax in arcseconds: d = 1 ÷ p.

Even the nearest star, Proxima Centauri, has p = 0.77″ (d = 1.3 pc). Parallaxes are so small that they were not measured until 1838 (Bessel, 61 Cygni). Ground-based parallax works to about 100 pc; the Hipparcos and Gaia satellites, above the blurring atmosphere, measure parallaxes far more accurately, out to thousands of parsecs.

#### Worked example

1. A star shifts by a total of 0.40″ between January and July. p = 0.20″. d = 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5.0 pc = 16.3 light years.
2. Convert 0.5° to arcseconds: 0.5 × 3600 = 1800″.

#### Common mistakes

- Using the full shift instead of half of it.
- Saying a larger parallax means a further star. Larger parallax means closer.

### 13.14 to 13.18 Variable stars, binaries and clusters

#### What you need to know

- Light curves of short- and long-period variables, eclipsing binaries, Cepheids, novae and supernovae.
- Why the light curve of an eclipsing binary varies.
- How Cepheid variables are used to determine distances.
- The structure of gravitationally bound groups such as binary stars and clusters.
- How the period of an eclipsing binary is found from its light curve.

#### Explained

A light curve is a graph of a star's brightness (magnitude, with brighter upwards) against time.

| Type | Light curve | Cause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short-period variable | Regular repeating changes over hours to days | Often pulsating stars or close binaries |
| Long-period variable (such as Mira) | Large, fairly regular changes over months to a year or more | Pulsating red giants that swell and shrink |
| Eclipsing binary | Mostly steady, with sharp regular dips | Two stars orbiting each other edge-on to us, one passing in front of the other |
| Cepheid variable | Regular, with a steep rise and slower fall; periods of about 1 to 100 days | Giant star pulsating in size and temperature |
| Nova | Sudden rise of many magnitudes in days, then slow fading over months | A white dwarf in a close binary pulls hydrogen from its companion; the hydrogen builds up and explodes in a runaway fusion reaction on its surface |
| Supernova | Huge sudden rise (can outshine a galaxy), then fading over months | Explosion of a massive star or the total destruction of a white dwarf |

Eclipsing binaries (13.15 and 13.18): the two stars orbit their common centre of mass. When the dimmer star passes in front of the brighter one, a deep primary minimum occurs; when the brighter star passes in front of the dimmer one, a shallower secondary minimum. Between eclipses the combined light is steady. The period of the system is the time between one primary minimum and the next (not between a primary and a secondary). The depth and shape of the dips give information on the stars' relative sizes and brightnesses. Algol in Perseus dims every 2.87 days.

Cepheids (13.16): Henrietta Leavitt found (1912) that Cepheids with longer periods are more luminous: the period-luminosity relationship. So: measure a Cepheid's period from its light curve; read its absolute magnitude from the period-luminosity graph; measure its apparent magnitude; use the distance modulus to find its distance. Cepheids are very luminous and can be seen in other galaxies, so they are "standard candles" for measuring distances far beyond parallax range. Edwin Hubble used them to show that the Andromeda Galaxy is far outside the Milky Way.

Binary stars and clusters (13.17):

- Binary stars: two stars bound by gravity, orbiting a common centre of mass. Visual binaries can be seen as two stars in a telescope. (An optical double is two stars that only appear close because they lie in the same direction, at different distances.) Most stars are in binary or multiple systems.
- Open clusters: loose groups of tens to thousands of young stars that formed together from the same cloud, found in the disc of the Galaxy, such as the Pleiades and the Hyades. They gradually drift apart.
- Globular clusters: dense, spherical groups of hundreds of thousands of very old stars, found in the halo around the Galaxy, such as M13 in Hercules. Tightly bound by gravity.

#### Worked example

A Cepheid has a period of 10 days, which from the period-luminosity graph gives M = −4.0. Its apparent magnitude is 11.0. m − M = 15 = 5 log d − 5, so log d = 4 and d = 10 000 pc (10 kpc).

Eclipsing binary: primary minima are observed at day 0.0, day 3.2 and day 6.4; a shallower minimum at day 1.6. The period is 3.2 days.

#### Common mistakes

- Taking the time between a primary and secondary minimum as the period.
- Saying open clusters are older than globular clusters. Globular clusters are much older.
- Mixing up novae (surface explosion on a white dwarf, star survives) and supernovae.

### 13.19 Star trails and the sidereal day

#### What you need to know

- Use star-trail photographs to determine the length of the sidereal day.

#### Explained

A camera on a fixed tripod, pointed at the celestial pole with its shutter open, records stars as arcs (star trails) around the pole as the Earth rotates. In a full sidereal day the stars would trace complete circles (360°). Method (task B12): take a long exposure of known duration t. Measure the angle θ of the arcs at the pole with a protractor. Then sidereal day = 360° ÷ θ × t. For accuracy, use long exposures and measure several trails (or take two short exposures hours apart and measure the rotation angle of the star pattern between them).

#### Worked example

A 2.0-hour exposure produces trails of 30.1°. Sidereal day = (360 ÷ 30.1) × 2.0 h = 23.9 h = 23 h 55 min. (Accepted value 23 h 56 min.)

#### Common mistakes

- Measuring the length of the trail in mm instead of the angle at the pole.

### 13.20 Digital sensors

#### What you need to know

- Most modern observations are recorded with digital sensors that convert light into electrical signals, which can be processed and stored as data files.

#### Explained

CCD (charge-coupled device) and CMOS sensors contain millions of tiny light-sensitive pixels. Light hitting each pixel releases electrons; the charge collected is proportional to the amount of light. The charges are read out, converted to numbers and stored as an image file.

Advantages over the eye and photographic film: very sensitive (they record most of the photons that arrive, compared with a few per cent for film); can make long exposures to build up faint images; the response is linear, so brightness can be measured accurately (photometry); images can be processed digitally (stacking, adjusting brightness and contrast, false colour) and shared instantly; they can record infrared and ultraviolet as well as visible light. Cooling the sensor reduces electronic noise.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying digital sensors make objects brighter. They collect and record light more efficiently.

### 13.22 to 13.33 Observing across the electromagnetic spectrum

#### What you need to know

- The Earth's atmosphere blocks almost all wavelengths except visible light and radio waves.
- Only optical and radio telescopes should be located at sea level.
- How a simple radio telescope works; why radio telescopes need very large apertures; how several radio telescopes work together as an aperture synthesis array.
- Radio astronomy's importance in discovering quasars, jets from black holes, the structure of the Milky Way and protoplanetary discs.
- Why some infrared telescopes can operate at high-altitude sites; infrared astronomy's importance in discovering protostars, dust and molecular clouds, and hotspots on moons.
- The damaging effect of the atmosphere on images from ground-based telescopes.
- Why telescopes working outside the optical and radio windows must be sited above the atmosphere.
- Advantages and disadvantages of space telescopes and orbiting platforms.
- How gamma-ray, X-ray and ultraviolet astronomy have been important in discovering gamma-ray bursts, black hole accretion discs and the structure of the corona and chromosphere of young stars.

#### Explained

Atmospheric windows (13.22, 13.23): the atmosphere absorbs gamma rays, X-rays and most ultraviolet (absorbed high up by oxygen and ozone), and most infrared (absorbed by water vapour and carbon dioxide), and microwaves at some wavelengths. Only visible light (the optical window) and a wide range of radio waves (the radio window) reach sea level. So only optical and radio telescopes can work at sea level.

Radio telescopes (13.24 to 13.26): a simple radio telescope is a large curved (parabolic) metal dish that reflects radio waves to a focus, where an antenna (receiver) converts them into an electrical signal; the signal is amplified, recorded and processed by computers into a map of the sky. The dish can be a mesh as long as its holes are much smaller than the wavelength. Radio telescopes can be used day and night and through cloud.

Resolution is proportional to wavelength ÷ diameter (11.22). Radio wavelengths (cm to m) are about a million times longer than visible light, so to resolve as finely as an optical telescope, a radio dish would need to be a million times wider. So radio telescopes are made as large as possible (Lovell 76 m; FAST in China 500 m), and still have poor resolution on their own.

Aperture synthesis: several radio dishes spread over a large area (kilometres, or even across continents) observe the same object at the same time; their signals are combined by computer. The array has the resolution of a single dish as wide as the largest separation between dishes. As the Earth rotates, the dishes' positions relative to the source change, filling in the picture. Examples: the Very Large Array (USA), e-MERLIN (UK), ALMA (Chile), and the Event Horizon Telescope, an Earth-sized array that imaged the shadow of the black hole in M87 (2019).

Radio discoveries (13.27): quasars (distant, extremely luminous galactic nuclei, first found as radio sources); jets of material shot out from near black holes in active galaxies; the spiral structure and rotation of the Milky Way, mapped using 21 cm hydrogen emission which passes through dust (15.3); protoplanetary discs around young stars (imaged in detail by ALMA at millimetre wavelengths); also pulsars and the cosmic microwave background.

Infrared (13.28, 13.29): infrared is absorbed mainly by water vapour, which is concentrated low in the atmosphere. At high, dry mountain sites (such as Mauna Kea in Hawaii, about 4200 m, or the Atacama Desert in Chile) much of the water vapour is below the telescope, so some infrared wavelengths get through. Infrared also passes through dust that blocks visible light. Infrared astronomy discovered: protostars hidden inside dusty clouds; the dust and cold molecular clouds where stars form; volcanic hotspots on Io and other moons; and it reveals the Galactic centre. Infrared telescopes are cooled to stop their own heat swamping the signal; space telescopes such as Spitzer and JWST avoid the atmosphere entirely.

Atmospheric effects on images (13.30): turbulence makes images twinkle and blur (seeing), limiting resolution to about 1″ even for huge telescopes; light pollution and skyglow reduce contrast; absorption dims light, especially near the horizon; clouds and weather stop observations. Remedies: high mountain sites; adaptive optics (a deformable mirror that changes shape hundreds of times a second to cancel turbulence); going into space.

Above the atmosphere (13.31): gamma-ray, X-ray, most ultraviolet and most infrared telescopes must be in space (or on high balloons and rockets), because these wavelengths never reach the ground.

Space telescopes (13.32): advantages: access to all wavelengths; no atmospheric blurring, so sharper images; no weather, no light pollution, no day-night cycle (can observe continuously); very stable conditions. Disadvantages: very expensive to build and launch; size limited by the rocket; difficult or impossible to repair or upgrade (the Hubble Space Telescope was serviced by astronauts; JWST at L2 cannot be); limited lifetime (fuel, coolant); risk of launch failure; damage from radiation and space debris. Orbiting platforms include Hubble (visible, UV), JWST (infrared), Chandra (X-ray), Fermi (gamma-ray), SOHO and SDO (Sun), Gaia (star positions).

High-energy astronomy (13.33):

- Gamma-ray astronomy discovered gamma-ray bursts: extremely powerful, brief flashes from distant galaxies, from the collapse of very massive stars or the merging of neutron stars.
- X-ray astronomy revealed the accretion discs of black holes: gas spiralling into a black hole (for example in the binary Cygnus X-1, or in galaxy centres) is heated to millions of degrees and emits X-rays. Also hot gas in galaxy clusters and supernova remnants.
- Ultraviolet (and X-ray) astronomy revealed the structure of the hot corona and chromosphere of the Sun and of young, active stars, where strong magnetic activity heats gas to very high temperatures.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying radio telescopes are big because radio waves are weak. The main reason is resolution at long wavelengths (though collecting area also helps).
- Saying X-ray telescopes can work on mountains. X-rays do not reach any ground level.
- Saying infrared is blocked by "the ozone layer". It is mainly absorbed by water vapour.

## Topic 14 Stellar evolution (Paper 2)

A star's life is a long contest between gravity pulling inwards and pressure pushing outwards. This topic covers how stars and deep-sky objects are named, the balance of forces at each stage of a star's life, the end states (white dwarf, neutron star, black hole) and what decides which one a star reaches, the stages and timescales of evolution, and how black holes are detected. For a simpler overview of the life cycle, see [AQA GCSE Physics 4.8](../../../aqa/gcse/physics-8463/4.8-space-physics.md).

### 14.1 and 14.2 Catalogues and star names

#### What you need to know

- Use the Messier and New General Catalogue (NGC) for nebulae, clusters and galaxies.
- Use the Bayer system for naming the brightest stars within a constellation.

#### Explained

The Messier catalogue was compiled by Charles Messier in the 1700s. He was a comet hunter and listed about 110 fuzzy objects that could be mistaken for comets, so he could ignore them. Objects are numbered M1 to M110: M1 is the Crab Nebula (a supernova remnant), M13 a globular cluster in Hercules, M31 the Andromeda Galaxy, M42 the Orion Nebula, M45 the Pleiades. Because Messier observed from France, it contains only objects visible from northern mid-latitudes, and only bright ones. It is a popular list for amateur observers (task B11).

The New General Catalogue (NGC), compiled by J. L. E. Dreyer in 1888 (building on the Herschels' work), lists about 7800 nebulae, clusters and galaxies from the whole sky, numbered in order of right ascension, for example NGC 224 (= M31), NGC 7000 (the North America Nebula). The supplementary Index Catalogues (IC) add thousands more. Many objects have both an M and an NGC number.

The Bayer system (Johann Bayer, 1603) names bright stars with a lower-case Greek letter followed by the Latin genitive (possessive) form of the constellation name. Usually the brightest star is α (alpha), the next β (beta), then γ, δ, and so on, although Bayer's order is not always strictly by brightness. Examples: Betelgeuse = α Orionis; Rigel = β Orionis; Sirius = α Canis Majoris; Polaris = α Ursae Minoris; Vega = α Lyrae; Deneb = α Cygni. (Fainter stars use Flamsteed numbers, such as 61 Cygni.)

#### Common mistakes

- Thinking Messier numbers go in order of brightness or type. They are in the order Messier listed them.
- Assuming α is always the brightest. In Orion, Rigel (β) is usually brighter than Betelgeuse (α).

### 14.3, 14.4 and 14.6 The pressure-gravity balance in stars

#### What you need to know

- The interaction between radiation pressure and gravity in a main sequence star.
- How the radiation pressure-gravity balance changes at different stages of a Sun-like star's life.
- How it changes for a star much more massive than the Sun.

#### Explained

Gravity pulls all of a star's material towards its centre. Fusion in the core releases energy; the hot gas and the flow of radiation outwards create pressure (gas pressure and radiation pressure) that pushes outwards. In a main sequence star these are balanced (hydrostatic equilibrium), so the star stays the same size and brightness for a long time. The balance is self-regulating: if the core contracts slightly, it heats up, fusion speeds up, pressure rises and the star expands back; if it expands, it cools, fusion slows and it contracts back.

Changes for a Sun-like star:

1. Protostar: gravity wins; the cloud collapses and heats up. There is no fusion yet.
2. Main sequence: hydrogen fuses to helium in the core; outward pressure balances gravity (about 10 billion years for the Sun).
3. When the core runs out of hydrogen, fusion there stops and pressure falls, so gravity makes the helium core contract and heat up. Hydrogen fusion starts in a shell around the core. The extra energy pushes the outer layers out: pressure now exceeds gravity in the outer layers, and the star swells into a red giant (the surface cools and reddens as it expands).
4. The core becomes hot enough (about 100 million K) for helium to fuse into carbon and oxygen; a new, temporary balance.
5. When the helium is used up, the core contracts again but is not massive enough to reach the temperature to fuse carbon. The outer layers are puffed off into space as a planetary nebula.
6. The exposed core is a white dwarf, held up not by fusion but by electron pressure (14.5).

Changes for a star much more massive than the Sun (more than about 8 solar masses):

1. Much stronger gravity means a hotter, denser core, faster fusion and much greater radiation pressure; the star is very luminous and uses its fuel quickly (millions of years).
2. Each time a fuel runs out in the core, pressure drops, gravity compresses the core, it heats up, and the next fuel starts fusing: hydrogen to helium, helium to carbon, then carbon, neon, oxygen, silicon, building up layers like an onion. The outer layers expand into a red supergiant.
3. Iron forms in the core. Fusing iron does not release energy, so there is no new source of pressure. Gravity wins: the core collapses in less than a second.
4. The collapse is halted when the core becomes as dense as an atomic nucleus; the infalling outer layers rebound and the star explodes as a supernova (a core-collapse supernova). The remnant is a neutron star or black hole.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a red giant expands because it is gaining fuel. It expands because shell burning around a contracting core pushes the outer layers out.
- Saying gravity stops acting in a main sequence star. Gravity is balanced, not absent.

### 14.5, 14.7 and 14.8 White dwarfs, neutron stars and the Chandrasekhar Limit

#### What you need to know

- The balance between electron pressure and gravity in a white dwarf.
- The balance between neutron pressure and gravity in a neutron star.
- How the Chandrasekhar Limit affects the outcome of the final stages of a star's life.

#### Explained

- White dwarf: the remaining core of a Sun-like star, about the size of the Earth but with roughly the mass of the Sun, so one teaspoon would weigh tonnes. No fusion happens. Gravity is resisted by electron (degeneracy) pressure: electrons packed extremely closely resist being squeezed any further, regardless of temperature. So the white dwarf does not collapse; it just slowly cools, over billions of years, towards a black dwarf.
- Chandrasekhar Limit: about 1.4 solar masses. Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar showed that electron pressure can support a stellar remnant only up to this mass. A remnant core below the limit becomes a stable white dwarf. A core above the limit cannot be held up by electrons; gravity forces electrons and protons together to make neutrons, and the core collapses further. (A white dwarf in a binary that gains mass from its companion and exceeds the limit explodes completely as a type Ia supernova.)
- Neutron star: if the collapsing core is above the Chandrasekhar Limit but below about 2 to 3 solar masses, it is stopped by neutron (degeneracy) pressure, forming a neutron star: about 20 km across, with a density like an atomic nucleus. Neutron stars spin very rapidly and have intense magnetic fields; some emit beams of radio waves that sweep past the Earth as regular pulses: pulsars.
- Black hole: if the remnant core is more than about 2 to 3 solar masses, even neutron pressure cannot stop gravity. The core collapses into a black hole, a region where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape from within its event horizon.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Chandrasekhar Limit applies to the whole star's starting mass. It is the maximum mass of the remnant core that electron pressure can support.
- Saying a white dwarf is supported by fusion.

### 14.9 and 14.10 Stages and timescales of stellar evolution

#### What you need to know

- The stages and timescales of evolution for stars of about the Sun's mass: emission and absorption nebula, main sequence star, red giant, planetary nebula, white dwarf, black dwarf.
- The stages and timescales for stars of much greater mass: emission and absorption nebula, main sequence star, red supergiant, supernova, neutron star, black hole.

#### Explained

Both start in a nebula: a giant cloud of gas (mostly hydrogen) and dust.

- An absorption (dark) nebula is a cold, dense cloud of dust and gas that blocks light from stars behind it (for example the Horsehead Nebula). Stars form inside such clouds when part of the cloud collapses under gravity, perhaps triggered by a nearby supernova shock wave.
- An emission nebula is a cloud of gas lit up from within by hot young stars: their ultraviolet radiation ionises the hydrogen, which glows red (for example the Orion Nebula, M42). Emission nebulae mark regions of recent star formation.

Collapsing clumps form protostars, which heat up over tens of thousands to millions of years until fusion begins.

| Stage | Sun-like star (about 1 solar mass) | Massive star (over about 8 solar masses) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Formation | Collapse in nebula; protostar for tens of millions of years | Faster collapse (hundreds of thousands of years) |
| Main sequence | About 10 billion years | Only a few million to tens of millions of years (more fuel, but used much faster) |
| Giant phase | Red giant: about 1 billion years | Red supergiant: hundreds of thousands to a million years |
| End | Planetary nebula: glowing shell of gas expanding away for tens of thousands of years (for example the Ring Nebula, M57) | Supernova: core collapse and explosion in seconds; shines for weeks to months; remnant expands for thousands of years (for example the Crab Nebula, M1) |
| Remnant | White dwarf, cooling over billions of years | Neutron star (core 1.4 to about 3 solar masses) or black hole (core above about 3 solar masses) |
| Final state | Black dwarf: a cold, dark white dwarf. It takes so long (longer than the current age of the Universe) that none exist yet | Neutron star slowly spins down; black hole remains |

Main sequence lifetime is much shorter for massive stars: a star 10 times the Sun's mass has about 10 times the fuel but is thousands of times more luminous, so it lasts only about 20 million years.

Supernovae and planetary nebulae return elements made by fusion (such as carbon, oxygen and iron) to space; supernovae also create elements heavier than iron. This enriched gas forms new nebulae, stars and planets.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a black dwarf has been observed.
- Saying massive stars live longer because they have more fuel.
- Saying planetary nebulae have anything to do with planets. The name comes from their round, planet-like appearance in early telescopes.

### 14.11 Evidence for black holes

#### What you need to know

- How astronomers study and gather evidence for the existence of black holes.

#### Explained

A black hole emits no light, so it is detected through its effects on its surroundings:

- Orbits of nearby objects: stars near the centre of the Milky Way orbit an invisible object at very high speeds. Using Kepler's third law, its mass is about 4 million solar masses, packed into a tiny region: a supermassive black hole called Sagittarius A*. The orbits were tracked in infrared over decades.
- X-ray binaries: in some binary systems, a visible star orbits an unseen companion that is too massive to be a neutron star (more than about 3 solar masses). Gas pulled off the visible star spirals into an accretion disc around the companion and is heated to millions of degrees, emitting X-rays. Cygnus X-1 was the first strong black hole candidate.
- Jets and active galaxies: powerful jets and enormous luminosity from galaxy centres (AGN, 15.10) are explained by matter falling into supermassive black holes.
- Gravitational lensing: black holes bend the light of objects behind them.
- Gravitational waves: since 2015 detectors such as LIGO have measured ripples in space-time from pairs of black holes spiralling together and merging.
- Direct imaging of the shadow: the Event Horizon Telescope (a worldwide radio array, 13.26) imaged the dark shadow surrounded by a bright ring of hot gas around the supermassive black holes in the galaxy M87 (2019) and in our own Galaxy (2022).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying black holes are seen directly through telescopes. Evidence comes from their effects on matter and light.
- Saying a black hole sucks in everything around it. At a distance, its gravity is like that of any object of the same mass.

## Topic 15 Our place in the Galaxy (Paper 2)

The Sun is one of a few hundred billion stars in the Milky Way, which is one galaxy among billions. This topic covers the Milky Way's appearance and structure, how radio waves reveal it, the Local Group, classifying galaxies, active galaxies, clusters and superclusters, and how galaxies form and evolve.

### 15.1 The Milky Way through binoculars and small telescopes

#### What you need to know

- The appearance of the Milky Way when seen with binoculars or a small telescope.

#### Explained

To the naked eye the Milky Way is a faint, misty band (6.21). Through binoculars or a small telescope, the glow breaks up into vast numbers of individual faint stars, too many to count, showing that it is made of stars (as Galileo first saw). Rich star clouds (for example in Sagittarius and Scutum), open star clusters, emission nebulae and dark dust lanes and clouds become visible within the band. Looking away from the band, far fewer stars are seen: the difference in star density inside and outside the plane of the Galaxy is what tasks A8 and B8 measure.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a telescope shows the Milky Way's spiral shape. We are inside it, so we cannot see its overall shape directly.

### 15.2 Size and shape of the Galaxy

#### What you need to know

- The size and shape of our Galaxy, and the location of the Sun, dust, sites of star formation and globular clusters.

#### Explained

- Shape: a barred spiral. A thin, flat disc with spiral arms, a central bulge with a bar of stars through it, surrounded by a roughly spherical halo.
- Size: the disc is about 100 000 light years (about 30 kpc) across, but only about 1000 light years thick. It contains roughly 100 to 400 billion stars.
- The Sun: in the disc, near (between) spiral arms, in a small spur called the Orion Arm, about 26 000 light years (8 kpc) from the centre, about halfway out. It orbits the centre once every 220 to 250 million years.
- Dust and gas: concentrated in the thin disc, especially along the spiral arms. Dust blocks visible light from the Galactic centre and the far side of the disc.
- Star formation: in the spiral arms, where gas and dust are compressed into dense clouds; marked by emission nebulae and young open clusters of bright blue stars.
- Globular clusters: about 150, orbiting in the halo in a roughly spherical distribution centred on the Galactic centre. They contain very old stars. (Harlow Shapley used their distribution in 1918 to show the Sun is not at the centre of the Galaxy.)
- Centre: in the direction of Sagittarius, containing the supermassive black hole Sagittarius A* (14.11).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Sun is at the centre of the Galaxy.
- Saying globular clusters are found in the spiral arms. They are in the halo.

### 15.3 Mapping the Galaxy with 21 cm radio waves

#### What you need to know

- Why 21 cm radio waves, rather than visible light, are used to determine the structure and rotation of our Galaxy.

#### Explained

Neutral hydrogen atoms, which are spread through the disc and concentrated in the spiral arms, occasionally emit radio waves with a wavelength of 21 cm. Visible light from distant parts of the disc is blocked by dust, so optical telescopes can only see a few thousand light years in the plane. Radio waves of 21 cm pass straight through dust, so they reach us from right across the Galaxy.

Radio telescopes measure the 21 cm emission in each direction. Hydrogen clouds moving towards or away from us have their 21 cm line Doppler shifted. Since each arm is at a different distance and moves at a different speed relative to us, the shifts separate the arms and show where they are, revealing the spiral structure. Measuring the speeds at different distances from the centre gives the Galaxy's rotation curve. This shows that the outer parts rotate much faster than expected from the visible mass, evidence for dark matter (16.10).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying 21 cm waves are used because they are brighter. They are used because they pass through dust and come from the widespread hydrogen.

### 15.4 and 15.5 The Local Group

#### What you need to know

- The group of galaxies gravitationally linked to the Milky Way is the Local Group.
- Its composition and scale, including the Andromeda Galaxy (M31), the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds (LMC and SMC) and the Triangulum Galaxy (M33).

#### Explained

The Local Group contains over 50 galaxies, mostly small dwarf galaxies, within a region about 10 million light years (3 Mpc) across, held together by gravity.

| Member | Type | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Milky Way | Barred spiral | Second largest; many satellite dwarf galaxies |
| Andromeda Galaxy (M31) | Spiral | Largest member; about 2.5 million light years away; most distant object easily visible to the naked eye; approaching us and expected to merge with the Milky Way in about 4 to 5 billion years |
| Triangulum Galaxy (M33) | Spiral | Third largest; about 2.7 million light years; a difficult naked-eye object from dark sites |

| Small Magellanic Cloud (SMC) | Irregular (dwarf) | Satellite of the Milky Way, about 200 000 light years away; southern sky |

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Magellanic Clouds are nebulae inside the Milky Way. They are separate galaxies.
- Saying the Local Group galaxies are expanding away from each other. They are bound by gravity; Andromeda is approaching us (blueshifted).

### 15.6, 15.7 and 15.8 Classifying galaxies

#### What you need to know

- Classify galaxies using the Hubble system: spiral, barred spiral, elliptical, irregular.
- How Hubble placed the types on his "tuning fork" diagram.
- The Milky Way is a barred spiral of type SBb.

#### Explained

- Elliptical (E0 to E7): smooth, featureless, round to elongated oval shapes; the number shows how elongated (E0 round, E7 very flattened). Mainly old, red-yellow stars, little gas or dust, little new star formation. They range from dwarfs to the largest galaxies known.
- Spiral (Sa, Sb, Sc): a central bulge and a flat disc with spiral arms. Sa: large bulge, tightly wound arms; Sc: small bulge, loosely wound open arms. Contain gas, dust and young blue stars in the arms.
- Barred spiral (SBa, SBb, SBc): like spirals, but the arms start from the ends of a straight bar of stars through the centre.
- Irregular (Irr): no regular shape, often rich in gas and active star formation; often disturbed by interactions with other galaxies (the Magellanic Clouds).
- Lenticular (S0): disc and bulge but no spiral arms; at the junction of the diagram.

Hubble's tuning fork diagram (1926): the ellipticals form the handle on the left, from E0 to E7, leading to S0 at the fork. The fork then splits into two prongs: normal spirals (Sa, Sb, Sc) along one prong and barred spirals (SBa, SBb, SBc) along the other. Irregulars are placed separately. Hubble originally thought galaxies might evolve along the diagram, but this is not correct; it is a classification by appearance only.

The Milky Way is SBb: a barred spiral with a medium-sized bulge and moderately wound arms.

#### Common mistakes

- Thinking the tuning fork shows evolution from ellipticals to spirals.
- Mixing up E0 (round) and E7 (elongated).

### 15.9 to 15.12 Active galaxies

#### What you need to know

- Some galaxies emit large amounts of radiation in addition to visible light.
- An Active Galactic Nucleus (AGN) is powered by matter falling onto a supermassive black hole.
- Types of active galaxy: Seyfert galaxies, quasars and blazars.
- Information about AGNs can be obtained from many regions of the electromagnetic spectrum.

#### Explained

Normal galaxies shine mainly by the light of their stars. Active galaxies emit huge amounts of energy, often far more than all their stars together, from a tiny central region, across the spectrum: radio, infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays.

The engine: a supermassive black hole (millions to billions of solar masses) at the centre. Gas, dust and stars falling towards it form a hot, spinning accretion disc, heated to extreme temperatures by friction, emitting intense radiation. Some AGNs shoot narrow jets of particles at nearly the speed of light from the poles, which can extend far beyond the galaxy and produce giant radio lobes.

| Type | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Seyfert galaxy | A spiral galaxy with an unusually bright, compact nucleus; strong emission lines from fast-moving gas; relatively nearby and less powerful |
| Quasar (QSO, quasi-stellar object) | Extremely luminous, very distant AGN; looks star-like in optical images because the nucleus outshines the whole galaxy; high redshifts, so we see them as they were billions of years ago |
| Blazar | An AGN whose jet points almost directly towards Earth, so we see it very bright and rapidly varying in brightness (in hours to days); strong gamma-ray emission |

The different types may be the same kind of object seen from different angles and at different power levels.

Studying AGNs across the spectrum: radio telescopes show jets and lobes; infrared shows dust heated by the nucleus; optical and ultraviolet show the hot accretion disc and emission lines; X-ray telescopes probe the hottest gas very close to the black hole; gamma-ray telescopes see the most energetic jet emission, especially from blazars. Each part of the spectrum reveals a different region and process, so combining them gives a full picture.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the energy comes from inside the black hole. It comes from matter heated in the accretion disc before it crosses the event horizon.
- Saying quasars are stars. They are the nuclei of distant galaxies.

### 15.13 Clusters and superclusters

#### What you need to know

- Why galaxies are grouped into larger clusters and superclusters.

#### Explained

Galaxies attract each other by gravity. Over billions of years, gravity has pulled galaxies together into groups (like the Local Group, tens of galaxies) and clusters (hundreds to thousands of galaxies, such as the Virgo Cluster about 55 million light years away). Clusters and groups are themselves arranged into superclusters, hundreds of millions of light years across (the Local Group is part of the Virgo Supercluster, itself part of the larger Laniakea Supercluster). On the largest scales, galaxies form a "cosmic web" of filaments and sheets around huge empty voids.

This structure grew from tiny differences in density in the early Universe (the fluctuations seen in the CMB, 16.9): slightly denser regions attracted more matter, and dark matter's gravity helped them grow. Superclusters are not fully bound: the expansion of the Universe is still stretching them apart.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying galaxies are spread evenly through space.

### 15.14 Formation and evolution of galaxies

#### What you need to know

- The main theories for the formation and evolution of galaxies.

#### Explained

- Top-down theory (monolithic collapse): a large cloud of gas collapsed early on to form a whole galaxy at once. Fast collapse with rapid star formation would give an elliptical galaxy; slower collapse while rotating would leave gas to settle into a disc and form a spiral.
- Bottom-up theory (hierarchical merging): small structures (gas clumps and dwarf galaxies) formed first, then merged over time into larger and larger galaxies. This is supported by computer simulations including dark matter, and by observations: very distant (young) galaxies are smaller and more irregular; galaxy collisions and mergers are seen happening now; large ellipticals are thought to be the result of mergers of spirals. The Milky Way is still absorbing dwarf galaxies, and will merge with Andromeda.

Most astronomers favour the bottom-up model, with dark matter halos providing the gravity that gathers gas into galaxies. Interactions trigger bursts of star formation and can feed supermassive black holes, making AGNs more common in the early Universe (quasars are mostly very distant).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying all galaxies formed at the same time in their present shapes.

## Topic 16 Cosmology (Paper 2)

Cosmology is the study of the Universe as a whole: its origin, structure and future. This topic covers redshift and the redshift formula, Hubble's law and the age of the Universe, the Big Bang and Steady State theories and the evidence for the Big Bang, the cosmic microwave background, dark matter and dark energy, and the possible futures of the Universe. Simpler treatments are in [AQA GCSE Physics 4.8](../../../aqa/gcse/physics-8463/4.8-space-physics.md) and the [Edexcel GCSE Physics astronomy file](../../edexcel-sciences/physics-04-astronomy.md).

Equations (given): (λ − λ₀) ÷ λ₀ = v ÷ c; v = H₀d.
Data: H₀ = 68 km/s/Mpc; c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s; 1 pc = 3.1 × 10¹³ km.

### 16.1, 16.2 and 16.3 Redshift

#### What you need to know

- Light from galaxies outside the Local Group is shifted to longer wavelengths (redshift).
- Redshift is caused by galaxies moving away (receding) from us.
- Use (λ − λ₀) ÷ λ₀ = v ÷ c, where λ is the observed wavelength, λ₀ the emitted wavelength, v the radial velocity of the source and c the speed of light.

#### Explained

The absorption lines in a galaxy's spectrum have the same pattern as in the laboratory, but every line is moved to a longer wavelength, towards the red end. Vesto Slipher found in the 1910s that most galaxies are redshifted. Apart from a few in the Local Group (such as Andromeda, which is approaching and is blueshifted), every galaxy is redshifted.

This is like the Doppler effect: waves from a source moving away are stretched to longer wavelengths. (Strictly, cosmological redshift is caused by the expansion of space stretching the light as it travels, but at GCSE the redshift formula treats it as a recession velocity.)

The fractional change in wavelength, (λ − λ₀) ÷ λ₀, is called the redshift z, and equals v ÷ c for speeds much less than the speed of light.

#### Worked example

A hydrogen line emitted at λ₀ = 656.3 nm is observed in a galaxy at λ = 669.4 nm.

- Δλ = 669.4 − 656.3 = 13.1 nm.
- v ÷ c = 13.1 ÷ 656.3 = 0.0200.
- v = 0.0200 × 3.0 × 10⁸ = 6.0 × 10⁶ m/s = 6000 km/s (moving away).

#### Common mistakes

- Dividing by the observed wavelength instead of the emitted wavelength.
- Mixing m/s and km/s.
- Saying redshift means the galaxy looks red.

### 16.4, 16.5 and 16.6 Expansion, Hubble's law and the age of the Universe

#### What you need to know

- The evidence that confirmed the discovery of the expanding Universe.
- Use Hubble's law v = H₀d, where v is the recession velocity, H₀ the Hubble constant and d the distance.
- How the Hubble constant is used to estimate the age and size of the Universe.

#### Explained

In 1929 Edwin Hubble combined Slipher's redshift velocities with his own distances to galaxies (found using Cepheid variables, 13.16). He found that the further away a galaxy is, the faster it is receding: speed is directly proportional to distance. A graph of recession velocity against distance is a straight line through the origin; its gradient is the Hubble constant, H₀. The modern value is about 68 km/s per megaparsec: for every megaparsec of distance, galaxies recede about 68 km/s faster.

Evidence for expansion: almost all galaxies are redshifted, and redshift increases in proportion to distance. This is exactly what happens if the whole Universe is expanding uniformly: every galaxy sees every other galaxy moving away, with more distant ones moving faster, like dots on an inflating balloon or raisins in a rising loaf. There is no centre.

Age: if the galaxies have been moving apart at constant speed, then running time backwards they were all together at time t = d ÷ v = 1 ÷ H₀. Converting units: 1 ÷ (68 km/s/Mpc). 1 Mpc = 3.1 × 10¹⁹ km. So t = 3.1 × 10¹⁹ ÷ 68 = 4.6 × 10¹⁷ s ≈ 14 billion years. (The accepted age is 13.8 billion years.) A larger H₀ means a faster expansion and so a younger Universe.

Size: the observable Universe's radius is roughly the distance light could travel in the age of the Universe, about 14 billion light years (the actual distance to the edge of the observable Universe is larger, about 46 billion light years, because space has expanded while the light travelled).

#### Worked example

1. A galaxy is 150 Mpc away. v = 68 × 150 = 10 200 km/s.
2. A galaxy recedes at 3400 km/s. d = v ÷ H₀ = 3400 ÷ 68 = 50 Mpc.
3. Combined: a galaxy's redshift is 0.010. v = 0.010 × 300 000 km/s = 3000 km/s; d = 3000 ÷ 68 ≈ 44 Mpc.

#### Common mistakes

- Using c in m/s with H₀ in km/s.
- Forgetting the unit conversion when finding 1 ÷ H₀.
- Saying we are at the centre of the expansion.

### 16.7 Big Bang and Steady State

#### What you need to know

- How the expansion of the Universe supports both the Big Bang theory and the Steady State theory.

#### Explained

- Big Bang theory: the Universe began about 13.8 billion years ago from an extremely hot, dense state and has been expanding and cooling ever since. As it expands, galaxies get further apart and the average density falls. It has a beginning and changes with time.
- Steady State theory (Hoyle, Bondi and Gold, 1948): the Universe has no beginning or end and looks the same at all times and places. It is expanding, but new matter is continuously created in the gaps between galaxies (a few hydrogen atoms per cubic metre per billion years), forming new galaxies, so the average density stays constant.

Both theories explain the expansion and the redshift–distance relationship, so redshift alone cannot decide between them. Other evidence is needed (16.8): it showed that the Universe has changed over time, which the Steady State theory cannot explain.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Steady State theory denies expansion. It accepts expansion but adds continuous creation of matter.
- Saying the Big Bang was an explosion at a point in space. Space itself has been expanding everywhere.

### 16.8 Evidence for the Big Bang

#### What you need to know

- The major observational evidence for the Big Bang: quasars (QSOs), cosmic microwave background (CMB) radiation, and the Hubble Deep Field image.

#### Explained

- Quasars: almost all quasars have large redshifts, so they are very far away; we see them as they were billions of years ago, because light takes billions of years to reach us. There are no nearby quasars. So the Universe was different in the past (more active galaxies then). The Steady State theory says the Universe should look the same at all times, so quasars should be spread evenly at all distances. Quasars support an evolving Universe, as in the Big Bang theory.
- Cosmic microwave background (CMB): discovered by accident in 1965 by Penzias and Wilson as a faint microwave signal coming equally from every direction. It is the afterglow of the hot early Universe. About 380 000 years after the Big Bang, the Universe cooled enough (to about 3000 K) for atoms to form and become transparent, releasing radiation. Expansion since then has stretched its wavelength by about 1100 times, into microwaves, matching a temperature of about 2.7 K. The Big Bang theory predicted this radiation; the Steady State theory has no natural explanation for it. This is the strongest evidence.
- Hubble Deep Field (1995): the Hubble Space Telescope stared at a tiny, apparently empty patch of sky for about 10 days. The image showed around 3000 galaxies, many extremely distant and seen as they were billions of years ago. These young galaxies look smaller, more irregular and different from nearby galaxies, showing galaxies have evolved over time, which supports the Big Bang. (Later images, such as the Hubble Ultra Deep Field and JWST deep fields, go further back.)

Also: the observed amounts of hydrogen and helium (about 3 : 1 by mass) match predictions of fusion in the first few minutes after the Big Bang.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the CMB is light from the first stars.
- Saying the Hubble Deep Field shows the edge of the Universe.

### 16.9 Fluctuations in the CMB

#### What you need to know

- The significance of fluctuations in the CMB for theories of the evolution of the Universe, including the discoveries of the WMAP and Planck missions.

#### Explained

The CMB is extremely smooth, the same temperature in every direction to about 1 part in 100 000. But space missions found tiny variations (anisotropies): slightly warmer and cooler patches.

- COBE (1992) first detected these fluctuations.
- WMAP (Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe, 2001 to 2010) mapped them across the whole sky in much more detail.
- Planck (ESA, 2009 to 2013) produced the most detailed map.

Significance: the patches show slightly denser and less dense regions in the early Universe. Gravity made the denser regions grow over billions of years into the galaxies, clusters and superclusters we see today (15.13). Without these seeds, structure could not have formed. The pattern and sizes of the patches let astronomers measure key properties of the Universe very precisely: its age (13.8 billion years), that its geometry is flat, and its make-up: about 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter and 68% dark energy. These measurements strongly support the Big Bang model with dark matter and dark energy.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the fluctuations disprove the Big Bang. They are exactly what is needed for structure to form, and they confirm the model.

### 16.10 and 16.11 Dark matter and dark energy

#### What you need to know

- The significance and possible nature of dark matter and dark energy.
- The difficulties in detecting them.

#### Explained

Dark matter:

- Evidence: the outer parts of spiral galaxies rotate much faster than expected from the visible stars and gas (flat rotation curves, 15.3), so there must be extra unseen mass holding them together; galaxies in clusters move too fast for the cluster to stay bound by visible mass alone; gravitational lensing shows more mass than is visible; it is needed for structure to grow from the CMB fluctuations.
- Possible nature: matter that does not emit, absorb or reflect light and interacts mainly through gravity. Ideas include WIMPs (weakly interacting massive particles) and axions; some suggested ordinary dark objects (MACHOs, such as faint stars and black holes) but these cannot account for most of it. Some scientists propose changing the law of gravity instead.
- Significance: about 85% of all matter in the Universe is dark matter.

Dark energy:

- Evidence: in 1998, observations of type Ia supernovae in distant galaxies (used as standard candles) showed they were fainter, so further away, than expected: the expansion of the Universe is accelerating, not slowing down.
- Possible nature: an unknown form of energy that fills space and causes repulsion, perhaps a property of empty space itself (the "cosmological constant").
- Significance: it makes up about 68% of the total energy content of the Universe and controls its future.

Difficulties in detection: neither interacts with light, so neither can be seen with any telescope; dark matter particles interact so weakly with ordinary matter that very sensitive underground detectors have not yet found them; particle accelerators have not produced them; dark energy is spread extremely thinly and is only noticed on the scale of the whole Universe. We only know about them from their gravitational effects.

#### Common mistakes

- Confusing dark matter (extra gravity, holds galaxies together) with dark energy (accelerates expansion).
- Saying dark matter is black holes or dust clouds. These are ordinary matter and cannot account for most of it.

### 16.12 The future of the Universe

#### What you need to know

- Current models predict different possible futures for the Universe.

#### Explained

The fate depends on the balance between gravity (from matter and dark matter) slowing expansion and dark energy speeding it up:

- Big Freeze (Heat Death): the Universe expands for ever. Galaxies move further apart, stars use up their fuel and die, and everything becomes cold and dark. This is the most likely future given the measured accelerating expansion.
- Big Rip: if dark energy grows stronger with time, the acceleration becomes so great that galaxies, stars, planets and eventually atoms are torn apart.
- Big Crunch: if gravity were strong enough (density above a critical value) or dark energy reversed, expansion would stop and reverse, and the Universe would collapse back into a hot, dense state, perhaps followed by another Big Bang (a "Big Bounce").

Which future happens depends on the true nature of dark energy, which is not yet known. Current observations favour continued, accelerating expansion.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Universe will definitely end in a Big Crunch. Present evidence favours expansion for ever.

## Observational skills and observation tasks

Every 1AS0 student must carry out at least one unaided (naked-eye) and one aided (binoculars, telescope or camera) observation task, chosen from different rows of the board's list, and keep a record. The tasks are not marked, but both papers ask about planning, making, analysing and evaluating observations. This file covers the four skill areas and the method behind each task.

### Obs 1 Designing observations

#### What you need to know

- Produce a list of feasible targets, locations, dates, times and instruments for the task.
- Use astronomical data to select the most appropriate target, location, date, time or instrument.
- Write an initial observing programme, including health and safety.
- Refine the programme after initial trials.

#### Explained

Astronomers cannot control their "experiment": the weather, the Moon's phase, the time of year and the object's position are fixed. So planning is essential.

- Target: is it above the horizon in darkness at a good altitude (6.12)? Is it bright enough for the instrument? Is the Moon nearby or bright?
- Date and time: use a planisphere, app or almanac to find when the object culminates, the Moon's phase, and dates of events (meteor shower peaks, eclipses, transits, maxima of variable stars).
- Location: dark sky (low light pollution), clear horizon in the right direction, safe access, stable ground.
- Instrument: naked eye, binoculars (wide field, easy), telescope (magnification, resolution, light grasp), camera (long exposures, measurements).
- Health and safety: never look at the Sun without proper protection; warm clothing; red torch; tell someone where you are; trip hazards in the dark; electrical safety outdoors.
- Refinement: after a trial night, change the timing, location, exposure or method to improve the data.

#### Common mistakes

- Planning to observe an object that is below the horizon or in daylight at the chosen time.

### Obs 2 Making observations

#### What you need to know

- Carry out unaided observations using techniques such as dark adaptation and averted vision.
- Carry out aided observations with binoculars, telescopes and cameras.
- Record full details with every observation: date, time, location, seeing conditions, instruments used.
- Take enough observations to give accurate data.

#### Explained

Good practice: allow 20 to 30 minutes for dark adaptation; use averted vision for faint objects (6.19); record times in UT; describe seeing (for example on a 1 to 5 scale) and transparency; note the Moon's phase and any cloud; for drawings, note orientation (north and east) and the field of view; for photographs, record exposure time, ISO or gain, focal length and any filter. Repeat observations on several nights to reduce the effect of random errors.

#### Common mistakes

- Recording a drawing without date, time or orientation, which makes it useless for comparison.

### Obs 3 Analysing observations

#### What you need to know

- Identify patterns or trends in a series of drawings or photographs.
- Present numerical data as graphs or charts.
- Perform calculations.
- Perform basic digital image processing: adjusting brightness and dynamic range, and using false colour.

#### Explained

- Patterns: for example, the movement of a sunspot from day to day, or changes in the shading of a lunar crater as the terminator passes.
- Graphs: light curves (magnitude against time, magnitudes increasing downwards so brighter is up), shadow length against time, sunspot position against date.
- Calculations: rotation period from angle per day; sidereal day from star trails; longitude from time difference; mean values and ranges.
- Image processing: stretching the brightness levels to reveal faint detail; adjusting contrast; stacking several images to reduce noise; false colour, where different brightness levels or wavelengths are given colours to show structure that the eye cannot easily see.

### Obs 4 Evaluating observations

#### What you need to know

- Compare results with professional images or accepted values to assess accuracy; calculate a quantitative measure of accuracy where possible.
- Identify the effects of light pollution on naked-eye observations, and of light pollution, exposure time and filtering on photographs.
- Recognise major image artefacts: scattered light, diffraction spikes, cosmic rays, trails from satellites, aircraft or meteors.
- Identify the major causes of error and suggest and implement improvements.

#### Explained

Percentage error = (your value − accepted value) ÷ accepted value × 100%.

Artefacts:

- Scattered light: glare or gradients from nearby lights or the Moon.
- Diffraction spikes: crossed lines from bright stars, caused by the supports of a reflector's secondary mirror.
- Cosmic rays: single bright pixels or short streaks, appearing randomly on one frame only.
- Trails: straight lines from satellites or aircraft (aircraft trails may be dotted by flashing lights); a meteor trail may be brighter in the middle and fade at the ends.

Exposure time: too short and faint objects are missed; too long and bright stars saturate, light pollution fogs the image, and stars trail if the camera is not tracking. Filters can cut light pollution or isolate one wavelength (such as H-alpha).

Typical sources of error: timing errors (reaction time); estimating rather than measuring positions; poor seeing; light pollution; small number of observations; the observer's eye judging brightness inconsistently.

## 4.8 Space physics (Physics only)

The whole of this topic is Physics only. It covers the solar system and how it formed, the life cycle of stars and where the elements come from, orbits of planets and satellites, and red-shift as evidence for the Big Bang.

### 4.8.1.1 Our solar system

#### What you need to know

- Within our solar system there is one star, the Sun, plus the eight planets and the dwarf planets that orbit around the Sun. Natural satellites, the moons, orbit planets.
- Our solar system is a small part of the Milky Way galaxy.
- The Sun was formed from a cloud of dust and gas (nebula) pulled together by gravitational attraction.
- You should be able to explain how, at the start of a star's life cycle, the dust and gas drawn together by gravity causes fusion reactions.
- Fusion reactions lead to an equilibrium between the gravitational collapse of a star and the expansion of a star due to fusion energy.

#### Explained

Scale: the Earth is one planet in a solar system around one star; the Sun is one of billions of stars in the Milky Way; the Milky Way is one of billions of galaxies in the universe.

Formation of the Sun:

1. Gravity pulls a cloud of dust and gas (a nebula) together.
2. As it collapses, it gets denser and hotter, forming a protostar.
3. When the core is hot and dense enough, hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium, releasing energy. The star is born.

A stable star is a balance of two effects: gravity pulling inwards and the outward pressure from the energy released by fusion. While these are equal, the star stays the same size. This is the main sequence stage, which the Sun is in now.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Sun burns hydrogen like a fire. It fuses hydrogen nuclei; there is no chemical burning.
- Calling Pluto a planet. It is a dwarf planet.

### 4.8.1.2 The life cycle of a star

#### What you need to know

- A star goes through a life cycle. The life cycle is determined by the size of the star.
- Fusion processes in stars produce all of the naturally occurring elements. Elements heavier than iron are produced in a supernova.
- The explosion of a massive star (supernova) distributes the elements throughout the universe.
- You should be able to describe the life cycle of a star the size of the Sun, and of a star much more massive than the Sun.
- You should be able to explain how fusion processes lead to the formation of new elements.

#### Explained

Both paths start the same way: nebula → protostar → main sequence star.

| Star about the size of the Sun | Star much more massive than the Sun |
| --- | --- |
| Main sequence star | Main sequence star (shorter life, because it fuses its hydrogen much faster) |
| Red giant: when hydrogen in the core runs low, the star swells and cools on the surface; helium fuses into heavier elements such as carbon | Red supergiant: swells even more; fusion makes heavier and heavier elements up to iron |
| White dwarf: the outer layers drift away and the hot, dense core is left; it shrinks and cools | Supernova: the core collapses and the star explodes, forming elements heavier than iron and scattering all elements into space |
| Black dwarf: a white dwarf that has cooled so much it no longer gives out much energy | Neutron star (very dense core left behind) or, for the most massive stars, a black hole (gravity so strong not even light can escape) |

Making elements: in the main sequence, hydrogen fuses to helium. In a red giant or supergiant, helium fuses into carbon, then oxygen and so on. Fusion in normal stars stops at iron. Elements heavier than iron (such as gold and uranium) are only made in the extreme conditions of a supernova. The explosion scatters these elements into space, where they become part of new nebulas, new stars and planets. The atoms in your body were made in stars.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Sun will become a supernova. Stars the size of the Sun end as white dwarfs.
- Saying elements heavier than iron are made on the main sequence.

### 4.8.1.3 Orbital motion, natural and artificial satellites

#### What you need to know

- Gravity provides the force that allows planets and satellites (both natural and artificial) to maintain their circular orbits.
- You should be able to describe the similarities and distinctions between the planets, their moons, and artificial satellites.
- (HT only) You should be able to explain qualitatively how, for circular orbits, the force of gravity can lead to changing velocity but unchanged speed.
- (HT only) You should be able to explain qualitatively how, for a stable orbit, the radius must change if the speed changes.

#### Explained

| | Planets | Moons | Artificial satellites |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Orbit around | The Sun | A planet | A planet (usually Earth) |
| Made by | Nature | Nature | People |
| Held in orbit by | Gravity of the Sun | Gravity of the planet | Gravity of the Earth |
| Examples | Earth, Mars, Jupiter | The Moon, moons of Jupiter | Weather, communication and GPS satellites; the International Space Station |

All orbits at GCSE are treated as nearly circular, and all are kept going by gravity.

(HT only) Constant speed, changing velocity: gravity pulls the satellite towards the centre of the orbit, at right angles to its motion. A force at right angles does not speed the satellite up or slow it down, but it does keep changing its direction. Velocity is a vector, so a change in direction is a change in velocity, which is an acceleration towards the centre.

(HT only) Speed and radius: closer to the planet, gravity is stronger, so to stay in a stable orbit a satellite must move faster (otherwise it would be pulled inwards). Further out, gravity is weaker, so a satellite must move more slowly (otherwise it would fly off). So if the speed of a satellite in a stable orbit changes, the radius of its orbit must change too: a smaller orbit needs a higher speed. Satellites in low orbits go round the Earth in about 90 minutes; geostationary satellites much further out take 24 hours.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying there is no gravity in orbit. Gravity is what keeps the satellite in orbit; astronauts float because they and their spacecraft are falling around the Earth together.
- (HT only) Saying a satellite is not accelerating because its speed is constant. Its direction is changing, so it is accelerating.

### 4.8.2 Red-shift

#### What you need to know

- There is an observed increase in the wavelength of light from most distant galaxies. The further away the galaxies, the faster they are moving and the bigger the observed increase in wavelength. This effect is called red-shift.
- The observed red-shift provides evidence that space itself (the universe) is expanding and supports the Big Bang theory.
- The Big Bang theory suggests that the universe began from a very small region that was extremely hot and dense.
- Since 1998 onwards, observations of supernovae suggest that distant galaxies are receding ever faster.
- You should be able to explain qualitatively the red-shift of light from galaxies that are receding, how the change of each galaxy's speed with distance is evidence of an expanding universe, how red-shift provides evidence for the Big Bang model, and how scientists are able to use observations to arrive at theories such as the Big Bang theory.
- You should be able to explain that there is still much about the universe that is not understood, for example dark mass and dark energy.

#### Explained

Each element absorbs light at particular wavelengths, giving dark lines in a spectrum. In light from a distant galaxy the same pattern of lines appears, but shifted towards the red (longer wavelength) end. This means the galaxy is moving away from us: as it recedes, the waves are stretched out.

Two observations:

1. Almost all distant galaxies are red-shifted, so they are moving away from us.
2. The further away a galaxy is, the bigger its red-shift, so the faster it is moving away.

This is what you would expect if space itself is expanding: think of dots on a balloon being blown up, where every dot moves away from every other, and the dots furthest apart separate fastest. Run the expansion backwards and everything comes together in a tiny, extremely hot, dense region: the Big Bang.

How scientists got there: observations (red-shift of many galaxies) were collected, a theory was proposed to explain them, and further evidence was tested against it. The Big Bang theory replaced the idea of a steady, unchanging universe because it explained the red-shift data better.

Open questions: since 1998, measurements of distant supernovae show that the expansion is speeding up. Scientists do not yet know why; the unknown cause is called dark energy. Galaxies also spin as if they contain much more mass than we can see; this unseen mass is called dark mass (dark matter). Neither is understood yet.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying red-shift means the light turns red. It means the wavelength gets longer, shifting the pattern of lines towards the red end.
- Saying galaxies are moving away from the Earth because we are at the centre. Every galaxy sees the others moving away; there is no centre.

## Astronomy (Edexcel gap file)

The Solar System, star life cycles, orbits and red-shift are in [AQA 8463 4.8](../../aqa/gcse/physics-8463/4.8-space-physics.md). Edexcel's Topic 7 (Physics only) goes further: this file adds what AQA does not teach. It also covers centripetal force (2.21), which is in Combined Science too.

#### Board differences

- AQA does not cover the Steady State theory, the cosmic microwave background, the Doppler effect for sound, the history of Solar System models, comets, or how telescopes have changed. Edexcel examines all of these in GCSE Physics.
- 2.21 is higher tier; it is in both Physics and Combined Science.

### 2.21 Centripetal force (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- An object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating, because its direction (and so its velocity) keeps changing.
- So there must be a resultant force on it, directed towards the centre of the circle. This is the **centripetal force**.
- The centripetal force is provided by another force: tension for a ball on a string, friction for a car turning a corner, gravity for a planet or satellite in orbit.

#### Explained

If the centripetal force stopped (the string snapped), the object would carry on in a straight line along the tangent, as Newton's first law predicts. Centripetal force is not an extra force: it is the name for whichever real force pulls the object towards the centre.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the force acts outwards. The resultant force points to the centre.
- Drawing centripetal force as a separate arrow next to gravity for an orbiting satellite. Gravity *is* the centripetal force.

### 7.1P Weight and g on different bodies (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- Gravitational field strength (g) depends on the mass of the body (planet, moon or star) and how far you are from its centre. A bigger mass gives a bigger g; a larger distance gives a smaller g.
- Mass stays the same everywhere; weight (W = mg) changes with g.

#### Worked example

g on Earth ≈ 10 N/kg; on the Moon ≈ 1.6 N/kg; on Jupiter ≈ 25 N/kg. An astronaut has a mass of 80 kg.

- Weight on Earth = 80 × 10 = 800 N.
- Weight on the Moon = 80 × 1.6 = 128 N.
- Weight on Jupiter = 80 × 25 = 2 000 N.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the astronaut's mass is less on the Moon.

### 7.3P The order of the planets (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

From the Sun outwards: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune. The first four are small and rocky; the last four are large gas or ice giants. The asteroid belt lies between Mars and Jupiter.

### 7.4P How models of the Solar System have changed (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- **Geocentric model** (ancient Greece, Ptolemy): Earth at the centre, with the Sun, Moon, planets and stars moving round it.
- **Heliocentric model** (Copernicus, 1500s): the Sun at the centre, with Earth and the planets orbiting it.
- Evidence that helped the heliocentric model win: Galileo's telescope observations (around 1610) of four moons orbiting Jupiter (so not everything orbits Earth) and of the phases of Venus. Kepler showed planetary orbits are ellipses.
- Later telescopes found Uranus (1781) and Neptune (1846), and the Sun was found to be one of billions of stars in the Milky Way.

#### Explained

This is a good example of how science works: a model is replaced when new evidence, often from new technology, does not fit it.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying Galileo invented the heliocentric model. Copernicus proposed it; Galileo found evidence for it.

### 7.5P Orbits of moons, planets, comets and artificial satellites (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- Planets orbit the Sun and moons orbit planets in nearly circular orbits.
- **Comets** (lumps of ice and dust) orbit the Sun in very elongated (elliptical) orbits. They move fastest when closest to the Sun, where they warm up and form a glowing tail pointing away from the Sun.
- Artificial satellites orbit Earth: geostationary satellites orbit above the equator once every 24 hours, so they stay above the same point (used for communications); low polar orbits pass over the poles every couple of hours and scan the whole Earth (used for weather and mapping).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying comets move at constant speed. They speed up near the Sun and slow down far away.

### 7.8P Steady State and Big Bang theories (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- **Big Bang**: the Universe began about 13.8 billion years ago from an extremely small, hot, dense region and has been expanding and cooling ever since.
- **Steady State**: the Universe has always existed and always looks the same on large scales. It is expanding, and new matter is continually created to fill the gaps, so its density stays constant.

#### Explained

Both theories agree the Universe is expanding. They differ on whether it had a beginning and whether it changes over time.

### 7.9P Evidence for the Big Bang (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- Red-shift: light from distant galaxies is shifted towards the red end of the spectrum; further galaxies show more red-shift, so they are moving away faster. The Universe is expanding (red-shift itself is in the AQA note 4.8.2).
- Cosmic microwave background (CMB) radiation: microwave radiation reaching us from every direction in space.

### 7.10P Why the Big Bang is the accepted model (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- Both theories explain red-shift, but only the Big Bang explains the CMB. With more evidence supporting it, the Big Bang is now the accepted model.

### 7.11P The Doppler effect (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- When a wave source moves relative to an observer, the observed frequency and wavelength change.
- Source moving **towards** the observer: waves are squashed together, so shorter wavelength and higher frequency.
- Source moving **away**: waves are stretched, so longer wavelength and lower frequency.

#### Explained

A siren on an ambulance sounds higher pitched as it approaches and lower as it moves away. The ambulance's siren itself does not change; only the waves reaching you do. For light from a galaxy moving away, the longer wavelength appears as a shift towards red: red-shift.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the sound gets louder, not higher, as the source approaches. Loudness may change too, but the Doppler effect is about frequency.

### 7.14P How both theories explain red-shift (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- Big Bang: galaxies are moving apart because space has been expanding since the beginning; more distant galaxies move away faster, giving more red-shift.
- Steady State: the Universe is also expanding, so galaxies also move apart and show red-shift; new matter forms to keep the density constant.
- So red-shift alone cannot decide between the two.

### 7.15P The CMB and the Big Bang (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- The CMB was discovered in 1965 (by Penzias and Wilson).
- Big Bang explanation: soon after the Big Bang the Universe was extremely hot and gave out high-energy radiation. As space expanded, this radiation was stretched to longer wavelengths, and it now reaches us as microwaves from all directions.
- The Steady State theory has no explanation for this radiation, so its discovery made the Big Bang the accepted theory.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the CMB comes from the Sun or stars. It comes evenly from all directions, from the early Universe.

### 7.19P How we observe the Universe (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- Early astronomers used the naked eye. Telescopes (from 1609) collected more light and magnified, showing fainter and more distant objects.
- Photography (and now digital cameras) let astronomers record images over long exposures.
- Telescopes now detect other parts of the electromagnetic spectrum: radio, microwave, infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays, giving information that visible light does not.
- **Telescopes in space** (such as the Hubble Space Telescope) are above the atmosphere, which absorbs many wavelengths (such as X-rays, UV and much infrared) and makes images blurry. In space there is also no light pollution or weather.
- Disadvantages of space telescopes: very expensive to launch and hard to repair.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying space telescopes are used because they are "closer to the stars". The gain is getting above the atmosphere, not distance.

## How to read the tables

- **Ref**: the spec statement number. A letter at the end (B, C or P) is Edexcel's own mark for content that is in the separate science only, not in Combined Science 1SC0.
- **Content**: a short paraphrase, not the board's wording.
- **Tier**: HT = higher tier only; part HT = the statement is partly higher tier. Tier marks were read automatically from the bold type in the spec PDF and need a person to check them.
- **File**: the existing AQA note and spec section that teaches the same content, and/or the Edexcel gap file in this folder that adds what the AQA note lacks. "(required practical)" means the AQA note's required practical covers the same technique as the Edexcel core practical; where the method differs, the gap file adds it.

## Enzyme rates, food energy, growth charts and DNA extraction (Edexcel gap file)

This file adds the Edexcel content that the AQA GCSE Biology notes do not teach. Enzymes, food tests, mitosis and DNA structure themselves are in the AQA notes; see [map-1BI0.md](map-1BI0.md) for where.

#### Board differences

- 1.13B and 1.14B are Biology only (not in Combined Science 1SC0).

### 1.11 Rate calculations for enzyme activity

#### What you need to know

- Rate is how much change happens per unit of time.
- For an enzyme, rate can be worked out from how fast substrate is used up or product is made.
- When the result is a time to finish (for example, the time for starch to disappear), rate = 1 ÷ time.
- Rate can be read from the gradient of a graph of product (or substrate) against time.

#### Explained

Enzyme experiments rarely measure rate directly. You usually measure one of two things:

1. **A quantity changing over time**, such as the volume of oxygen given off by catalase breaking down hydrogen peroxide. Rate = amount of change ÷ time taken, with units such as cm³/s or cm³/min.
2. **The time for a reaction to reach an end point**, such as the time until iodine stops turning blue-black when amylase digests starch. A shorter time means a faster reaction, so rate = 1 ÷ time, with units s⁻¹ (per second).

On a graph of product made against time, the line is steepest at the start, when there is most substrate. The gradient of the straight early part (change in y ÷ change in x) is the initial rate. The line flattens when the substrate runs out, not because the enzyme has stopped working.

#### Worked example

Amylase is mixed with starch at pH 7. Samples are tested with iodine every 10 seconds. The blue-black colour is last seen at 110 s and gone at 120 s. Find the rate.

- Take the time as 120 s (the first sample with no starch).
- Rate = 1 ÷ 120 = 0.0083 s⁻¹ (2 s.f.).

A second example: catalase produces 36 cm³ of oxygen in the first 60 s. Rate = 36 ÷ 60 = 0.6 cm³/s.

#### Common mistakes

- Writing rate = time. A longer time is a slower rate, so you must use 1 ÷ time.
- Forgetting units. 1 ÷ time in seconds gives s⁻¹.
- Saying the graph levels off because the enzyme is denatured. At a steady temperature and pH it levels off because the substrate is used up.

### 1.13B Core practical: identifying food molecules with chemical reagents (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Starch: add iodine solution; orange-brown turns blue-black if starch is present.
- Reducing sugars (such as glucose): add Benedict's reagent and heat in a water bath at about 80 °C; blue turns green, yellow or brick red depending on how much sugar there is.
- Protein: add Biuret reagent (or potassium hydroxide then copper sulfate solution); blue turns purple (lilac).
- Fats: shake the food with ethanol, then pour the liquid into water; a cloudy white emulsion shows fat.

#### Explained

These tests are qualitative: they show whether a molecule is there. Benedict's is semi-quantitative, because the colour moves from blue through green and yellow to orange and brick red as the sugar concentration rises. Solid foods are ground with a little distilled water first so the molecules are in solution.

The tests for starch, sugars and protein are taught in the AQA note (4.2.2.1 required practical). The one Edexcel names that AQA does not is the **ethanol emulsion test** for fats (AQA uses Sudan III).

#### Common mistakes

- Heating the Biuret or iodine test. Only the Benedict's test needs heat.
- Saying sucrose gives a positive Benedict's test. Sucrose is not a reducing sugar.
- Using a naked flame near ethanol. Ethanol is flammable; keep it away from Bunsen burners.

#### Practical

- Hazards: Benedict's reagent and Biuret reagent are irritants; ethanol is highly flammable; hot water baths can scald. Wear eye protection.
- Use a control (distilled water) with each reagent so you can compare colours.

### 1.14B Measuring the energy in food by calorimetry (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Burning a food sample transfers its stored chemical energy to heat, which can be used to warm a known mass of water.
- Energy transferred (J) = mass of water (g) × 4.2 J/g °C × temperature rise (°C).
- Energy per gram of food = energy transferred ÷ mass of food burned.
- The simple method gives a lower value than the true energy content because much of the heat is lost.

#### Explained

Set-up: a known volume of water (1 cm³ has a mass of 1 g) in a boiling tube clamped above the food. Record the starting water temperature. Weigh a small piece of dry food, hold it on a mounted needle, light it in a Bunsen flame and quickly put it under the tube. When it stops burning, relight it until it will not burn any more. Record the highest temperature and reweigh what is left of the food.

Why the result is too low:

- Heat goes into the air, the glass and the clamp, not just the water.
- The food may not burn completely (black residue).
- Heat is lost while moving the burning food from the flame to the tube.

A food calorimeter (food burned in oxygen inside a chamber surrounded by water) loses much less heat and gives values closer to the true figure.

#### Worked example

0.50 g of a crisp is burned under 20 cm³ of water. The water warms from 19 °C to 43 °C.

- Temperature rise = 43 − 19 = 24 °C.
- Energy = 20 × 4.2 × 24 = 2016 J.
- Energy per gram = 2016 ÷ 0.50 = 4032 J/g ≈ 4.0 kJ/g.

#### Common mistakes

- Using the final temperature instead of the temperature rise.
- Dividing by the starting mass of food when some was left unburned. Use the mass actually burned.
- Saying the error is "human error". Name the real source: heat lost to the surroundings or incomplete burning.

#### Practical

- Hazards: burning food can drop off the needle; work over a heatproof mat. Some foods (nuts) can trigger allergies; avoid them.
- Keep the distance between flame and tube the same each time, and stir the water before reading the temperature.

### 2.7 Percentile charts to monitor growth

#### What you need to know

- A percentile chart shows how a child's measurement (mass, height or head size) compares with other children of the same age and sex.
- The 50th percentile line is the middle value: half of children are above it and half below.
- A child on the 90th percentile is bigger than 90% of children of that age.
- Doctors look for a child who is above the top line or below the bottom line, or whose measurements cross percentile lines quickly, as this can show a problem.

#### Explained

Each curved line on the chart is a percentile. Growth is normal if a child follows roughly the same line as they get older, even if that line is low or high, because children differ. What matters more is the pattern over time:

- Steady growth along the 25th percentile is normal.
- A baby who drops from the 75th to the 9th percentile in a few months may not be getting enough food or may be ill.
- Head size growing much faster than the percentile lines can suggest fluid on the brain.

#### Worked example

A boy is 12 months old and has a mass of 9.6 kg. On the chart this point lies on the 25th percentile line. What does this mean?

- 25% of 12-month-old boys have a mass below 9.6 kg and 75% have a higher mass. This is in the normal range.

#### Common mistakes

- Thinking the 50th percentile is the "correct" size. Any steady line within the chart is normal.
- Reading "90th percentile" as "90% of the average mass".

### 3.6 Extracting DNA from fruit

#### What you need to know

- DNA can be extracted from soft fruit (such as strawberries or kiwi) in the lab.
- The steps break the cells open, separate the DNA from other material and make the DNA come out of solution.

#### Explained

| Step | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Mash the fruit with a mixture of washing-up liquid and salt solution | Detergent breaks down the cell membranes and nuclear membranes, releasing DNA. Salt makes the DNA strands clump together. |
| Warm in a water bath at about 60 °C for 15 minutes, then cool | Helps break up membranes and slows enzymes that would cut up the DNA. Cooling stops the DNA breaking down. |
| Filter | Removes cell walls and other solid debris; the DNA is in the filtrate. |
| (Optional) add a little protease | Breaks down proteins that are bound to the DNA. |
| Pour ice-cold ethanol gently down the side so it forms a layer on top | DNA does not dissolve in cold ethanol, so it comes out as white strands at the boundary. |

The white, stringy material can be wound onto a glass rod. Strawberries work well because their cells have many copies of each chromosome.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the detergent "dissolves the DNA". It breaks down the lipid membranes.
- Mixing the ethanol in. It must form a separate layer so the DNA collects at the boundary.
- Thinking you can see the double helix. You see millions of DNA molecules clumped together.

## Protein synthesis and inheritance patterns (Edexcel gap file)

- Codominance with multiple alleles (ABO blood groups) and sex-linked inheritance are Edexcel only.

### 3.7B Base sequence, amino acids and protein shape (Biology only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- The order of bases in a gene decides the order of amino acids in a protein.
- Each group of three bases (a triplet or codon) codes for one amino acid.
- The chain of amino acids folds into a particular shape, and this shape lets the protein do its job (for example, the active site of an enzyme).

#### Explained

DNA has four bases: A, T, C and G. Three bases together make a code word. For example, the triplet TAC on the template strand leads to the amino acid methionine being added. A gene of 900 bases codes for a protein of 300 amino acids.

Once joined, the amino acid chain folds. Amino acids at different points attract or repel each other, so the order of amino acids decides the final 3D shape. Change the order and the shape may change, so the protein may no longer work.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying one base codes for one amino acid. It takes three.
- Saying the gene "is" the protein. The gene is the instructions; the protein is made from them.

### 3.8B Transcription and translation (Biology only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- **Transcription** happens in the nucleus:
  - RNA polymerase binds to non-coding DNA just in front of a gene.
  - It makes a single strand of messenger RNA (mRNA) that is complementary to the gene's template strand.
  - The mRNA leaves the nucleus through a pore.
- **Translation** happens at a ribosome in the cytoplasm:
  - The mRNA attaches to a ribosome.
  - Transfer RNA (tRNA) molecules bring specific amino acids. Each tRNA has an anticodon that matches a codon on the mRNA.
  - The amino acids are joined in the order of the codons, making a polypeptide chain.

#### Explained

DNA is too big to leave the nucleus and must be kept safe, so the cell makes a working copy of just one gene. RNA is like DNA but single-stranded and uses the base U (uracil) in place of T.

Base pairing in transcription: DNA template A → mRNA U; T → A; C → G; G → C.

At the ribosome, the mRNA is read three bases at a time. A tRNA whose anticodon pairs with the codon brings its amino acid. The ribosome joins this amino acid to the one before, then moves along to the next codon. This continues until a stop codon is reached and the finished chain is released to fold.

#### Worked example

A section of DNA template strand reads: TAC GGA TTC.

- mRNA (complementary, U for A): AUG CCU AAG.
- Three codons, so three amino acids are joined.
- The tRNA anticodons are: UAC GGA UUC.

#### Common mistakes

- Writing T in mRNA. RNA uses U.
- Saying translation happens in the nucleus. It happens at ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
- Mixing up codon (on mRNA) and anticodon (on tRNA).

### 3.9B Variants in non-coding DNA (Biology only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Not all DNA codes for proteins. Some non-coding DNA controls whether genes are switched on.
- A variant (change in the base sequence) in the non-coding region where RNA polymerase binds can change how easily RNA polymerase attaches.
- This changes how much mRNA, and so how much protein, is made, which can change the phenotype.

#### Explained

Think of the non-coding region in front of a gene as a "start here" sign. If a variant makes the sign hard to read, RNA polymerase binds less well, less mRNA is made and less protein is produced. If the sign becomes easier to read, more protein is made. The protein itself is normal; only the amount changes.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying non-coding variants always have no effect. They can change the amount of protein made.

### 3.10B Variants in coding DNA (Biology only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- A variant inside a gene can change a codon, which may change one amino acid in the protein.
- This can change the protein's shape and so its activity. For an enzyme, the active site may no longer fit the substrate.
- Some variants have no effect because the new codon still codes for the same amino acid, or the change is in a part of the protein that does not affect its shape.

#### Explained

Coding variants change *what* protein is made; non-coding variants change *how much*. Examples of coding changes:

- Substitution: one base swapped. May change one amino acid or none.
- Insertion or deletion: shifts every codon after it, so usually many amino acids change and the protein does not work.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying every mutation causes a disease. Most have no effect on the phenotype.

### 3.17B ABO blood groups: codominance and multiple alleles (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- The ABO blood group gene has three alleles: Iᴬ, Iᴮ and Iᴼ. This is an example of **multiple alleles** (more than two forms of a gene in the population). Each person still has only two.
- Iᴬ and Iᴮ are **codominant**: if both are present, both are expressed and the person has blood group AB.
- Iᴼ is recessive to both Iᴬ and Iᴮ.

#### Explained

| Genotype | Blood group |
| --- | --- |
| IᴬIᴬ or IᴬIᴼ | A |
| IᴮIᴮ or IᴮIᴼ | B |
| IᴬIᴮ | AB |
| IᴼIᴼ | O |

Codominance is different from simple dominance: neither allele hides the other, so the heterozygote has a third phenotype that shows both.

#### Worked example

A father is group A (IᴬIᴼ) and a mother is group B (IᴮIᴼ). What blood groups can their children have?

| | Iᴮ | Iᴼ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Iᴬ** | IᴬIᴮ (AB) | IᴬIᴼ (A) |
| **Iᴼ** | IᴮIᴼ (B) | IᴼIᴼ (O) |

Each of AB, A, B and O has a probability of 1 in 4 (25%).

#### Common mistakes

- Writing alleles as A, B and O without the I. Edexcel expects Iᴬ, Iᴮ and Iᴼ.
- Thinking two group A parents cannot have a group O child. If both are IᴬIᴼ, there is a 1 in 4 chance.

### 3.18B Sex-linked genetic disorders (Biology only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Sex-linked disorders are caused by alleles on the X chromosome. The Y chromosome is much smaller and does not carry a matching allele.
- Most are recessive, for example red-green colour blindness and haemophilia.
- Males (XY) have only one X, so a single recessive allele causes the disorder. Females (XX) need two copies; with one copy they are **carriers**.
- So these disorders are much more common in males.

#### Explained

Write the allele as a superscript on the X: Xᴴ (normal clotting) and Xʰ (haemophilia). The Y has no allele.

| Genotype | Phenotype |
| --- | --- |
| XᴴXᴴ | Female, unaffected |
| XᴴXʰ | Female carrier (unaffected) |
| XʰXʰ | Female with haemophilia (rare) |
| XᴴY | Male, unaffected |
| XʰY | Male with haemophilia |

A son always gets his X from his mother, so an affected son inherits the allele from his mother, never his father.

#### Worked example

A carrier mother (XᴴXʰ) and an unaffected father (XᴴY):

| | Xᴴ | Y |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Xᴴ** | XᴴXᴴ | XᴴY |
| **Xʰ** | XᴴXʰ | XʰY |

- Probability a child has haemophilia = 1/4 (25%).
- Probability a son has haemophilia = 1/2 (50%).
- No daughters are affected, but half are carriers.

#### Common mistakes

- Putting the allele on the Y chromosome.
- Saying a father can pass an X-linked disorder to his son. He passes his Y to sons.
- Mixing up "probability a child is affected" and "probability a son is affected".

### 3.21 The Human Genome Project and medicine

#### What you need to know

- The Human Genome Project (finished in 2003) worked out the order of all the bases in human DNA and helped locate about 20 000 genes.
- Possible uses in medicine:
  - finding genes linked to diseases, and testing people for alleles that raise their risk;
  - developing new treatments and drugs targeted at faulty genes;
  - personalised medicine: choosing the drug and dose that works best with a person's genotype.
- There are concerns: people may be anxious about their risk, insurers or employers might discriminate, and having a risk allele does not mean you will get the disease.

#### Explained

Before the project, scientists hunting for a disease gene had to search blind. With the full sequence, they can compare the DNA of people with and without a disease to find the differences. This has led to tests such as screening for alleles that raise breast cancer risk.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the project "cured" genetic diseases. It gave information that helps research and testing.
- Saying a high-risk allele means you will definitely get the disease. It raises the probability.

### 4.4 Fossil evidence for human evolution

#### What you need to know

- Fossils show that humans evolved from earlier ape-like ancestors over millions of years.
- Three finds to know, from oldest to youngest:
  - **Ardi** (*Ardipithecus ramidus*), about 4.4 million years old.
  - **Lucy** (*Australopithecus afarensis*), about 3.2 million years old.
  - Fossils found by **Richard Leakey's** team, about 1.6 million years old (the "Turkana Boy", *Homo erectus*).
- Comparing them shows trends: walking more upright, longer legs, shorter arms, and a bigger brain.

#### Explained

| Feature | Ardi (4.4 mya) | Lucy (3.2 mya) | Leakey's find (1.6 mya) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Brain size | about 350 cm³ (similar to a chimpanzee) | about 450 cm³ | about 850 cm³ |
| Walking | could walk upright, but foot had a grasping big toe for climbing | walked upright; foot more like ours, arched | walked upright, like a modern human |
| Limbs | long arms, short legs | arms and legs between ape and human proportions | short arms, long legs |
| Height | about 1.2 m | about 1.1 m | about 1.6 m |

(Modern humans have a brain of about 1 300 to 1 500 cm³.)

The pattern across these three supports evolution: features change gradually in one direction over time. Brain size roughly doubled between Ardi and the Leakey fossils, and the body moved from part climbing to fully walking on two legs.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying humans evolved from chimpanzees. Humans and chimps share a common ancestor.
- Getting the order wrong. Ardi is oldest, then Lucy, then Leakey's find.
- Saying fossils "prove" each one is the direct ancestor of the next. They show the trend, but some may be side branches.

### 4.5 Stone tool evidence for human evolution

#### What you need to know

- Stone tools became more complex over time, which suggests that brain size and skill increased.
- Tools can be dated by the layer of rock or sediment they are found in (stratigraphy): deeper layers are usually older.
- Volcanic ash layers above and below a tool can be dated using radioactive isotopes (such as the decay of potassium to argon), and carbon dating can date plant or animal remains found with the tools.

#### Explained

A rough timeline of tool types:

| Approximate age | Tools | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2.6 million years | simple pebble tools: a stone with a few flakes knocked off to make a sharp edge (Oldowan) | able to plan simple cutting |
| 1.7 million years | hand axes shaped on both sides into a teardrop (Acheulean) | more planning and skill |
| 300 000 years | smaller flakes shaped from a prepared core, used as points and scrapers | fine control; tools for hunting |
| 40 000 years onward | thin blades, needles, fish hooks, often of bone or antler | complex tool kits, clothing |

Dating from the environment: sediment builds up in layers, so a tool found in a deeper layer was usually left earlier. If a layer of volcanic ash lies above the tool, the tool must be older than that ash.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying carbon dating dates the stone. Stone does not contain carbon from a living thing; carbon dating is used on organic remains found with the tools, and only for the last 50 000 years or so.
- Assuming deeper always means older without considering disturbance (such as earthquakes).

### 4.6B The pentadactyl limb (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- A pentadactyl limb is a limb with five digits (fingers or toes).
- Many vertebrates (mammals, birds, reptiles and amphibians) have limbs with the same basic bone plan: one upper bone, two lower bones, wrist or ankle bones, and five digits.
- The limbs are used for very different jobs (grasping, flying, swimming, running), but the same plan suggests these animals evolved from a common ancestor.

#### Explained

A human arm, a bat wing, a whale flipper and a horse leg look different, but all contain the humerus, radius and ulna, carpals and digits. If each animal had been designed separately for its job, there is no reason for them to share a plan. The simplest explanation is that they inherited it from a shared ancestor, and natural selection then changed the bones' sizes and shapes to suit each animal's way of life.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the limbs have the same function. They have the same structure but different functions.
- Saying all five digits are always visible. In horses, some digits are reduced, but the basic plan is the same.

### 4.13B Agricultural solutions: fertilisers and biological control (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- A growing human population needs more food. Farmers use fertilisers to increase crop yield and biological control to reduce pests.
- **Fertilisers** add mineral ions (nitrates, phosphates and potassium) to the soil.
- **Biological control** uses a living organism (a predator, parasite or pathogen) to reduce a pest population, instead of chemical pesticides.

#### Explained

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fertilisers | Bigger yields from the same land; replace minerals taken out by harvesting | Run-off into rivers causes eutrophication; cost; can harm soil organisms if overused |
| Biological control | No chemical residues on food; can be long-lasting as the control organism breeds; targets the pest | Slow to act; the control organism may become a pest itself or eat native species; can be hard to predict |

Examples of biological control: ladybirds released in greenhouses to eat aphids; a parasitic wasp that lays eggs in whitefly larvae. A famous failure is the cane toad, released in Australia to eat beetles on sugar cane, which instead spread widely and poisoned native predators.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying biological control "kills all the pests". It reduces the population; predator and prey numbers then usually settle at a lower level.
- Forgetting that fertiliser run-off, not the fertiliser on the crop, causes eutrophication.

### Check questions

1. Put these in order from oldest to youngest: Lucy, Leakey's fossils, Ardi. (1 mark)
2. Describe two changes shown by the fossils of Ardi, Lucy and Leakey's find that support the idea of human evolution. (2 marks)
3. Explain how scientists could estimate the age of a stone tool found in a cliff with layers of rock and volcanic ash. (3 marks)
4. Explain why stone tools from 40 000 years ago are evidence that brain size had increased compared with 2.6 million years ago. (2 marks)
5. A bat's wing and a human arm have the same bones. Explain how this is evidence for evolution. (2 marks)
6. A farmer has aphids on a greenhouse crop. Evaluate using ladybirds instead of a chemical pesticide. (4 marks)

### Mark scheme

1. Ardi, Lucy, Leakey's fossils (1).
2. Any two: brain size increases; more upright walking/fully bipedal; legs get longer/arms shorter; less adapted for climbing/big toe no longer grasping (2).
3. Tools in deeper layers are older/layers build up over time (1); find which layer the tool is in (1); date volcanic ash layers above/below by radiometric dating to give a range of ages (1).
4. Later tools are more complex/finer/more varied (1); making them needs more planning, skill or intelligence, linked to a bigger brain (1).
5. Same structure/pentadactyl plan used for different functions (1); suggests they share a common ancestor from which both evolved (1).
6. For ladybirds: no chemical residues on the crop; ladybirds keep breeding so control lasts; only target insects they eat. Against: slower; may not remove all aphids; ladybirds may leave. Judgement (4 marks: 1 for each valid point up to 3, 1 for a justified conclusion).

## Named infections and virus life cycles (Edexcel gap file)

Pathogens in general, malaria and HIV are taught in [AQA 8461 4.3.1](../../aqa/gcse/biology-8461/4.3-infection-and-response.md). Edexcel names a different set of example diseases; this file covers the ones AQA does not.

#### Board differences

- Edexcel's named diseases: cholera, tuberculosis, Chalara ash dieback, malaria, HIV, and (Biology only) stomach ulcers caused by *Helicobacter* and Ebola. AQA uses measles, TMV, salmonella, gonorrhoea and rose black spot instead.
- Edexcel's named STIs are chlamydia and HIV.

- In 5.5 and 5.6, *Helicobacter* and Ebola are Biology only; the rest are also in Combined Science.

### 5.5 Some common infections

#### What you need to know

| Disease | Pathogen type | Main effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cholera | bacterium (*Vibrio cholerae*) | severe watery diarrhoea, causing dehydration |
| Tuberculosis (TB) | bacterium (*Mycobacterium tuberculosis*) | damages lung tissue; coughing, sometimes coughing blood |
| Chalara ash dieback | fungus | ash trees lose leaves and get lesions (dead patches) on the bark |
| Malaria | protist (*Plasmodium*) | damages red blood cells and the liver; recurring fevers |
| HIV | virus | destroys a type of white blood cell (lymphocytes), leading to AIDS |
| Stomach ulcers (Biology only) | bacterium (*Helicobacter pylori*) | open sores in the stomach lining |
| Ebola (Biology only) | virus | haemorrhagic fever: fever with internal and external bleeding |

#### Explained

The key skill is linking each disease to its pathogen type, because treatment depends on it. Antibiotics work on bacterial diseases (cholera, TB, stomach ulcers). They do nothing for viral diseases (HIV, Ebola), which are managed with antiviral drugs or supportive care. AIDS is not a separate pathogen: it is the late stage of HIV infection, when the immune system is so weak that other infections take hold.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling Chalara a bacterium or a virus. It is a fungus.
- Saying antibiotics treat HIV or Ebola.
- Saying ulcers are caused only by stress or spicy food. Most are caused by *Helicobacter*.

### 5.6 How these pathogens spread and how spread is reduced

#### What you need to know

| Disease | How it spreads | Ways to reduce or prevent spread |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cholera | water contaminated with infected faeces | clean, treated drinking water; proper sewage disposal; boil water |
| TB | airborne droplets from coughs and sneezes | isolate infected people; good ventilation; BCG vaccination; finish the full course of antibiotics |
| Chalara ash dieback | airborne fungal spores carried by wind; also moved on infected young trees | remove and burn infected trees; ban imports of ash; replant with resistant varieties |
| Malaria | animal vector: female *Anopheles* mosquitoes | mosquito nets; insect repellent; drain standing water; insecticides; antimalarial drugs |
| Stomach ulcers (Biology only) | oral transmission: contaminated food or water, or saliva | clean water; good hygiene; antibiotics to kill the bacteria |
| Ebola (Biology only) | contact with body fluids (blood, vomit) of an infected person or body | isolate patients; protective clothing for health workers; safe burial; contact tracing |

#### Explained

Each control method breaks the chain of transmission at a specific point. For a waterborne disease you cut off the route (clean water). For a vector-borne disease you stop the vector reaching people or breeding. For an airborne disease you reduce contact with droplets or make people immune.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying malaria is spread by all mosquitoes, or that the mosquito is the pathogen. The mosquito is the vector; *Plasmodium* is the pathogen.
- Giving "wash your hands" for every disease. Match the method to the route.

### 5.7B The life cycle of a virus: lytic and lysogenic pathways (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Viruses can only reproduce inside a host cell.
- **Lytic pathway**:
  1. The virus attaches to the host cell and injects its genetic material.
  2. The host cell uses the viral genetic material to make new viral proteins and copies of the viral genetic material.
  3. New viruses are assembled.
  4. The cell bursts (lyses), releasing many new viruses that infect other cells.
- **Lysogenic pathway**:
  1. The viral genetic material is inserted into the host's DNA.
  2. It is copied each time the cell divides, so all daughter cells carry it. The virus is dormant and no new viruses are made.
  3. Later, a trigger (such as a chemical or UV light) makes the viral genetic material leave the host DNA and the virus enters the lytic pathway.

#### Explained

The lytic pathway is fast and destructive: lots of new viruses and a dead cell. The lysogenic pathway is a hiding phase: the virus can stay in the cells for a long time with no symptoms. This is why some infections return; for example, the virus that causes cold sores stays dormant in nerve cells and becomes active when the person is run down.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying viruses divide by themselves. They need the host cell's machinery.
- Saying the lysogenic pathway makes new viruses. It does not, until it switches to lytic.

### 5.8 Sexually transmitted infections: chlamydia and HIV

#### What you need to know

- STIs are spread by sexual contact, through the exchange of body fluids.
- **Chlamydia** is caused by a bacterium. It often has no symptoms but can cause infertility. It can be treated with antibiotics.
- **HIV** is a virus spread by sexual contact and also by sharing needles, from mother to baby at birth or in breast milk, and through blood. It cannot be cured, but antiretroviral drugs slow it down.
- Spread of both can be reduced by: using condoms; screening and testing, then treating people and informing partners; for HIV, not sharing needles (needle exchange schemes) and screening blood for transfusions.

#### Explained

Because chlamydia often has no symptoms, people can pass it on without knowing. Screening (especially of young adults) finds infections so they can be treated with antibiotics. Condoms are a physical barrier that stops body fluids passing between partners.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the contraceptive pill protects against STIs. Only barrier methods such as condoms reduce the risk.
- Saying chlamydia is a virus. It is a bacterium, so antibiotics work.

2. Explain why providing clean water reduces cases of cholera but does not reduce cases of TB. (3 marks)
3. Suggest two ways to stop the spread of ash dieback into a new forest. (2 marks)
4. Describe the lytic pathway of a virus. (4 marks)
5. Explain why a person infected by a virus in the lysogenic pathway may have no symptoms for a long time. (2 marks)
6. Explain why screening young adults for chlamydia helps reduce its spread. (2 marks)
7. Give two ways the spread of HIV among people who inject drugs can be reduced. (2 marks)

### Mark scheme

1. (a) Bacterium (1). (b) Fungus (1). (c) Protist (1).
2. Cholera is spread through contaminated water (1); clean water removes the route of transmission (1); TB is airborne/spread in droplets, so water is not its route (1).

## Brain scans, eye defects and hormones (Edexcel gap file)

The brain, the eye, hormones, IVF and diabetes are taught in [AQA 8461 4.5](../../aqa/gcse/biology-8461/4.5-homeostasis-and-response.md). This file adds what Edexcel names and AQA does not: CT and PET scans, cataracts and colour blindness, the TRH–TSH–thyroxine loop, clomifene, and BMI and waist:hip ratio.

#### Board differences

- 2.11B to 2.17B are Biology only. 2.11B and 2.12B are higher tier.
- 7.3 and 7.8 are higher tier, and are in both Biology and Combined Science.

### 2.11B Investigating the brain with CT and PET scans (Biology only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- The brain is hard to study because it is enclosed by the skull, and removing or damaging brain tissue could harm the person.
- **CT scans** (computerised tomography) use X-rays from many angles to build a detailed image of the brain's structure. They show damaged areas, bleeds and tumours.
- **PET scans** (positron emission tomography) use a radioactive tracer, usually a form of glucose, injected into the blood. Active brain areas use more glucose, so they show up more strongly. PET shows how the brain is working, not just its structure.

#### Explained

CT is like a set of very detailed X-ray "slices" put together by a computer. It is good for finding where a stroke or injury is. PET adds information about activity: for example, if a person reads, the language areas light up. Comparing PET scans of healthy people with those of patients can show areas that are less active in conditions such as Alzheimer's disease.

Drawbacks: both use ionising radiation (X-rays or a radioactive tracer), so they are not used more than needed. PET is expensive and needs a tracer made nearby because it has a short half-life.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying CT shows brain activity. CT shows structure; PET shows activity.
- Saying PET uses X-rays. It detects radiation from a tracer inside the body.

### 2.12B Limits of treating damage and disease in the nervous system (Biology only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Damage to the brain and spinal cord is hard to treat, for example spinal injuries and brain tumours.
- Reasons:
  - Nerve cells in the brain and spinal cord do not repair or regrow well.
  - The brain is protected by the skull, and surgery risks damaging healthy tissue nearby.
  - Many drugs cannot pass from the blood into the brain.
  - It is hard to know exactly which area controls which function, so treatment of one area may affect others.

#### Explained

A tumour deep in the brain may sit among areas that control speech or movement. Removing it could cause new damage. A cut spinal cord means signals cannot pass below the injury, and the neurones do not reconnect, which is why such injuries often cause permanent paralysis. Stem cell research aims to replace damaged neurones but is not yet a routine treatment.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying nerve cells "can't be operated on". Surgery is possible but risky; the main problem is that damaged neurones do not regrow.

### 2.16B Defects of the eye (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- **Short-sightedness (myopia)**: distant objects are blurred. The eyeball is too long or the lens too strong, so light focuses in front of the retina.
- **Long-sightedness (hyperopia)**: near objects are blurred. The eyeball is too short or the lens too weak, so light would focus behind the retina.
- **Cataracts**: the lens becomes cloudy, so less light gets through and vision is blurred or dim, with colours faded.
- **Colour blindness**: some of the cone cells in the retina do not work properly, so some colours (often red and green) cannot be told apart. It is usually inherited (sex-linked).

#### Explained

Myopia and hyperopia are focusing problems. Cataracts are a clarity problem: the lens is not see-through. Colour blindness is a detection problem in the retina, not the lens. Cataracts are common in older people.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying colour-blind people see only in black and white. Most cannot tell certain colours apart.
- Saying cataracts are a film over the eye. The cloudiness is in the lens itself.

### 2.17B Correcting eye defects (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Short-sightedness: a concave (diverging) lens in glasses or contact lenses; or laser surgery to reshape (flatten) the cornea.
- Long-sightedness: a convex (converging) lens; or laser surgery.
- Cataracts: surgery to remove the cloudy lens and replace it with an artificial plastic lens.
- Colour blindness cannot be cured.

#### Explained

| Defect | Correction | How it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short sight | concave lens | spreads the light out a little so it focuses further back, on the retina |
| Long sight | convex lens | bends light in more so it focuses sooner, on the retina |
| Cataract | replacement lens | a clear lens lets light through again |

#### Common mistakes

- Mixing up the lenses. Short sight needs concave (diverging); long sight needs convex (converging).

### 7.3 Thyroxine and negative feedback (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Thyroxine, made by the thyroid gland, controls the basal metabolic rate (how fast the body uses energy at rest).
- The level is controlled by negative feedback:
  1. A low level of thyroxine in the blood stimulates the hypothalamus to release TRH (thyrotropin-releasing hormone).
  2. TRH makes the pituitary gland release TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone).
  3. TSH makes the thyroid produce thyroxine.
  4. When thyroxine is back to normal, it inhibits the release of TRH and TSH, so less thyroxine is made.

#### Explained

Negative feedback means a change triggers a response that reverses it. Here the product (thyroxine) switches off its own production when there is enough. If thyroxine falls, the brake comes off and more TRH and TSH are released.

Hypothalamus → TRH → pituitary → TSH → thyroid → thyroxine, with thyroxine feeding back to stop TRH and TSH.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the pituitary makes TRH. The hypothalamus makes TRH; the pituitary makes TSH.
- Saying high thyroxine stimulates more TSH. High thyroxine inhibits TSH.

### 7.8 Clomifene therapy (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Clomifene is a drug used to treat infertility in women who do not ovulate regularly.
- It stimulates the release of more FSH and LH from the pituitary gland, which stimulates eggs to mature and be released.
- It is a form of Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART), like IVF. IVF itself is in the AQA note 4.5.3.6.

#### Explained

Normally, oestrogen feeds back on the brain to limit FSH and LH. Clomifene blocks the effect of oestrogen in the brain, so the brain responds as if oestrogen is low and releases more FSH and LH. More FSH makes follicles develop; the LH surge triggers ovulation. The couple then try to conceive naturally, timed to ovulation.

Compared with IVF, clomifene is cheaper and less invasive, but it only helps if the cause of infertility is a lack of ovulation, and it can cause multiple pregnancies.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying clomifene contains FSH. It causes the woman's own pituitary to release more FSH and LH.

### 7.17 Body mass, BMI, waist:hip ratio and type 2 diabetes

#### What you need to know

- **BMI** (body mass index) = mass (kg) ÷ (height (m))².
- Adult BMI bands (approximate): below 18.5 underweight; 18.5 to 24.9 healthy; 25 to 29.9 overweight; 30 and above obese.
- **Waist:hip ratio** = waist circumference ÷ hip circumference. A high ratio (above about 0.9 for men and 0.85 for women) means fat stored around the abdomen.
- There is a positive correlation between obesity (especially abdominal fat) and type 2 diabetes. Correlation alone does not prove that one causes the other.

#### Explained

BMI does not tell muscle from fat, so a muscular athlete can have a high BMI and be healthy. Waist:hip ratio shows where the fat is stored. Fat around the abdomen is linked more strongly to type 2 diabetes than fat elsewhere, so the two measures together give a better picture.

#### Worked example

A person has a mass of 90 kg and a height of 1.75 m. Waist 104 cm, hips 108 cm.

- BMI = 90 ÷ 1.75² = 90 ÷ 3.0625 = 29.4 (overweight).
- Waist:hip = 104 ÷ 108 = 0.96 (high, so higher risk).

#### Common mistakes

- Squaring the mass or forgetting to square the height.
- Using height in cm. Convert to metres first.
- Saying the graph proves obesity causes diabetes. A correlation shows a link; other evidence is needed to show cause.

2. Give two reasons why it is difficult to treat a tumour deep inside the brain. (2 marks)
3. Describe the difference between short-sightedness and a cataract, and how each is corrected. (4 marks)
4. Describe how negative feedback returns a low thyroxine level to normal. (4 marks)
5. Explain how clomifene can help a woman who does not ovulate to become pregnant. (3 marks)
6. A man has a mass of 102 kg and height 1.80 m. Calculate his BMI and state his BMI category. (3 marks)
7. Explain why a doctor might measure a person's waist:hip ratio as well as their BMI. (2 marks)

### Mark scheme

2. Any two: risk of damaging nearby healthy tissue; skull limits access; damaged neurones do not regrow; drugs may not reach the brain from the blood (2).

6. 1.80² = 3.24 (1); 102 ÷ 3.24 = 31.5 (1); obese (1).

## Diffusion, respiration rate and the heart (Edexcel gap file)

Diffusion and the factors that affect it are in [AQA 8461 4.1.3.1](../../aqa/gcse/biology-8461/4.1-cell-biology.md); respiration is in [AQA 8461 4.4.2](../../aqa/gcse/biology-8461/4.4-bioenergetics.md); the heart is in [AQA 8461 4.2.2.2](../../aqa/gcse/biology-8461/4.2-organisation.md). This file adds Fick's law, the respirometer and cardiac output.

#### Board differences

- AQA has no respirometer practical and no cardiac output equation. Both are in Edexcel Combined Science and Biology.
- Fick's law (8.5B) is Biology only.

### 8.5B Fick's law (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Rate of diffusion ∝ (surface area × concentration difference) ÷ thickness of membrane.
- "∝" means "is proportional to". Doubling surface area or concentration difference doubles the rate; doubling the thickness halves it.

#### Explained

Fick's law puts the three factors in one relationship. It explains why exchange surfaces such as alveoli have a huge surface area, a steep concentration gradient (kept up by ventilation and blood flow) and a very thin barrier (one cell thick).

#### Worked example

A surface has an area of 6 units, a concentration difference of 4 units and a thickness of 2 units. Disease doubles the thickness and halves the area. By what factor does the rate change?

- Before: (6 × 4) ÷ 2 = 12.
- After: (3 × 4) ÷ 4 = 3.
- The rate falls to 3 ÷ 12 = one quarter of what it was.

#### Common mistakes

- Putting thickness on the top. A thicker membrane slows diffusion.
- Treating ∝ as "=". Fick's law compares rates; you usually find a factor or a relative rate.

### 8.11 Core practical: the rate of respiration in living organisms

#### What you need to know

- A simple respirometer measures how fast small organisms (such as woodlice, maggots or germinating seeds) take in oxygen.
- Soda lime (or potassium hydroxide) absorbs the carbon dioxide given out, so any change in gas volume is due to oxygen being used.
- As oxygen is used, the pressure in the tube falls and a drop of coloured liquid in a capillary tube moves towards the organisms.
- The distance moved in a set time is a measure of the rate of respiration. A control tube with glass beads instead of organisms checks that nothing else is moving the liquid.

#### Explained

Typical method to investigate temperature:

1. Put a known mass of organisms in a boiling tube above a layer of soda lime, separated by gauze so they do not touch it.
2. Seal the tube with a bung connected to a capillary tube with a small drop of coloured liquid.
3. Set up a control tube with the same mass of glass beads.
4. Stand both tubes in a water bath at the chosen temperature and leave them for 5 minutes to adjust.
5. Mark the liquid's position, then measure how far it moves in, say, 5 minutes.
6. Repeat at different water bath temperatures.

The volume of oxygen used can be found from the distance moved: volume = πr² × distance, where r is the radius of the capillary bore.

#### Worked example

The drop moves 24 mm in 4 minutes. The capillary radius is 0.5 mm. Find the rate of oxygen uptake.

- Volume = π × 0.5² × 24 = 18.8 mm³.
- Rate = 18.8 ÷ 4 = 4.7 mm³/min.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the liquid moves because carbon dioxide is produced. The CO₂ is absorbed; the liquid moves because oxygen is used.
- Forgetting to let the tubes reach the water bath temperature before timing.
- Leaving out the control.

#### Practical

- Hazards: soda lime is corrosive; wear eye protection and do not let organisms touch it. Handle animals with care and return them to their habitat.
- Keep the mass of organisms, time and capillary tube the same for each temperature.

### 8.12 Heart rate, stroke volume and cardiac output

#### What you need to know

- **Cardiac output** = stroke volume × heart rate.
- Heart rate is the number of beats per minute. Stroke volume is the volume of blood pumped out of the left ventricle in one beat (cm³ or dm³).
- Cardiac output is the volume of blood pumped per minute (cm³/min or dm³/min).
- During exercise, both heart rate and stroke volume rise, so cardiac output rises, delivering more oxygen and glucose to muscles.

#### Explained

Heart rate can be measured by counting the pulse for 15 seconds and multiplying by 4. Trained athletes have a larger, stronger heart, so a bigger stroke volume; they can supply the same cardiac output at rest with a lower heart rate.

#### Worked example

At rest, a student's heart rate is 70 beats per minute and stroke volume is 75 cm³.

- Cardiac output = 75 × 70 = 5 250 cm³/min = 5.25 dm³/min.

During exercise, cardiac output is 18 000 cm³/min and stroke volume is 120 cm³. Heart rate = 18 000 ÷ 120 = 150 beats per minute.

#### Common mistakes

- Mixing units: stroke volume in cm³ gives cardiac output in cm³/min.
- Rearranging wrongly. Heart rate = cardiac output ÷ stroke volume.

### Check questions

1. Use Fick's law to explain why emphysema, which thickens alveolar walls and reduces their surface area, makes a person breathless. (3 marks)
2. In a respirometer, explain why soda lime is used. (2 marks)
3. A student finds the drop of liquid moves further in 5 minutes at 25 °C than at 15 °C. Explain this result. (2 marks)
4. Suggest why a tube containing glass beads is set up alongside the tube with organisms. (1 mark)
5. A person's stroke volume is 70 cm³ and their cardiac output is 4.9 dm³/min. Calculate their heart rate. (2 marks)
6. Explain why cardiac output increases during exercise. (2 marks)

### Mark scheme

1. Rate of diffusion ∝ area × concentration difference ÷ thickness (1); thicker walls and smaller area both reduce the rate of oxygen diffusion into blood (1); so less oxygen reaches cells for respiration (1).
2. Absorbs carbon dioxide produced (1); so the change in volume/movement of liquid is due only to oxygen used (1).
3. Higher temperature increases enzyme activity/rate of respiration (1); more oxygen used per minute so the liquid moves further (1).
4. Control: to show that movement is caused by the organisms and not by changes in temperature/pressure (1).
5. 4.9 dm³ = 4 900 cm³ (1); 4 900 ÷ 70 = 70 beats per minute (1).
6. Muscles respire faster and need more oxygen and glucose/need CO₂ removed (1); heart rate and stroke volume increase so more blood is pumped per minute (1).

## Ecosystems and the nitrogen cycle (Edexcel gap file)

Communities, adaptations, the carbon and water cycles, decomposition and biodiversity are in [AQA 8461 4.7](../../aqa/gcse/biology-8461/4.7-ecology.md). This file adds the Edexcel content AQA does not teach.

#### Board differences

- The nitrogen cycle, parasitism and mutualism, and fish farming are not in AQA GCSE Biology but are in Edexcel Combined Science and Biology.
- Indicator species, food preservation and decay-rate calculations (9.16B, 9.17B, 9.19B) and plants in extreme environments (6.14B) are Biology only. 9.16B is higher tier.

### 6.14B Plants in extreme environments (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Plants in very dry, hot, cold or salty places have adaptations that reduce water loss and let them survive.
- Leaf size and shape, the cuticle and the stomata are the main features.

#### Explained

| Feature | Adaptation in a dry habitat (e.g. cactus, marram grass) | Why it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leaf size and shape | small leaves or spines; leaves that roll up | smaller surface area, so less water evaporates; rolled leaves trap moist air |
| Cuticle | thick and waxy | waterproof layer cuts evaporation from the leaf surface |
| Stomata | fewer stomata; sunk in pits; surrounded by hairs; open at night | still, humid air around the stomata slows diffusion of water vapour out |
| Stems and roots | thick stems that store water; deep or very wide shallow roots | store water; reach water deep down or catch rain quickly |

In cold places (such as mountains) plants are often small and low-growing to avoid wind, which also reduces water loss when the ground is frozen and water cannot be taken up.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a cactus has no stomata. It has fewer, and often opens them at night when it is cooler.
- Saying spines are "to stop being eaten" only. They also reduce surface area for water loss.

### 9.4 Parasitism and mutualism

#### What you need to know

- Some organisms depend on other species to survive.
- **Parasitism**: the parasite lives in or on a host and benefits, while the host is harmed. Examples: fleas on mammals, head lice on humans, tapeworms in the gut, mistletoe on trees.
- **Mutualism**: both species benefit. Examples: oxpecker birds eating ticks off buffalo; cleaner fish removing parasites from bigger fish; nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the root nodules of legumes (the bacteria get sugars, the plant gets nitrogen compounds).

#### Explained

The key difference is who gains. A parasite usually does not kill its host quickly, because it depends on the host. In mutualism, each partner provides something the other needs.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling a predator a parasite. A predator kills and eats its prey; a parasite lives on a living host.

### 9.9 Human interactions with ecosystems and biodiversity

#### What you need to know

- **Fish farming**: fish are kept in nets or tanks.
  - Positive: reduces fishing of wild stocks; provides food.
  - Negative: waste and uneaten food pollute water and can cause eutrophication; diseases and parasites spread to wild fish; escaped fish may breed with wild fish; predators are sometimes killed.
- **Non-indigenous species** (brought from elsewhere):
  - Positive: may be a food source or control a pest.
  - Negative: may have no natural predators so spread fast and outcompete native species, or bring new diseases. Examples: grey squirrels in the UK (outcompete red squirrels and spread squirrel pox); signal crayfish.
- **Eutrophication**: fertiliser or sewage gets into water → more nitrates and phosphates → algae grow fast and cover the surface → light cannot reach plants below, which die → bacteria decompose the dead plants and use up oxygen as they respire → fish and other animals die. Biodiversity falls.

#### Explained

All three examples show that a change made for one purpose can ripple through food webs. Positive interactions include conservation schemes and reforestation (in the AQA note 4.7.3.6).

#### Common mistakes

- In eutrophication, saying the algae use up the oxygen. Most oxygen is used by decomposing bacteria.
- Saying fish die because of the fertiliser itself. They die from lack of oxygen.

### 9.15 Nitrates and the nitrogen cycle

#### What you need to know

- Plants need nitrates to make proteins (amino acids) and DNA.
- Nitrogen gas (78% of air) is unreactive, so plants cannot use it directly.
- Bacteria in the nitrogen cycle:
  - **Nitrogen-fixing bacteria** (in soil or in root nodules of legumes such as peas and clover) turn nitrogen gas into ammonium or nitrogen compounds.
  - **Decomposers** break down proteins and urea in dead organisms and waste into ammonia/ammonium.
  - **Nitrifying bacteria** turn ammonium into nitrites, then nitrates.
  - **Denitrifying bacteria** turn nitrates back into nitrogen gas. They thrive in waterlogged soil with little oxygen.
- Lightning can also fix nitrogen into nitrogen oxides that dissolve in rain.
- Farmers increase soil nitrates by adding fertilisers (artificial or manure) and by **crop rotation**: growing a legume crop in some years, then ploughing it in, adds nitrogen compounds to the soil.

#### Explained

Follow one nitrogen atom: nitrogen gas → fixed by bacteria in a clover root → used to make protein in the clover → eaten by a rabbit → the rabbit dies → decomposers make ammonium → nitrifying bacteria make nitrate → absorbed by grass roots by active transport. If the soil becomes waterlogged, denitrifying bacteria may return it to the air as nitrogen gas.

Ploughing and draining soil add oxygen, which helps nitrifying bacteria and slows denitrification.

#### Common mistakes

- Mixing up nitrifying (makes nitrates) and denitrifying (removes nitrates).
- Saying plants absorb nitrogen gas through their leaves.
- Saying crop rotation "puts nutrients back by resting the soil". Legumes add nitrogen compounds through their root nodule bacteria.

### 9.16B Indicator species (Biology only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- An indicator species is an organism whose presence, absence or numbers show how polluted an environment is.
- Polluted water (low oxygen): bloodworms and sludgeworms survive.
- Clean water (high oxygen): freshwater shrimps and stonefly larvae are found.
- Air quality: lichens. Many species of lichen are killed by sulfur dioxide, so many types of lichen means clean air. Blackspot fungus on roses is also killed by sulfur dioxide, so blackspot means clean air.

#### Explained

Indicator species are cheap and show pollution over a long period. But they do not give an exact measurement or show which pollutant is present, and other factors (such as temperature or predators) may also affect them. Instruments such as oxygen meters give exact values at one moment, but are costly. Scientists often use both.

#### Common mistakes

- Thinking blackspot on roses means polluted air. It is the opposite: the fungus cannot survive high sulfur dioxide.

### 9.17B Decomposition and food preservation (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Food decays because microorganisms (bacteria and fungi) feed on it and break it down.
- Microorganisms need a suitable temperature, water and (for most) oxygen. Preservation methods remove one or more of these.

#### Explained

| Method | How it slows decay |
| --- | --- |
| Refrigeration (about 4 °C) | low temperature slows microbial enzymes and growth |
| Freezing | stops growth; water is frozen so not available |
| Cooking, canning or pasteurising | heat kills microorganisms; sealed cans stop new ones entering |
| Drying | removes water |
| Salting or adding sugar | water leaves microbes by osmosis |
| Pickling in vinegar | low pH denatures microbial enzymes |
| Vacuum packing | removes oxygen |

#### Common mistakes

- Saying freezing kills all bacteria. It mostly stops growth; bacteria can grow again when food thaws.

### 9.19B Calculating rate changes in decay (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Rate of decay = change in mass (or other measurement) ÷ time.
- Percentage change = (change ÷ starting value) × 100.

#### Worked example

A 400 g pile of grass cuttings has a mass of 260 g after 28 days.

- Mass lost = 400 − 260 = 140 g.
- Rate of decay = 140 ÷ 28 = 5 g/day.
- Percentage decrease = (140 ÷ 400) × 100 = 35%.

#### Common mistakes

- Dividing by the final mass instead of the starting mass for a percentage change.

### Check questions

1. Explain two ways the leaves of a cactus are adapted to its desert habitat. (4 marks)
2. Explain the difference between parasitism and mutualism, using one example of each. (4 marks)

4. Describe the roles of nitrogen-fixing bacteria and nitrifying bacteria in making nitrates available to plants. (3 marks)
5. Explain why a farmer might grow clover in a field one year and wheat the next. (2 marks)
6. A river has many sludgeworms but no stonefly larvae. What does this suggest? Give one disadvantage of using indicator species. (2 marks)
7. Explain why vacuum packing slows the decay of meat. (2 marks)
8. A compost heap loses mass from 2.4 kg to 1.5 kg in 30 days. Calculate the rate of decay in g/day. (2 marks)

### Mark scheme

1. Any two adaptations, each with explanation: spines/small leaves (1) reduce surface area for water loss (1); thick waxy cuticle (1) reduces evaporation (1); few/sunken stomata (1) reduce water vapour diffusing out (1).

4. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria turn nitrogen gas into ammonium/nitrogen compounds (1); nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium into nitrites (1) then nitrates (1).
5. Clover is a legume with nitrogen-fixing bacteria in its root nodules (1); ploughing it in adds nitrogen compounds/nitrates for the wheat (1).

7. Removes oxygen (1); so aerobic microorganisms cannot respire/grow (1).

## 4.1 Cell biology

Cells are the basic unit of all living things. This topic covers what cells contain, how they divide, and how substances move in and out of them.

### 4.1.1.1 Eukaryotes and prokaryotes

#### What you need to know

- Plant and animal cells are eukaryotic. They have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, and genetic material inside a nucleus.
- Bacterial cells are prokaryotic. They are much smaller than eukaryotic cells. They have a cell membrane, cytoplasm and a cell wall, but their genetic material is not inside a nucleus.
- A prokaryote's DNA is a single loop in the cytoplasm. Many also have plasmids: small rings of DNA.
- You should be able to compare cell sizes using orders of magnitude.

#### Explained

The key difference is the nucleus. A eukaryotic cell keeps its DNA inside a nucleus. A prokaryotic cell has its DNA loose in the cytoplasm.

Sizes: most animal and plant cells are roughly 10 to 100 micrometres (µm) across. Most bacteria are roughly 1 to 10 µm. So a typical eukaryotic cell is about 10 times bigger, or one order of magnitude, than a typical bacterium.

An order of magnitude is a power of 10. If one thing is 10 times bigger than another, it is one order of magnitude bigger. If it is 1000 times bigger, it is three orders of magnitude bigger (10³).

#### Worked example

A plant cell is 50 µm long. A bacterium is 0.5 µm long. How many orders of magnitude bigger is the plant cell?

1. 50 ÷ 0.5 = 100.
2. 100 = 10².

Answer: two orders of magnitude.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying bacteria have no DNA. They do. It is just not inside a nucleus.
- Saying bacteria have no cell wall. They do, but it is not made of cellulose.
- Forgetting plasmids when asked to name structures found only in prokaryotes.

### 4.1.1.2 Animal and plant cells

#### What you need to know

Animal cells and plant cells both contain:

| Structure | Job |
| --- | --- |
| Nucleus | Contains the genetic material that controls the cell's activities |
| Cytoplasm | Gel where most chemical reactions happen, controlled by enzymes |
| Cell membrane | Controls which substances pass in and out |
| Mitochondria | Where aerobic respiration happens, releasing energy |
| Ribosomes | Where proteins are made (protein synthesis) |

Plant cells often also have:

| Structure | Job |
| --- | --- |
| Chloroplasts | Absorb light for photosynthesis; contain chlorophyll |
| Permanent vacuole | Filled with cell sap; keeps the cell firm |
| Cell wall made of cellulose | Strengthens and supports the cell |

Algal cells also have a cellulose cell wall.

- You should be able to explain how the structures in a cell relate to its job, and estimate the sizes of cells and structures.

#### Explained

Not every plant cell has chloroplasts. Root cells are underground, get no light and have none. So "plant cells have chloroplasts" is true for leaf cells, not all plant cells.

Ribosomes and mitochondria are too small to see clearly with a light microscope. You see them in electron micrographs.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying plant cells have chloroplasts instead of mitochondria. Plant cells have both: they respire all the time.
- Saying the cell wall controls what enters the cell. That is the membrane. The wall only supports.
- Saying the nucleus "makes proteins". Proteins are made at the ribosomes.

### 4.1.1.3 Cell specialisation

#### What you need to know

Cells specialise to carry out a particular job. You should be able to explain how the structure of these cells relates to their job:

- Animal: sperm cells, nerve cells, muscle cells.
- Plant: root hair cells, xylem cells, phloem cells.

#### Explained

| Cell | Job | Adaptations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sperm cell | Reach and fertilise an egg | Tail to swim; many mitochondria to release energy for swimming; enzymes in the head to digest a way into the egg; nucleus with half the normal genetic material |
| Nerve cell | Carry electrical impulses | Long axon to carry impulses over a distance; branched endings (dendrites) to connect to many other cells |
| Muscle cell | Contract to cause movement | Protein fibres that slide to shorten the cell; many mitochondria to release energy |
| Root hair cell | Absorb water and mineral ions from soil | Long thin projection gives a large surface area; many mitochondria for active transport of mineral ions; no chloroplasts |
| Xylem cell | Carry water and mineral ions up the plant | Dead cells joined end to end into hollow tubes; walls strengthened with lignin; no end walls, so water flows freely |
| Phloem cell | Carry dissolved sugars around the plant | Sieve tube cells with few internal structures and pores in the end walls (sieve plates) so sap flows through; companion cells next to them supply energy |

An exam answer should link each feature to what it does. "Sperm has mitochondria" scores less than "sperm has many mitochondria to release energy for the tail to swim."

#### Common mistakes

- Listing a feature without saying how it helps the cell's job.
- Saying xylem carries sugar. Xylem carries water and mineral ions; phloem carries sugars.

### 4.1.1.4 Cell differentiation

#### What you need to know

- As an organism develops, cells differentiate to form different types of cells.
- Most types of animal cell differentiate at an early stage.
- Many types of plant cells keep the ability to differentiate throughout life.
- In mature animals, cell division is mainly for repair and replacement.
- As a cell differentiates, it gains new structures that suit its job.

#### Explained

Differentiation means a cell becomes specialised. A cell that has differentiated has switched on the genes it needs for its job and switched others off. In animals this mostly happens in the embryo. In plants, the growing tips (meristems) keep making new cells that can become any type, which is why a cutting can grow into a whole new plant.

#### Common mistakes

- Mixing up differentiation (becoming specialised) with division (making more cells).

### 4.1.1.5 Microscopy

#### What you need to know

- Light microscopes and electron microscopes are used to study cells.
- An electron microscope has much higher magnification and resolving power than a light microscope.
- So an electron microscope can be used to study cells in finer detail, including subcellular structures like ribosomes and the internal structure of mitochondria and chloroplasts.
- You should be able to carry out calculations with magnification, real size and image size, and use standard form and prefixes (milli, micro, nano).

```latex
\text{magnification} = \frac{\text{size of image}}{\text{size of real object}}
```

#### Explained

Magnification is how many times bigger the image is than the real object. Resolution is the smallest distance between two points that can still be seen as separate. A higher resolution shows more detail. A light microscope can magnify an image a lot but it will just become blurry: its resolution is limited by the wavelength of light. Electrons have a much shorter wavelength, so an electron microscope resolves much finer detail.

Units:

| Unit | Symbol | In metres | Conversion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| millimetre | mm | 10⁻³ m | 1 mm = 1000 µm |
| micrometre | µm | 10⁻⁶ m | 1 µm = 1000 nm |
| nanometre | nm | 10⁻⁹ m | |

The development of the electron microscope let scientists see subcellular structures for the first time, which changed our understanding of cells.

#### Worked example

A drawing of a cell is 24 mm long. The real cell is 80 µm long. Calculate the magnification.

1. Convert to the same unit: 24 mm = 24 000 µm.
2. Magnification = 24 000 ÷ 80 = 300.

Answer: ×300.

A mitochondrion is 0.002 mm long. Write this in standard form in metres.

1. 0.002 mm = 2 × 10⁻³ mm.
2. 1 mm = 10⁻³ m, so 2 × 10⁻³ × 10⁻³ = 2 × 10⁻⁶ m.

Answer: 2 × 10⁻⁶ m (which is 2 µm).

#### Common mistakes

- Not converting both sizes to the same unit before dividing.
- Giving magnification with a unit. It has none; write "×300".
- Saying an electron microscope is "clearer". Use the word resolution.

#### Practical

**Required practical: using a light microscope to observe, draw and label plant and animal cells.**

1. Peel a thin layer of onion epidermis and lay it flat on a slide in a drop of water.
2. Add a drop of iodine solution as a stain and lower a coverslip at an angle so no air bubbles are trapped.
3. Place the slide on the stage. Start with the lowest-power objective lens.
4. Use the coarse focus to bring the cells into view, then the fine focus to sharpen them.
5. Switch to a higher-power objective and refocus with the fine focus.
6. Draw what you see in pencil, with clear unshaded lines, labels with ruled lines, and the magnification.

Why each step: a thin layer lets light pass through; the stain makes the nucleus and other parts visible; lowering the coverslip at an angle avoids bubbles that look like cells; starting on low power makes the cells easier to find.

Total magnification = eyepiece lens magnification × objective lens magnification.

### 4.1.1.6 Culturing microorganisms (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Bacteria multiply by simple cell division called binary fission. This can be as often as once every 20 minutes if there are enough nutrients and a suitable temperature.
- Bacteria can be grown in a nutrient broth solution or as colonies on an agar gel plate.
- Uncontaminated cultures are needed to investigate the effect of chemicals such as disinfectants and antibiotics.
- You should be able to describe how to prepare an uncontaminated culture using aseptic technique, and explain why each step is taken.
- You should be able to calculate the number of bacteria in a population after a certain time, and calculate cross-sectional areas of colonies or clear areas using πr².

#### Explained

Aseptic technique and why:

| Step | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Sterilise Petri dishes and agar before use (for example in an autoclave) | Kills any bacteria already present |
| Sterilise the inoculating loop by passing it through a flame | Kills microorganisms on the loop |
| Lift the Petri dish lid only slightly, and work near a Bunsen flame | Stops microorganisms in the air getting in |
| Secure the lid with tape, but do not seal it all the way round | Stops microorganisms getting in; oxygen can still enter, so harmful anaerobic bacteria do not grow |
| Store the dish upside down | Stops condensation dripping onto the agar |
| Incubate at 25 °C at most in school | Lowers the chance of growing pathogens that grow at body temperature |

The number of bacteria doubles with each division. After n divisions, one bacterium becomes 2ⁿ bacteria.

```latex
\text{number of bacteria} = \text{starting number} \times 2^{n}
```

#### Worked example

A bacterium divides every 30 minutes. Starting with 1 bacterium, how many are there after 4 hours? Give your answer in standard form.

1. 4 hours = 240 minutes. 240 ÷ 30 = 8 divisions.
2. 2⁸ = 256.
3. 256 = 2.56 × 10².

Answer: 2.56 × 10² bacteria.

A clear zone around an antibiotic disc has a diameter of 18 mm. Calculate its area.

1. Radius = 18 ÷ 2 = 9 mm.
2. Area = π × 9² = 254.5 mm² (to 1 d.p.).

#### Common mistakes

- Using the diameter instead of the radius in πr².
- Counting the number of divisions wrong: count how many times the generation time fits into the total time.
- Saying the lid is sealed completely. It is taped, not sealed.

#### Practical

**Required practical (Biology only): investigating the effect of antiseptics or antibiotics on bacterial growth using agar plates and measuring zones of inhibition.**

1. Spread a bacterial culture evenly over an agar plate using aseptic technique.
2. Soak paper discs in different antiseptics or antibiotics, plus one disc in sterile water as a control.
3. Place the discs on the agar, tape the lid and incubate at 25 °C for about 48 hours.
4. Measure the diameter of the clear zone around each disc and calculate its area.

The larger the clear zone, the more effective the substance at killing bacteria or stopping their growth. The water disc shows that any clear zone is caused by the substance, not the disc.

### 4.1.2.1 Chromosomes

#### What you need to know

- The nucleus of a cell contains chromosomes made of DNA molecules.
- Each chromosome carries a large number of genes.
- In body cells, chromosomes are normally found in pairs.

#### Explained

A gene is a short section of DNA that codes for a particular protein. One chromosome is one very long DNA molecule, coiled up, carrying hundreds or thousands of genes. Human body cells have 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs: one chromosome of each pair came from the mother, the other from the father.

### 4.1.2.2 Mitosis and the cell cycle

#### What you need to know

Cells divide in a series of stages called the cell cycle:

1. **Growth and DNA replication.** The cell grows. It increases the number of subcellular structures such as ribosomes and mitochondria. The DNA replicates to form two copies of each chromosome.
2. **Mitosis.** One set of chromosomes is pulled to each end of the cell, and the nucleus divides.
3. **Cytokinesis.** The cytoplasm and cell membrane divide to form two identical daughter cells.

- Cell division by mitosis is important for growth and development of multicellular organisms.
- You should be able to recognise and describe situations where mitosis happens.

#### Explained

The two daughter cells are genetically identical to each other and to the parent cell. Mitosis happens when an organism grows, when damaged tissue is repaired (such as a cut healing), and in asexual reproduction.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the DNA is copied during mitosis. It is copied before, in the growth stage.
- Saying mitosis makes four cells. Mitosis makes two identical cells. Meiosis makes four non-identical gametes.
- Saying the daughter cells have half the chromosomes. They have the full number.

### 4.1.2.3 Stem cells

#### What you need to know

- A stem cell is an undifferentiated cell that can give rise to many more cells of the same type, and certain other cells can arise from stem cells by differentiation.
- Stem cells from human embryos can be cloned and made to differentiate into most different types of human cells.
- Stem cells from adult bone marrow can form many types of cells, including blood cells.
- Meristem tissue in plants can differentiate into any type of plant cell, throughout the life of the plant.
- Treatment with stem cells may be able to help conditions such as diabetes and paralysis.
- In therapeutic cloning, an embryo is produced with the same genes as the patient. Stem cells from the embryo are not rejected by the patient's body, so they may be used for medical treatment.
- The use of stem cells has potential risks, such as transfer of viral infection, and some people have ethical or religious objections.
- Stem cells from meristems can be used to produce clones of plants quickly and cheaply.

#### Explained

| Source | What it can become | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Human embryo | Most types of human cell | Most useful for treatment; ethical objections because the embryo is destroyed |
| Adult bone marrow | A limited range, such as blood cells | Fewer ethical issues; already used in bone marrow transplants |
| Plant meristem | Any type of plant cell, all through life | Used to clone plants |

Uses of plant cloning from meristems: protecting rare species from extinction, and producing large numbers of identical crop plants with useful features such as disease resistance.

An "evaluate" question on stem cells needs points for and against, then a judgement.

- For: could replace damaged cells (for example insulin-producing cells in diabetes, nerve cells in paralysis); therapeutic cloning avoids rejection.
- Against: risk of transferring viral infection; ethical and religious objections to using embryos; treatments are still being developed and may not work; possible risk of the cells growing out of control.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying adult stem cells can become any cell type.
- Giving only one side in an evaluate question.

### 4.1.3.1 Diffusion

#### What you need to know

- Substances move into and out of cells across cell membranes by diffusion.
- Diffusion is the spreading out of the particles of any substance in a solution, or particles of a gas, resulting in a net movement from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration.
- Examples: oxygen and carbon dioxide in gas exchange, and the waste product urea diffusing from cells into the blood plasma to be taken to the kidney.
- Factors that affect the rate of diffusion: the difference in concentration (concentration gradient), the temperature, and the surface area of the membrane.
- A single-celled organism has a relatively large surface area to volume ratio, so diffusion across its membrane can meet its needs.
- In multicellular organisms, surfaces and organ systems are specialised for exchanging materials. Exchange surfaces are made effective by: a large surface area; a membrane that is thin, giving a short diffusion path; (in animals) an efficient blood supply; (in animals, for gas exchange) being ventilated.
- You should be able to calculate and compare surface area to volume ratios.

#### Explained

Diffusion is passive: it needs no energy from the cell. It happens because particles move about randomly, so more move from where there are many to where there are few than the other way.

| Factor | Change | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concentration gradient | Steeper | Faster diffusion |
| Temperature | Higher | Faster, because particles have more kinetic energy |
| Surface area | Larger | Faster, because more particles can cross at once |

Examples of exchange surfaces and their adaptations:

| Surface | Adaptations |
| --- | --- |
| Lungs (alveoli) | Huge total surface area; walls one cell thick; rich blood supply; ventilated by breathing |
| Small intestine (villi) | Villi and microvilli give a large surface area; thin lining; rich blood supply |
| Fish gills | Gill filaments and lamellae give a large surface area; thin; rich blood supply; water flows over them |
| Leaves | Flat and thin; stomata let gases in and out; air spaces inside |
| Plant roots | Root hair cells give a large surface area |

#### Worked example

Compare the surface area to volume ratio of a cube with 1 cm sides and a cube with 3 cm sides.

- 1 cm cube: surface area = 6 × 1 × 1 = 6 cm². Volume = 1 cm³. Ratio = 6 : 1.
- 3 cm cube: surface area = 6 × 3 × 3 = 54 cm². Volume = 27 cm³. Ratio = 54 : 27 = 2 : 1.

The smaller cube has the larger ratio. As organisms get bigger, their surface area to volume ratio falls, so they need exchange surfaces and transport systems.

#### Common mistakes

- Leaving out "net" movement in the definition.
- Saying diffusion needs energy.
- Saying "large surface area" without saying it speeds up diffusion.

### 4.1.3.2 Osmosis

#### What you need to know

- Water may move across cell membranes by osmosis.
- Osmosis is the diffusion of water from a dilute solution to a concentrated solution through a partially permeable membrane.

#### Explained

A dilute solution has a lot of water and little solute. A concentrated solution has less water and more solute. Water moves by osmosis from the dilute side to the concentrated side.

What happens to cells:

| Solution outside the cell | Animal cell | Plant cell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| More dilute than the cell | Water moves in; the cell swells and may burst | Water moves in; the cell becomes firm (turgid); the wall stops it bursting |
| Same concentration | No net movement | No net movement |
| More concentrated than the cell | Water moves out; the cell shrinks | Water moves out; the cell becomes floppy, and the membrane can pull away from the wall |

#### Practical

**Required practical: investigating the effect of a range of concentrations of salt or sugar solutions on the mass of plant tissue.**

1. Use a cork borer to cut potato cylinders of the same diameter, and trim them to the same length.
2. Blot each cylinder dry and measure its starting mass.
3. Put each cylinder in a different concentration of sugar solution (for example 0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 mol/dm³) for the same time, at the same temperature.
4. Remove, blot dry gently, and measure the final mass.
5. Calculate the percentage change in mass for each.
6. Plot percentage change in mass against concentration.

```latex
\text{percentage change in mass} = \frac{\text{final mass} - \text{starting mass}}{\text{starting mass}} \times 100
```

Where the line crosses the x-axis (0% change), the solution has the same concentration as the potato cells. Percentage change is used, not change in mass, because the cylinders start at slightly different masses.

Variables: the independent variable is concentration; the dependent variable is the percentage change in mass; control variables include the cylinder size, the potato, the time and the temperature.

#### Worked example

A potato cylinder's mass goes from 4.00 g to 4.36 g. Calculate the percentage change.

1. Change = 4.36 − 4.00 = 0.36 g.
2. 0.36 ÷ 4.00 × 100 = 9%.

Answer: +9%. The solution was more dilute than the potato cells, so water moved into the potato by osmosis.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying water moves from "high concentration to low concentration" without saying of what. Say from a dilute solution to a concentrated solution.
- Saying salt or sugar moves by osmosis. Osmosis is water only.
- Not blotting the cylinders before weighing, which adds surface water to the mass.

### 4.1.3.3 Active transport

#### What you need to know

- Active transport moves substances from a more dilute solution to a more concentrated solution, against a concentration gradient.
- This requires energy from respiration.
- Active transport lets mineral ions be absorbed into plant root hairs from very dilute solutions in the soil. Plants need these ions for healthy growth.
- It also lets sugar molecules be absorbed from lower concentrations in the gut into the blood, which has a higher sugar concentration. Sugar is used for cell respiration.
- You should be able to describe how substances are transported into and out of cells by diffusion, osmosis and active transport, and explain the differences between them.

#### Explained

| | Diffusion | Osmosis | Active transport |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What moves | Any dissolved substance or gas | Water only | Particular substances, such as mineral ions and glucose |
| Direction | High to low concentration | Dilute to concentrated solution | Low to high concentration, against the gradient |
| Energy from respiration? | No | No | Yes |
| Membrane needed? | Not always | Partially permeable membrane | Yes, with carrier proteins |

Cells that do a lot of active transport, such as root hair cells and cells lining the small intestine, have many mitochondria to release the energy needed.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying active transport "uses energy" without saying the energy comes from respiration.
- Saying active transport moves substances "down" a gradient.

## 4.2 Organisation

Cells are organised into tissues, organs and organ systems. This topic covers digestion, the heart and blood, non-communicable disease, and how plants are organised to transport water and sugar.

### 4.2.1 Principles of organisation

#### What you need to know

- Cells are the basic building blocks of all living organisms.
- A tissue is a group of cells with a similar structure and function.
- An organ is an aggregation of tissues performing a specific function.
- Organs are organised into organ systems, which work together to form organisms.

#### Explained

The levels, smallest first: cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism.

Example: muscle cells form muscle tissue; the stomach is an organ made of muscle tissue, glandular tissue (makes digestive juices) and epithelial tissue (covers the inside and outside); the stomach is part of the digestive system; the digestive system is one of the organ systems in a human.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling the stomach a tissue. It is an organ: it contains several tissues.

### 4.2.2.1 The human digestive system

#### What you need to know

- The digestive system is an organ system in which several organs work together to digest and absorb food.
- Enzymes catalyse specific reactions in living organisms because of the shape of their active site.
- You should be able to use the "lock and key theory" as a simple model of enzyme action.
- You should be able to explain the effect of temperature and pH on enzymes.
- Digestive enzymes convert food into small soluble molecules that can be absorbed into the bloodstream.
  - Carbohydrases break down carbohydrates to simple sugars. Amylase is a carbohydrase that breaks down starch.
  - Proteases break down proteins to amino acids.
  - Lipases break down lipids (fats) to glycerol and fatty acids.
- The products of digestion are used to build new carbohydrates, lipids and proteins. Some glucose is used in respiration.
- Bile is made in the liver and stored in the gall bladder. It is alkaline, to neutralise hydrochloric acid from the stomach. It also emulsifies fat to form small droplets, which increases the surface area. Both of these increase the rate of fat breakdown by lipase.
- You should be able to calculate the rate of chemical reactions.

#### Explained

**How enzymes work.** An enzyme is a protein that acts as a biological catalyst: it speeds up a reaction without being used up. Each enzyme has an active site with a particular shape. Only a substrate with the matching shape fits into it, like a key in a lock. The substrate binds, the reaction happens, and the products leave. The enzyme is unchanged and can be used again.

**Temperature.** As temperature rises, molecules move faster and collide more often, so the rate rises. Above the optimum temperature (about 37 °C for human enzymes), the bonds holding the enzyme's shape break. The active site changes shape so the substrate no longer fits. The enzyme is denatured, and the rate falls quickly. Denaturing is permanent.

**pH.** Each enzyme has an optimum pH. Too far either side changes the shape of the active site and denatures the enzyme. Protease in the stomach (pepsin) works best in acid, around pH 2. Enzymes in the small intestine work best in slightly alkaline conditions.

**Where the enzymes are made:**

| Enzyme | Made in | Breaks down | Into |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Amylase (a carbohydrase) | Salivary glands, pancreas, small intestine | Starch | Sugars (maltose, then glucose) |
| Protease | Stomach, pancreas, small intestine | Proteins | Amino acids |
| Lipase | Pancreas, small intestine | Lipids | Glycerol and fatty acids |

**The organs in order:** mouth (chewing; amylase in saliva) → oesophagus → stomach (protease; hydrochloric acid kills bacteria and gives the right pH for stomach protease; muscles churn food) → small intestine (enzymes from the pancreas and the intestine itself finish digestion; villi absorb the products into the blood) → large intestine (water absorbed) → rectum and anus. The liver makes bile and the gall bladder stores it.

```latex
\text{rate of reaction} = \frac{1}{\text{time taken}}
```

#### Worked example

Amylase took 40 seconds to break down all the starch at pH 6 and 120 seconds at pH 4. Calculate the rate at each pH.

- pH 6: 1 ÷ 40 = 0.025 s⁻¹.
- pH 4: 1 ÷ 120 = 0.0083 s⁻¹ (2 s.f.).

The reaction was three times faster at pH 6.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying enzymes are "killed". Enzymes are not alive; they are denatured.
- Saying the substrate is denatured. The enzyme is.
- Saying bile is an enzyme, or that it digests fat. Bile emulsifies fat, which speeds up digestion by lipase.
- Saying lipids break down into "fatty acids" only. Include glycerol.

#### Practical

**Required practical: use qualitative reagents to test for carbohydrates, lipids and proteins.**

| Test for | Reagent | Method | Positive result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Starch | Iodine solution | Add a few drops to the food sample | Orange-brown turns blue-black |
| Sugars (reducing sugars such as glucose) | Benedict's solution | Add to the food solution and heat in a water bath at about 75 °C for 5 minutes | Blue turns green, yellow or brick red, depending on how much sugar |
| Protein | Biuret reagent | Add to the food solution | Blue turns purple (lilac) |
| Lipids | Sudan III stain | Add a few drops to the food solution and shake | A red-stained oily layer forms on top |

Safety: wear eye protection; take care with the hot water bath; Biuret reagent contains an irritant.

**Required practical: investigate the effect of pH on the rate of reaction of amylase.**

1. Put a drop of iodine solution into each well of a spotting tile.
2. Put amylase solution, a pH buffer and starch solution in separate tubes in a water bath at a set temperature (for example 35 °C) so they reach the same temperature.
3. Mix the amylase and buffer with the starch and start a stopwatch.
4. Every 10 seconds, put a drop of the mixture onto a fresh iodine well.
5. When the iodine stays orange-brown, all the starch has been broken down. Record the time.
6. Repeat with buffers of different pH.

The independent variable is pH; the dependent variable is the time taken for starch to disappear; control variables include temperature, volumes and concentrations of amylase and starch.

### 4.2.2.2 The heart and blood vessels

#### What you need to know

- You should know the structure and functioning of the human heart and lungs, including how lungs are adapted for gaseous exchange.
- The heart is an organ that pumps blood around the body in a double circulatory system. The right ventricle pumps blood to the lungs, where gas exchange takes place. The left ventricle pumps blood around the rest of the body.
- The natural resting heart rate is controlled by a group of cells located in the right atrium that act as a pacemaker. Artificial pacemakers are electrical devices used to correct irregularities in the heart rate.
- The body contains three different types of blood vessel: arteries, veins and capillaries. You should be able to explain how their structure relates to their functions.
- You should be able to use simple compound measures such as rate, and carry out rate calculations for blood flow.

#### Explained

**The heart.** Blood flows: body → vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body. Valves stop blood flowing backwards. The left ventricle wall is thicker than the right because it pumps blood all round the body at higher pressure, not just to the lungs next door. The coronary arteries branch off the aorta and supply the heart muscle itself with oxygenated blood.

Double circulation means blood passes through the heart twice in each full circuit: once to the lungs and once to the body. This keeps blood pressure high for delivering oxygen to the body.

**The lungs.** Air passes down the trachea, into two bronchi, then smaller bronchioles, ending in tiny air sacs called alveoli. Oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli into the blood; carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveoli. Alveoli are adapted for gas exchange: a very large total surface area, walls only one cell thick for a short diffusion path, a rich network of capillaries to keep the concentration gradient steep, and ventilation by breathing.

**Blood vessels.**

| Vessel | Job | Structure, and why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Artery | Carries blood away from the heart at high pressure | Thick walls of muscle and elastic fibres to withstand and even out the high pressure; narrow lumen |
| Vein | Carries blood back to the heart at low pressure | Thin walls; large lumen to reduce resistance to flow; valves to stop backflow |
| Capillary | Exchanges substances with body cells | Walls one cell thick, so diffusion is fast; very narrow; huge network so every cell is near one |

```latex
\text{rate of blood flow} = \frac{\text{volume of blood}}{\text{time}}
```

#### Worked example

1500 cm³ of blood flows through an artery in 5 minutes. Calculate the rate of blood flow in cm³ per second.

1. 5 minutes = 300 seconds.
2. 1500 ÷ 300 = 5 cm³/s.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying arteries carry oxygenated blood and veins carry deoxygenated blood. That is usually true, but the definition is about direction: arteries carry blood away from the heart. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood.
- Saying veins have thick walls.
- Saying the left side of the heart pumps to the lungs. The right side does.

### 4.2.2.3 Blood

#### What you need to know

- Blood is a tissue consisting of plasma, in which the red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets are suspended.
- You should know the functions of each of these blood components.
- You should be able to recognise different types of blood cells in a photograph or diagram, and explain how they are adapted to their functions.

#### Explained

| Part | Job | Adaptations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plasma | Pale yellow liquid that carries blood cells, dissolved food (glucose, amino acids), carbon dioxide, urea, hormones and antibodies | Mostly water, so substances dissolve in it |
| Red blood cells | Carry oxygen from the lungs to the body | Contain haemoglobin, which binds oxygen; no nucleus, leaving more room for haemoglobin; biconcave disc shape gives a large surface area for oxygen to diffuse in and out |
| White blood cells | Part of the immune system; defend against pathogens | Have a nucleus; some engulf pathogens, some make antibodies or antitoxins |
| Platelets | Help blood clot at a wound | Small fragments of cells with no nucleus |

In a diagram: red blood cells are many, round, with a pale centre and no nucleus; white blood cells are larger and have a nucleus, often lobed; platelets are tiny fragments.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling platelets cells. They are cell fragments.
- Saying plasma carries oxygen. Red blood cells carry almost all the oxygen.

### 4.2.2.4 Coronary heart disease: a non-communicable disease

#### What you need to know

- In coronary heart disease, layers of fatty material build up inside the coronary arteries, narrowing them. This reduces the flow of blood through the coronary arteries, resulting in a lack of oxygen for the heart muscle.
- Stents are used to keep the coronary arteries open.
- Statins are widely used to reduce blood cholesterol levels, which slows down the rate of fatty material deposit.
- In some people heart valves may become faulty, preventing the valve from opening fully, or the heart valve might develop a leak. Faulty heart valves can be replaced using biological or mechanical valves.
- In the case of heart failure, a donor heart, or heart and lungs, can be transplanted. Artificial hearts are occasionally used to keep patients alive while waiting for a transplant, or to allow the heart to rest as an aid to recovery.
- You should be able to evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of treating cardiovascular diseases by drugs, mechanical devices or transplant.

#### Explained

| Treatment | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stent (a mesh tube inserted into the artery to hold it open) | Quick effect; minor surgery with a fast recovery; lasts a long time | Risk of complications from surgery, infection or blood clots; the artery can narrow again |
| Statins | No surgery; reduce the risk of heart attack and stroke; can raise "good" cholesterol | Must be taken long-term; side effects such as muscle pain or liver problems; take time to work |
| Replacement valve: biological (from a pig or cow, or a human donor) | No need for lifelong anti-clotting drugs | Wears out after some years; may need replacing |
| Replacement valve: mechanical | Lasts a long time | Patient needs drugs to stop blood clots for life; can make a clicking sound |
| Heart transplant | Can save a patient with heart failure | Shortage of donors; risk of rejection, so immunosuppressant drugs for life; major surgery |
| Artificial heart | Keeps a patient alive until a donor is found; less risk of rejection, as it is not living tissue | Temporary; risk of blood clots and infection; patient needs to carry a power source |

#### Common mistakes

- Saying fatty deposits build up in the heart. They build up in the coronary arteries.
- Saying coronary heart disease is communicable.

### 4.2.2.5 Health issues

#### What you need to know

- Health is the state of physical and mental well-being.
- Diseases, both communicable and non-communicable, are major causes of ill health. Other factors including diet, stress and life situations may have a profound effect on both physical and mental health.
- Different types of disease may interact:
  - defects in the immune system mean that an individual is more likely to suffer from infectious diseases
  - viruses living in cells can be the trigger for cancers
  - immune reactions initially caused by a pathogen can trigger allergies such as skin rashes and asthma
  - severe physical ill health can lead to depression and other mental illness.
- You should be able to read and interpret information from graphs, tables and charts, and use a scatter diagram to identify a correlation between two variables. You should understand the principles of sampling as applied to scientific data, including epidemiological data.

#### Explained

A communicable disease can spread from person to person or between animals and people, because it is caused by a pathogen. A non-communicable disease cannot spread, for example coronary heart disease, most cancers and type 2 diabetes.

Epidemiology is the study of patterns of disease in populations. A good study uses a large, random sample so the results represent the whole population.

#### Common mistakes

- Defining health as "not being ill". Health includes mental well-being.

### 4.2.2.6 The effect of lifestyle on some non-communicable diseases

#### What you need to know

- Risk factors are linked to an increased rate of a disease. They can be aspects of a person's lifestyle, or substances in the person's body or environment.
- A causal mechanism has been proven for some risk factors, but not in others.
  - The effects of diet, smoking and exercise on cardiovascular disease.
  - Obesity as a risk factor for type 2 diabetes.
  - The effect of alcohol on the liver and brain function.
  - The effect of smoking on lung disease and lung cancer.
  - The effects of smoking and alcohol on unborn babies.
  - Carcinogens, including ionising radiation, as risk factors in cancer.
- Many diseases are caused by the interaction of a number of factors.
- You should be able to discuss the human and financial cost of these diseases to individuals, local communities, nations and globally, and explain the effect of lifestyle factors on the incidence of non-communicable diseases at local, national and global levels.

#### Explained

| Risk factor | Disease |
| --- | --- |
| Diet high in saturated fat; lack of exercise; smoking | Cardiovascular disease |
| Obesity | Type 2 diabetes |
| Too much alcohol | Liver disease (cirrhosis); damage to brain function |
| Smoking | Lung disease (such as bronchitis and emphysema); lung cancer |
| Smoking and alcohol during pregnancy | Harm to the unborn baby, such as low birth weight |
| Carcinogens, including ionising radiation (such as UV light and X-rays) | Cancer |

A correlation between a risk factor and a disease does not prove the factor causes the disease. A causal mechanism is the explanation of how one leads to the other. For example, scientists showed how chemicals in tobacco smoke damage DNA in lung cells, which proved that smoking causes lung cancer.

Costs: to individuals (pain, shorter life, loss of income); to communities and nations (care costs for the health service, lost working days); globally (a heavy burden in poorer countries where treatment is less available).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a correlation proves a cause.
- Saying a risk factor means you will get the disease. It only increases the chance.

### 4.2.2.7 Cancer

#### What you need to know

- Cancer is the result of changes in cells that lead to uncontrolled growth and division.
- Benign tumours are growths of abnormal cells that are contained in one area, usually within a membrane. They do not invade other parts of the body.
- Malignant tumour cells are cancers. They invade neighbouring tissues and spread to different parts of the body in the blood, where they form secondary tumours.
- Scientists have identified lifestyle risk factors for various types of cancer. There are also genetic risk factors for some cancers.

#### Explained

| | Benign tumour | Malignant tumour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spread | Stays in one place, usually inside a membrane | Invades nearby tissue; spreads through the blood |
| Secondary tumours | No | Yes |
| Is it cancer? | No | Yes |
| Can it be harmful? | Yes, if it presses on an organ such as the brain | Yes |

Lifestyle risk factors include smoking, obesity, UV exposure and some viral infections (such as HPV and cervical cancer). Genetic risk factors include inherited faulty genes, such as BRCA genes linked to breast cancer.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying benign tumours are always harmless.
- Saying cancer is caused by cells dividing too fast. The key is uncontrolled division.

### 4.2.3.1 Plant tissues

#### What you need to know

Plant tissues include:

- epidermal tissues
- palisade mesophyll
- spongy mesophyll
- xylem and phloem
- meristem tissue found at the growing tips of shoots and roots.

The leaf is a plant organ. You should know the structure of the tissues in a leaf, and explain how their structure relates to their function.

#### Explained

| Tissue | Job | Adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Upper epidermis | Covers the leaf | Transparent, so light passes through; covered in a waxy cuticle that reduces water loss |
| Palisade mesophyll | Main site of photosynthesis | Tall cells packed with chloroplasts, near the top of the leaf where light is strongest |
| Spongy mesophyll | Gas exchange | Loosely packed with air spaces, so gases diffuse quickly to and from cells |
| Lower epidermis | Covers the underside | Contains stomata, surrounded by guard cells, for gas exchange |
| Xylem | Carries water and mineral ions | Hollow tubes strengthened by lignin |
| Phloem | Carries dissolved sugars | Sieve tubes with pores in the end walls |
| Meristem | Makes new cells at shoot and root tips | Cells divide by mitosis and can differentiate into any plant cell |

Guard cells open the stomata to let carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis and close them to reduce water loss.

### 4.2.3.2 Plant organ system

#### What you need to know

- The roots, stem and leaves form a plant organ system for transport of substances around the plant.
- Root hair cells, xylem and phloem are adapted to their functions.
- Xylem tissue transports water and mineral ions from the roots to the stems and leaves. It is composed of hollow tubes strengthened by lignin, adapted for the transport of water in the transpiration stream.
- The role of stomata and guard cells is to control gas exchange and water loss.
- Phloem tissue transports dissolved sugars from the leaves to the rest of the plant for immediate use or storage. The movement of food molecules through phloem tissue is called translocation.
- Phloem is composed of tubes of elongated cells. Cell sap can move from one phloem cell to the next through pores in the end walls.
- The rate of transpiration is affected by temperature, humidity, air movement and light intensity.
- You should be able to explain the effect of changing these factors on the rate of transpiration, and understand and use simple compound measures such as the rate of transpiration. You should be able to calculate surface area and mean values, and use them in estimating rates.

#### Explained

**Transpiration** is the loss of water vapour from the leaves, mostly through the stomata. Water evaporates from cells inside the leaf and diffuses out. This pulls more water up the xylem from the roots, in a continuous flow called the transpiration stream. It also carries mineral ions up the plant.

| Factor | Change | Effect on transpiration | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | Higher | Faster | Water molecules have more energy, so evaporation and diffusion are faster |
| Humidity | Higher | Slower | The air already holds more water vapour, so the concentration gradient is smaller |
| Air movement (wind) | More | Faster | Water vapour is blown away from the leaf, keeping the gradient steep |
| Light intensity | Higher | Faster | More stomata open to let in carbon dioxide for photosynthesis, so more water escapes |

**Measuring transpiration.** A potometer measures water uptake by a cut shoot. As the shoot takes up water, an air bubble moves along a capillary tube. The distance moved in a set time gives the rate of water uptake, which is close to the rate of transpiration (a little water is used in photosynthesis).

**Translocation** moves sugars, made by photosynthesis in the leaves, through the phloem to where they are used or stored, such as growing tips and roots. It can move both up and down the plant.

#### Worked example

In a potometer, the bubble moves 30 mm in 5 minutes. Calculate the rate of water uptake in mm per minute.

30 ÷ 5 = 6 mm per minute.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying transpiration happens in the roots. It is loss of water from the leaves.
- Saying phloem carries water up the plant.
- Saying higher humidity speeds up transpiration.

## 4.5 Homeostasis and response

The body keeps its internal conditions steady using nerves and hormones. This topic covers the nervous system, the brain and eye (Biology only), hormones, blood glucose, the kidneys (Biology only), reproduction hormones, contraception, and plant hormones (Biology only).

### 4.5.1 Homeostasis

#### What you need to know

- Homeostasis is the regulation of the internal conditions of a cell or organism to maintain optimum conditions for function, in response to internal and external changes.
- Homeostasis maintains optimal conditions for enzyme action and all cell functions.
- In the human body, these include control of blood glucose concentration, body temperature and water levels.
- These automatic control systems may involve nervous responses or chemical responses.
- All control systems include:
  - cells called receptors, which detect stimuli (changes in the environment)
  - coordination centres (such as the brain, spinal cord and pancreas) that receive and process information from receptors
  - effectors, muscles or glands, which bring about responses that restore optimum levels.

#### Explained

The pattern for every control system:

stimulus → receptor → coordination centre → effector → response

Example: blood glucose rises (stimulus) → cells in the pancreas detect it (receptor and coordination centre) → the pancreas releases insulin → liver and muscle cells take in glucose (effectors) → blood glucose falls (response).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying homeostasis keeps conditions "the same". It keeps them within a narrow optimum range.
- Saying an effector is a nerve. Effectors are muscles or glands.

### 4.5.2.1 Structure and function of the nervous system

#### What you need to know

- The nervous system enables humans to react to their surroundings and to coordinate their behaviour.
- Information from receptors passes along cells (neurones) as electrical impulses to the central nervous system (CNS). The CNS is the brain and spinal cord. The CNS coordinates the response of effectors, which may be muscles contracting or glands secreting hormones.
- stimulus → receptor → coordinator → effector → response
- Reflex actions are automatic and rapid; they do not involve the conscious part of the brain.
- You should be able to explain how the structure of the nervous system is adapted to its functions, explain how the various structures in a reflex arc (including the sensory neurone, synapse, relay neurone and motor neurone) relate to their function, and explain why reflex actions are important.

#### Explained

**Neurones.**

| Neurone | Carries impulses |
| --- | --- |
| Sensory neurone | From the receptor to the CNS |
| Relay neurone | Within the CNS, from sensory to motor neurone |
| Motor neurone | From the CNS to the effector |

**Synapse.** The gap between two neurones. When an impulse reaches the end of a neurone, a chemical is released. It diffuses across the gap and binds to receptors on the next neurone, starting a new impulse there.

**Reflex arc.**

receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone (in the spinal cord) → motor neurone → effector

Example: touching a hot pan. Pain receptors in the skin detect heat; an impulse travels up the sensory neurone to the spinal cord; it crosses a synapse to a relay neurone, then another synapse to a motor neurone; the motor neurone carries the impulse to a muscle in the arm, which contracts and pulls the hand away.

Reflexes are important because they protect the body from harm (burns, bright light, choking) quickly, before the conscious brain has time to decide.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the impulse "jumps" across a synapse. A chemical diffuses across it.
- Saying a reflex goes through the conscious brain.

#### Practical

**Required practical: plan and carry out an investigation into the effect of a factor on human reaction time.**

A common method is the ruler drop test.

1. Person A holds a metre ruler vertically, with the zero mark between person B's open thumb and finger.
2. Person A drops the ruler without warning. Person B catches it as fast as possible.
3. Record the distance on the ruler at the top of B's thumb. A shorter distance means a faster reaction time.
4. Repeat several times and calculate a mean.
5. Change one factor, such as after drinking a caffeinated drink, using the dominant or non-dominant hand, or after practice, and repeat.

Control variables: the same person, the same hand (unless that is the factor), the same starting position, and no warning before the drop. A conversion table changes distance into reaction time.

### 4.5.2.2 The brain (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- The brain controls complex behaviour. It is made of billions of interconnected neurones and has different regions that carry out different functions.
- You should be able to identify the cerebral cortex, cerebellum and medulla on a diagram of the brain, and describe their functions.
- (HT only) Neuroscientists have been able to map the regions of the brain to particular functions by studying patients with brain damage, electrically stimulating different parts of the brain, and using MRI scanning techniques.
- (HT only) The complexity and delicacy of the brain makes investigating and treating brain disorders very difficult.

#### Explained

| Region | Position | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cerebral cortex | The large, folded outer layer | Consciousness, intelligence, memory and language |
| Cerebellum | At the back, underneath the cortex | Coordinates muscle movement and balance |
| Medulla | At the base, joining the spinal cord | Controls unconscious activities such as heartbeat and breathing |

(HT only) Why treating the brain is hard: surgery can damage healthy tissue; drugs often cannot pass from the blood into the brain; we still do not fully understand how many regions work together.

### 4.5.2.3 The eye (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- The eye is a sense organ containing receptors sensitive to light intensity and colour.
- You should be able to relate the structures of the eye to their functions: retina, optic nerve, sclera, cornea, iris, ciliary muscles and suspensory ligaments.
- Accommodation is the process of changing the shape of the lens to focus on near or distant objects.
- Two common defects of the eyes are myopia (short sightedness) and hyperopia (long sightedness), in which rays of light do not focus on the retina. Generally these defects are treated with spectacle lenses, which refract the light rays so that they do focus on the retina.
- New technologies now include hard and soft contact lenses, laser surgery to change the shape of the cornea, and a replacement lens in the eye.
- You should be able to interpret ray diagrams showing these two common defects and how spectacle lenses correct them.

#### Explained

| Structure | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Cornea | Transparent front layer that refracts light into the eye |
| Iris | Coloured ring of muscle that controls the size of the pupil, and so how much light enters |
| Pupil | The hole in the iris that lets light in |
| Lens | Focuses light onto the retina |
| Ciliary muscles | Ring of muscle that changes the shape of the lens |
| Suspensory ligaments | Connect the ciliary muscles to the lens |
| Retina | Contains receptor cells sensitive to light intensity (rods) and colour (cones) |
| Optic nerve | Carries impulses from the retina to the brain |
| Sclera | Tough white outer layer that protects the eye |

**Pupil reflex.** In bright light, the circular muscles of the iris contract and the radial muscles relax, so the pupil gets smaller and less light enters. In dim light, the radial muscles contract and the circular muscles relax, so the pupil gets bigger.

**Accommodation.**

| Looking at | Ciliary muscles | Suspensory ligaments | Lens shape | Light refracted |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A near object | Contract | Slacken | Thicker, more rounded | More |
| A distant object | Relax | Pulled tight | Thinner, flatter | Less |

**Defects.**

| Defect | Can see clearly | Problem | Correction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Myopia (short sight) | Near objects | Light from distant objects focuses in front of the retina, often because the eyeball is too long or the lens too thick | Concave (diverging) lens |
| Hyperopia (long sight) | Distant objects | Light from near objects focuses behind the retina, often because the eyeball is too short or the lens cannot become round enough | Convex (converging) lens |

#### Common mistakes

- Mixing up which way the ciliary muscles and ligaments move. Muscles contract for near, ligaments go slack.
- Saying a concave lens corrects long sight.

### 4.5.2.4 Control of body temperature (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Body temperature is monitored and controlled by the thermoregulatory centre in the brain. The thermoregulatory centre contains receptors sensitive to the temperature of the blood.
- The skin contains temperature receptors and sends nervous impulses to the thermoregulatory centre.
- (HT only) If the body temperature is too high, blood vessels dilate (vasodilation) and sweat is produced from the sweat glands. Both these mechanisms cause a transfer of energy from the skin to the environment.
- (HT only) If the body temperature is too low, blood vessels constrict (vasoconstriction), sweating stops and skeletal muscles contract (shiver).

#### Explained

(HT only)

| Too hot | Too cold |
| --- | --- |
| Blood vessels supplying the skin capillaries dilate, so more blood flows near the surface and more energy is transferred to the surroundings | Blood vessels supplying the skin capillaries constrict, so less blood flows near the surface and less energy is lost |
| Sweat glands release sweat; as it evaporates it transfers energy away from the skin | Sweating stops |
| | Muscles shiver; the extra respiration releases energy that warms the body |

#### Common mistakes

- (HT) Saying the blood vessels themselves move up or down in the skin. They widen or narrow.
- (HT) Saying sweat cools you just by being wet. The energy is transferred when it evaporates.

### 4.5.3.1 Human endocrine system

#### What you need to know

- The endocrine system is composed of glands that secrete chemicals called hormones directly into the bloodstream. The blood carries the hormone to a target organ, where it produces an effect.
- Compared with the nervous system, the effects are slower but act for longer.
- The pituitary gland in the brain is a "master gland" which secretes several hormones into the blood in response to body conditions. These hormones in turn act on other glands to stimulate other hormones to be released to bring about effects.
- You should be able to identify the position of the following on a diagram of the human body: pituitary gland, pancreas, thyroid, adrenal gland, ovary and testes.

#### Explained

| Gland | Position | Hormone | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pituitary | Base of the brain | Several, including FSH, LH, ADH and TSH | Controls other glands |
| Thyroid | Neck | Thyroxine | Controls metabolic rate |
| Adrenal glands | On top of the kidneys | Adrenaline | Prepares the body for "fight or flight" |
| Pancreas | Behind the stomach | Insulin (and glucagon) | Controls blood glucose |
| Ovaries | Lower abdomen (females) | Oestrogen, progesterone | Female sex characteristics; menstrual cycle |
| Testes | Scrotum (males) | Testosterone | Male sex characteristics; sperm production |

| | Nervous system | Endocrine system |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Signal | Electrical impulses along neurones | Hormones in the blood |
| Speed | Very fast | Slower |
| Duration | Short | Longer |
| Target | Precise place | Can be widespread |

### 4.5.3.2 Control of blood glucose concentration

#### What you need to know

- Blood glucose concentration is monitored and controlled by the pancreas.
- If the blood glucose concentration is too high, the pancreas produces the hormone insulin, which causes glucose to move from the blood into the cells. In liver and muscle cells excess glucose is converted to glycogen for storage.
- Type 1 diabetes is a disorder in which the pancreas fails to produce sufficient insulin. It is characterised by uncontrolled high blood glucose levels and is normally treated with insulin injections.
- In Type 2 diabetes the body cells no longer respond to insulin produced by the pancreas. A carbohydrate-controlled diet and an exercise regime are common treatments. Obesity is a risk factor for Type 2 diabetes.
- You should be able to compare Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes and explain how they can be treated.
- (HT only) If the blood glucose concentration is too low, the pancreas produces the hormone glucagon, which causes glycogen to be converted into glucose and released into the blood.
- (HT only) You should be able to explain how glucagon interacts with insulin in a negative feedback cycle to control blood glucose levels.

#### Explained

| | Type 1 | Type 2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Pancreas does not make enough insulin | Body cells stop responding to insulin |
| Usual onset | Often in childhood | Often in later life; linked to obesity |
| Treatment | Insulin injections; monitoring blood glucose; a balanced diet | Carbohydrate-controlled diet; exercise; sometimes drugs or insulin |

(HT only) **Negative feedback.** A change away from normal triggers a response that brings it back.

- Blood glucose too high → pancreas releases insulin → glucose moves into cells; liver and muscles convert it to glycogen → blood glucose falls.
- Blood glucose too low → pancreas releases glucagon → liver converts glycogen back to glucose, released into the blood → blood glucose rises.

#### Common mistakes

- Spelling and mixing up glucose, glycogen and glucagon. Glucose is the sugar; glycogen is the store; glucagon is the hormone (HT).
- Saying insulin turns glucose into glycogen "in the blood". It happens in liver and muscle cells.
- Saying Type 2 diabetics make no insulin.

### 4.5.3.3 Maintaining water and nitrogen balance in the body (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- If body cells lose or gain too much water by osmosis they do not function efficiently.
- Water leaves the body via the lungs during exhalation.
- Water, ions and urea are lost from the skin in sweat.
- There is no control over water, ion or urea loss by the lungs or skin.
- Excess water, ions and urea are removed via the kidneys in the urine.
- (HT only) The digestion of proteins from the diet results in excess amino acids, which need to be excreted safely. In the liver these amino acids are deaminated to form ammonia. Ammonia is toxic, so it is immediately converted to urea for safe excretion.
- The kidneys produce urine by filtration of the blood and selective reabsorption of useful substances such as glucose, some ions and water.
- (HT only) The water level in the body is controlled by the hormone ADH, which acts on the kidney tubules. ADH is released by the pituitary gland when the blood is too concentrated, and it causes more water to be reabsorbed back into the blood from the kidney tubules. This is controlled by negative feedback.
- People who suffer from kidney failure may be treated by organ transplant or by using kidney dialysis.

#### Explained

**How a kidney makes urine.**

1. Filtration: blood is filtered at high pressure. Small molecules (water, glucose, ions and urea) pass into the kidney tubule. Large molecules such as proteins, and blood cells, stay in the blood.
2. Selective reabsorption: all the glucose, the ions the body needs and as much water as the body needs are taken back into the blood.
3. What is left (urea, excess ions and excess water) is urine. It goes to the bladder.

(HT only) **ADH.** Blood too concentrated (for example after sweating) → pituitary releases more ADH → kidney tubules become more permeable → more water reabsorbed → small volume of concentrated urine. Blood too dilute → less ADH → less water reabsorbed → a large volume of dilute urine.

**Kidney failure.**

| | Dialysis | Transplant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How it works | Blood flows past a partially permeable membrane surrounded by dialysis fluid. The fluid has the same concentration of glucose and useful ions as normal blood, so these are not lost, but has no urea, so urea diffuses out | A healthy donor kidney is put into the patient |
| Advantages | Available while waiting for a donor; no rejection | Long-term cure; patient can live a normal life; cheaper over many years |
| Disadvantages | Several sessions a week, each lasting hours; restricted diet; expensive over time; risk of infection and blood clots | Shortage of donors; risk of rejection, so immunosuppressant drugs for life; risks of major surgery |

#### Common mistakes

- Saying glucose is in normal urine. It is all reabsorbed in a healthy person.
- Saying dialysis fluid contains no glucose. It contains the normal amount, so glucose is not lost.

### 4.5.3.4 Hormones in human reproduction

#### What you need to know

- During puberty reproductive hormones cause secondary sex characteristics to develop.
- Oestrogen is the main female reproductive hormone, produced in the ovary. At puberty eggs begin to mature and one is released approximately every 28 days. This is called ovulation.
- Testosterone is the main male reproductive hormone, produced by the testes, and it stimulates sperm production.
- Several hormones are involved in the menstrual cycle of a woman:
  - follicle stimulating hormone (FSH) causes maturation of an egg in the ovary
  - luteinising hormone (LH) stimulates the release of the egg
  - oestrogen and progesterone are involved in maintaining the uterus lining.
- (HT only) You should be able to explain the interactions of FSH, oestrogen, LH and progesterone in the control of the menstrual cycle, and extract and interpret data from graphs showing hormone levels during the cycle.

#### Explained

The menstrual cycle lasts about 28 days.

1. Days 1 to 4: menstruation. The uterus lining breaks down and leaves the body.
2. Days 4 to 14: the lining builds up again.
3. Around day 14: ovulation; an egg is released.
4. Days 14 to 28: the lining is kept thick, ready for a fertilised egg. If no egg implants, the lining breaks down and the cycle starts again.

| Hormone | Made in | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FSH | Pituitary gland | Causes an egg to mature in the ovary; stimulates the ovaries to make oestrogen |
| Oestrogen | Ovaries | Builds up the uterus lining; (HT) inhibits FSH and stimulates the release of LH |
| LH | Pituitary gland | Triggers ovulation |
| Progesterone | Ovaries (from the empty follicle) | Maintains the uterus lining; (HT) inhibits FSH and LH |

(HT only) On a graph: FSH peaks early, oestrogen rises in the first half, LH peaks sharply just before ovulation, and progesterone is high in the second half and then falls, which causes the lining to break down.

### 4.5.3.5 Contraception

#### What you need to know

Fertility can be controlled by a variety of hormonal and non-hormonal methods of contraception. These include:

- oral contraceptives that contain hormones to inhibit FSH production so that no eggs mature
- injection, implant or skin patch of slow release progesterone to inhibit the maturation and release of eggs for a number of months or years
- barrier methods such as condoms and diaphragms, which prevent the sperm reaching an egg
- intrauterine devices, which prevent the implantation of an embryo or release a hormone
- spermicidal agents, which kill or disable sperm
- abstaining from intercourse when an egg may be in the oviduct
- surgical methods of male and female sterilisation.

You should be able to evaluate the different hormonal and non-hormonal methods of contraception.

#### Explained

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oral contraceptive (the pill) | Very effective if taken correctly | Must be taken every day; side effects such as mood changes; no protection from STIs |
| Implant, injection or patch | Lasts months or years; nothing to remember daily | Side effects; no protection from STIs |
| Condom | Protects against STIs; no hormones | Can split; must be used every time |
| Diaphragm | No hormones | Less effective; must be used with spermicide; no STI protection |
| Intrauterine device | Lasts years | Must be fitted by a doctor; small risk of infection |
| Spermicide | Easy to use | Not very effective on its own |
| Abstaining at fertile times | No hormones or devices | Unreliable, because the fertile time is hard to predict |
| Sterilisation (vasectomy or tying the oviducts) | Almost 100% effective | Very difficult to reverse; surgery |

An evaluation should weigh effectiveness, side effects, ease of use and STI protection.

### 4.5.3.6 The use of hormones to treat infertility (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- This includes giving FSH and LH in a "fertility drug" to a woman. She may then become pregnant in the normal way.
- In vitro fertilisation (IVF) treatment:
  - IVF involves giving a mother FSH and LH to stimulate the maturation of several eggs
  - the eggs are collected from the mother and fertilised by sperm from the father in the laboratory
  - the fertilised eggs develop into embryos
  - at the stage when they are tiny balls of cells, one or two embryos are inserted into the mother's uterus (womb).
- Although fertility treatment gives a woman the chance to have a baby of her own, it is very emotionally and physically stressful, the success rates are not high, and it can lead to multiple births, which are a risk to both the babies and the mother.
- You should be able to explain the use of hormones in modern reproductive technologies to treat infertility, and understand the social and ethical issues.

#### Explained

Ethical issues include: what to do with unused embryos, which are often destroyed; the cost and whether health services should pay; and the possibility of screening embryos for genetic disorders.

### 4.5.3.7 Negative feedback (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Adrenaline is produced by the adrenal glands in times of fear or stress. It increases the heart rate and boosts the delivery of oxygen and glucose to the brain and muscles, preparing the body for "flight or fight".
- Thyroxine from the thyroid gland stimulates the basal metabolic rate. It plays an important role in growth and development.
- Thyroxine levels are controlled by negative feedback.
- You should be able to interpret and explain simple diagrams of negative feedback control.

#### Explained

**Thyroxine.** Thyroxine level falls → the pituitary releases more TSH (thyroid stimulating hormone) → the thyroid releases more thyroxine → the level rises back to normal → the pituitary releases less TSH. The response always reverses the change: that is what "negative" feedback means.

Adrenaline is not controlled this way in the exam: it is released in response to a stimulus (fear or stress), then falls when the stress passes.

### 4.5.4.1 Control and coordination in plants (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Plants produce hormones to coordinate and control growth, and responses to light (phototropism) and gravity (gravitropism or geotropism).
- Unequal distributions of auxin cause unequal growth rates in plant roots and shoots.
- (HT only) Gibberellins are important in initiating seed germination.
- (HT only) Ethene controls cell division and ripening of fruits.

#### Explained

| Situation | Where auxin builds up | Effect | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shoot lit from one side | Shaded side | Auxin speeds up cell elongation in shoots, so the shaded side grows faster | Shoot bends towards the light (positive phototropism) |
| Shoot lying on its side | Lower side | Lower side grows faster | Shoot bends upwards (negative gravitropism) |
| Root lying on its side | Lower side | Auxin slows growth in roots, so the upper side grows faster | Root bends downwards (positive gravitropism) |

Why it helps: shoots growing towards light can photosynthesise more; roots growing down anchor the plant and reach water and mineral ions.

#### Practical

**Required practical (Biology only): investigate the effect of light or gravity on the growth of newly germinated seedlings.**

1. Germinate seeds (such as cress or mustard) on damp cotton wool in three Petri dishes.
2. Put one dish in light from all sides, one in the dark, and one with light from one side only (in a box with a slit).
3. After several days, measure the length and record the direction of growth of each seedling.

To test gravity, fix seedlings in dishes turned at different angles in the dark, so light is not a factor, and observe how roots and shoots bend. Keep the temperature, water and seed type the same.

### 4.5.4.2 Use of plant hormones (Biology only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

Plant growth hormones are used in agriculture and horticulture.

- Auxins are used as weed killers, as rooting powders, and for promoting growth in tissue culture.
- Ethene is used in the food industry to control ripening of fruit during storage and transport.
- Gibberellins can be used to end seed dormancy, promote flowering, and increase fruit size.

#### Explained

| Hormone | Use | How |

| Auxin | Rooting powder | Cuttings dipped in it grow roots quickly, so clones can be produced |

| Ethene | Controlling ripening | Fruit is picked unripe and transported; ethene is added to ripen it just before sale |
| Gibberellins | Ending dormancy, flowering, bigger fruit | Seeds germinate when wanted; plants flower at the right time; seedless grapes grow larger |

## 4.6 Inheritance, variation and evolution

Genes are passed from parents to offspring, and populations change over time. This topic covers reproduction and meiosis, DNA, genetic crosses, inherited disorders, variation, evolution, breeding and genetic engineering, the history of these ideas, fossils, resistant bacteria and classification.

### 4.6.1.1 Sexual and asexual reproduction

#### What you need to know

- Sexual reproduction involves the joining (fusion) of male and female gametes:
  - sperm and egg cells in animals
  - pollen and egg cells in flowering plants.
- In sexual reproduction there is mixing of genetic information, which leads to variety in the offspring. The formation of gametes involves meiosis.
- Asexual reproduction involves only one parent and no fusion of gametes. There is no mixing of genetic information. This leads to genetically identical offspring (clones). Only mitosis is involved.

#### Explained

| | Sexual | Asexual |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Parents | Two | One |
| Gametes | Yes, they fuse | No |
| Cell division | Meiosis to make gametes | Mitosis only |
| Offspring | Genetically different | Genetically identical (clones) |

Examples of asexual reproduction: bacteria by binary fission; strawberry plants by runners; daffodils by bulbs; yeast by budding.

### 4.6.1.2 Meiosis

#### What you need to know

- Cells in reproductive organs divide by meiosis to form gametes.
- When a cell divides to form gametes:
  - copies of the genetic information are made
  - the cell divides twice to form four gametes, each with a single set of chromosomes
  - all gametes are genetically different from each other.
- Gametes join at fertilisation to restore the normal number of chromosomes. The new cell divides by mitosis. The number of cells increases. As the embryo develops, cells differentiate.
- You should be able to explain how meiosis halves the number of chromosomes in gametes, and how fertilisation restores the full number.

#### Explained

A human body cell has 46 chromosomes (23 pairs). Meiosis makes gametes with 23 chromosomes: one from each pair. At fertilisation, a sperm (23) joins an egg (23) to make a zygote with 46. If gametes had the full 46, the number would double every generation.

| | Mitosis | Meiosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where | Body cells | Reproductive organs (testes, ovaries; anthers and ovaries in plants) |
| Divisions | One | Two |
| Cells made | Two | Four |
| Chromosomes in each new cell | Full set (46 in humans) | Half (23 in humans) |
| Genetically | Identical to the parent | All different |
| Used for | Growth, repair, asexual reproduction | Making gametes |

#### Common mistakes

- Saying meiosis makes two cells.
- Saying gametes have 23 pairs. They have 23 single chromosomes.

### 4.6.1.3 Advantages and disadvantages of sexual and asexual reproduction (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

Advantages of sexual reproduction:

- it produces variation in the offspring
- if the environment changes, variation gives a survival advantage by natural selection
- natural selection can be speeded up by humans in selective breeding to increase food production.

Advantages of asexual reproduction:

- only one parent is needed
- it is more time and energy efficient, as a mate is not needed
- it is faster than sexual reproduction
- many identical offspring can be produced when conditions are favourable.

Some organisms reproduce by both methods depending on the circumstances:

- malarial parasites reproduce asexually in the human host, but sexually in the mosquito
- many fungi reproduce asexually by spores but also reproduce sexually to give variation
- many plants produce seeds sexually, but also reproduce asexually by runners such as strawberry plants, or bulb division such as daffodils.

#### Explained

The disadvantages are the reverse of the other method's advantages. Sexual reproduction is slower and needs a mate. Asexual reproduction produces no variation, so a single disease or a change in the environment could wipe out the whole population.

### 4.6.1.4 DNA and the genome

#### What you need to know

- The genetic material in the nucleus of a cell is composed of a chemical called DNA. DNA is a polymer made up of two strands forming a double helix. The DNA is contained in structures called chromosomes.
- A gene is a small section of DNA on a chromosome. Each gene codes for a particular sequence of amino acids, to make a specific protein.
- The genome of an organism is the entire genetic material of that organism. The whole human genome has now been studied, and this will have great importance for medicine in the future.
- You should be able to discuss the importance of understanding the human genome, limited to:
  - the search for genes linked to different types of disease
  - understanding and treatment of inherited disorders
  - use in tracing human migrations from the past.

#### Explained

From biggest to smallest: nucleus → chromosomes → DNA → genes → the sequence of bases that codes for amino acids.

Why the genome matters:

- Doctors can find genes that increase the risk of diseases such as some cancers, and offer screening or lifestyle advice.
- Inherited disorders can be understood, diagnosed early, and new treatments developed.
- Comparing the genomes of people around the world shows how populations moved and spread in the past.

### 4.6.1.5 DNA structure (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- DNA is a polymer made from four different nucleotides. Each nucleotide consists of a common sugar and a phosphate group with one of four different bases attached to the sugar.
- DNA contains four bases: A, C, G and T.
- A sequence of three bases is the code for a particular amino acid. The order of bases controls the order in which amino acids are assembled to produce a particular protein.
- The long strands of DNA consist of alternating sugar and phosphate sections. Attached to each sugar is one of the four bases.
- The DNA polymer is made up of repeating nucleotide units.
- (HT only) You should be able to recall a simple description of protein synthesis, explain simply how the structure of DNA affects the protein made, and describe how genetic variants may influence phenotype in coding DNA (by altering the activity of a protein) and in non-coding DNA (by altering how genes are expressed).
- (HT only) In the complementary strands, a C is always linked to a G on the opposite strand, and a T to an A.

#### Explained

**Structure.** Each strand is a chain of nucleotides: the sugar and phosphate form the backbone; the bases stick out. The two strands are joined by their bases, which pair up.

(HT only) **Complementary pairs:** A with T, C with G. If one strand reads ATCG, the other reads TAGC.

(HT only) **Protein synthesis.**

1. In the nucleus, the gene's base sequence is copied into a template molecule.
2. The template leaves the nucleus and goes to a ribosome in the cytoplasm.
3. Carrier molecules bring specific amino acids to the ribosome, matching each triplet of bases.
4. The ribosome joins the amino acids in the correct order to make a protein chain.
5. When complete, the chain folds into a unique shape, which lets it do its job, for example as an enzyme, a hormone, or a structural protein such as collagen.

(HT only) **Mutations** are changes in the base sequence. They happen continuously. Most do not change the protein, or change it only slightly so its appearance or function is unaffected. A few change the shape a lot, so an enzyme's active site no longer binds its substrate, or a structural protein loses its strength.

(HT only) **Non-coding DNA.** Not all DNA codes for proteins. Some non-coding parts switch genes on and off. A variant there can change whether a gene is expressed, which can change the phenotype.

#### Common mistakes

- (HT) Pairing A with G or C with T.
- Saying each base codes for an amino acid. It takes three bases.

### 4.6.1.6 Genetic inheritance

#### What you need to know

- You should be able to explain the terms: gamete, chromosome, gene, allele, dominant, recessive, homozygous, heterozygous, genotype, phenotype.
- Some characteristics are controlled by a single gene, such as fur colour in mice and red-green colour blindness in humans. Each gene may have different forms called alleles.
- The alleles present, or genotype, operate at a molecular level to develop characteristics that can be expressed as a phenotype.
- A dominant allele is always expressed, even if only one copy is present. A recessive allele is only expressed if two copies are present (therefore no dominant allele is present).
- If the two alleles present are the same, the individual is homozygous for that trait; if the alleles are different, they are heterozygous.
- Most characteristics are a result of multiple genes interacting, rather than a single gene.
- You should be able to understand the concept of probability in predicting the results of a single gene cross, use direct proportion and simple ratios to express the outcome of a genetic cross, complete a Punnett square diagram and extract and interpret information from genetic crosses and family trees.
- (HT only) You should be able to construct a genetic cross by Punnett square diagram and use it to make predictions using the theory of probability.

#### Explained

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Gamete | Sex cell (sperm or egg) with one set of chromosomes |
| Chromosome | A long DNA molecule carrying many genes |
| Gene | A section of DNA that codes for one protein |
| Allele | A different version of the same gene |
| Dominant | Expressed even when only one copy is present (capital letter, e.g. B) |
| Recessive | Expressed only when two copies are present (small letter, e.g. b) |
| Homozygous | Two identical alleles (BB or bb) |
| Heterozygous | Two different alleles (Bb) |
| Genotype | The alleles an organism has (e.g. Bb) |
| Phenotype | The characteristic that shows (e.g. brown fur) |

#### Worked example

In mice, brown fur (B) is dominant to white fur (b). Two heterozygous mice are crossed. What proportion of the offspring will be white?

Parents: Bb × Bb. Gametes: B or b from each parent.

| | B | b |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **B** | BB | Bb |
| **b** | Bb | bb |

- BB: brown; Bb: brown; Bb: brown; bb: white.
- Ratio 3 brown : 1 white.
- Probability of white = 1 in 4 = 0.25 = 25%.

A ratio is a prediction of probability. With only a few offspring, the actual numbers may not match the ratio.

#### Common mistakes

- Using different letters for the two alleles (B and w). Use the same letter in capital and small.
- Saying a 3 : 1 ratio means exactly 3 of every 4 offspring will be brown.

### 4.6.1.7 Inherited disorders

#### What you need to know

- Some disorders are inherited. These disorders are caused by the inheritance of certain alleles.
  - Polydactyly (having extra fingers or toes) is caused by a dominant allele.
  - Cystic fibrosis (a disorder of cell membranes) is caused by a recessive allele.
- You should make informed judgements about the economic, social and ethical issues concerning embryo screening and gene therapy.

#### Explained

Polydactyly: one dominant allele (D) is enough. A person who is Dd or DD has the disorder.

Cystic fibrosis: the person must inherit two recessive alleles (ff). A person who is Ff is a carrier: they do not have the disorder but can pass on the allele. Two carriers have a 1 in 4 chance of each child having cystic fibrosis. It causes thick, sticky mucus in the lungs and digestive system.

Embryo screening: in IVF, a cell is taken from each embryo and tested; only embryos without the disorder are implanted.

| For screening | Against screening |
| --- | --- |
| Reduces suffering | Embryos with the disorder are destroyed |
| Saves the cost of lifelong treatment | Could lead to choosing embryos for other traits ("designer babies") |
| | Expensive, and not every test is accurate |
| | Some people feel it devalues people living with the disorder |

Gene therapy aims to put a working allele into a patient's cells. It is still being developed.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a carrier has the disorder.

### 4.6.1.8 Sex determination

#### What you need to know

- Ordinary human body cells contain 23 pairs of chromosomes.
- 22 pairs control characteristics only, but one of the pairs carries the genes that determine sex.
  - In females the sex chromosomes are the same (XX).
  - In males the chromosomes are different (XY).
- You should be able to carry out a genetic cross to show sex inheritance.

#### Explained

| | X (from mother) | X (from mother) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **X (from father)** | XX | XX |
| **Y (from father)** | XY | XY |

Every egg carries an X. Half of sperm carry X, half carry Y. So each child has a 50% chance of being female (XX) and 50% of being male (XY). The father's sperm determines the sex.

### 4.6.2.1 Variation

#### What you need to know

- Differences in the characteristics of individuals in a population is called variation and may be due to differences in:
  - the genes they have inherited (genetic causes)
  - the conditions in which they have developed (environmental causes)
  - a combination of genes and the environment.
- There is usually extensive genetic variation within a population of a species.
- All variants arise from mutations, and most have no effect on the phenotype; some influence phenotype and very few determine phenotype.
- Mutations occur continuously. Very rarely a mutation will lead to a new phenotype. If the new phenotype is suited to an environmental change, it can lead to a relatively rapid change in the species.

#### Explained

| Cause | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Genetic only | Blood group; eye colour |
| Environmental only | Scars; language spoken |
| Both | Height (genes set the range; diet affects where in it you end up); weight; plant size (genes, plus light and nutrients) |

### 4.6.2.2 Evolution

#### What you need to know

- Evolution is a change in the inherited characteristics of a population over time through a process of natural selection, which may result in the formation of a new species.
- The theory of evolution by natural selection states that all species of living things have evolved from simple life forms that first developed more than three billion years ago.
- You should be able to describe evolution as a change in the inherited characteristics of a population over time, through a process of natural selection, which may result in the formation of new species.
- If two populations of one species become so different in phenotype that they can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring, they have formed two new species.

#### Explained

Natural selection in steps:

1. There is variation in a population, caused by mutations.
2. Some individuals have characteristics that suit the environment better.
3. These individuals are more likely to survive and reproduce.
4. They pass the alleles for these characteristics on to their offspring.
5. Over many generations, the advantageous allele becomes more common in the population.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying an organism "adapts" or changes in its lifetime to suit the environment. Individuals do not evolve; populations do, over generations.
- Saying an animal "needs" a feature so it develops one. The variation must already be there.

### 4.6.2.3 Selective breeding

#### What you need to know

- Selective breeding (artificial selection) is the process by which humans breed plants and animals for particular genetic characteristics. Humans have been doing this for thousands of years, since they first bred food crops from wild plants and domesticated animals.
- Selective breeding involves choosing parents with the desired characteristic from a mixed population. They are bred together. From the offspring, those with the desired characteristic are bred together. This continues over many generations until all the offspring show the desired characteristic.
- The characteristic can be chosen for usefulness or appearance:
  - disease resistance in food crops
  - animals which produce more meat or milk
  - domestic dogs with a gentle nature
  - large or unusual flowers.
- Selective breeding can lead to "inbreeding", where some breeds are particularly prone to disease or inherited defects.

#### Explained

Inbreeding reduces variation: the population ends up with very similar alleles. That makes it more likely that harmful recessive alleles are inherited in pairs, and less likely that any individual can survive a new disease.

### 4.6.2.4 Genetic engineering

#### What you need to know

- Genetic engineering is a process which involves modifying the genome of an organism by introducing a gene from another organism to give a desired characteristic.
- Plant crops have been genetically engineered to be resistant to diseases or to produce bigger, better fruits.
- Bacterial cells have been genetically engineered to produce useful substances such as human insulin to treat diabetes.
- You should be able to explain the potential benefits and risks of genetic engineering in agriculture and in medicine, and that some people have objections.
- In genetic engineering, genes from the chromosomes of humans and other organisms can be "cut out" and transferred to cells of other organisms.
- Crops that have had their genes modified in this way are called genetically modified (GM) crops. GM crops include ones that were genetically engineered to be resistant to insect attack or to herbicides. GM crops generally show increased yields.
- Concerns about GM crops include the effect on populations of wild flowers and insects. Some people feel the effects of eating GM crops on human health have not been fully explored.
- Modern medical research is exploring the possibility of genetic modification to overcome some inherited disorders.
- (HT only) You should be able to describe the main steps in the process of genetic engineering:
  - enzymes are used to isolate the required gene
  - this gene is inserted into a vector, usually a bacterial plasmid or a virus
  - the vector is used to insert the gene into the required cells
  - genes are transferred to the cells of animals, plants or microorganisms at an early stage in their development so that they develop with desired characteristics.

#### Explained

| Benefits | Risks and concerns |
| --- | --- |
| Higher crop yields to feed more people | Genes could spread to wild plants (for example, herbicide resistance into weeds) |
| Crops resistant to insects need less pesticide | Fewer insects that feed on the crop, which affects food chains |
| Crops can be made to contain extra nutrients | Long-term effects of eating GM food are not fully known |
| Bacteria make cheap, pure human insulin | Ethical objections to changing an organism's genome |
| May treat inherited disorders in future | |

(HT only) In making human insulin: the insulin gene is cut out of human DNA with an enzyme; the same enzyme cuts open a bacterial plasmid; the gene is inserted into the plasmid, which is put back into a bacterium; the bacterium multiplies in a fermenter, and every copy makes insulin.

### 4.6.2.5 Cloning (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- **Tissue culture:** using small groups of cells from part of a plant to grow identical new plants. This is important for preserving rare plant species, and commercially in nurseries.
- **Cuttings:** an older, simple method used by gardeners to produce many identical new plants from a parent plant.
- **Embryo transplants:** splitting apart cells from a developing animal embryo before they become specialised, then transplanting the identical embryos into host mothers.
- **Adult cell cloning:**
  - the nucleus is removed from an unfertilised egg cell
  - the nucleus from an adult body cell, such as a skin cell, is inserted into the egg cell
  - an electric shock stimulates the egg cell to divide to form an embryo
  - these embryo cells contain the same genetic information as the adult skin cell
  - when the embryo has developed into a ball of cells, it is inserted into the womb of an adult female to continue its development.
- You should be able to explain the potential benefits and risks of cloning in agriculture and in medicine, and that some people have ethical objections.

#### Explained

| Benefits | Risks and concerns |
| --- | --- |
| Many identical copies of a plant or animal with useful features | No variation, so a single disease could wipe out all the clones |
| Saves rare species | Cloned animals may have health problems and shorter lives |
| Could produce animals that make medicines | Ethical objections, including fears it could lead to human cloning |

Dolly the sheep (1996) was the first mammal cloned from an adult body cell.

#### Common mistakes

- In adult cell cloning, saying the nucleus comes from the egg. The egg's nucleus is removed; the nucleus comes from the adult body cell, so the clone is identical to the animal that gave the body cell.

### 4.6.3.1 Theory of evolution (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Charles Darwin, as a result of observations on a round-the-world expedition, backed by years of experimentation and discussion and linked to developing knowledge of geology and fossils, proposed the theory of evolution by natural selection:
  - individual organisms within a particular species show a wide range of variation for a characteristic
  - individuals with characteristics most suited to the environment are more likely to survive to breed successfully
  - the characteristics that have enabled these individuals to survive are then passed on to the next generation.
- Darwin published his ideas in "On the Origin of Species" (1859). There was much controversy surrounding these revolutionary new ideas. The theory of evolution by natural selection was only gradually accepted because:
  - the theory challenged the idea that God made all the animals and plants that live on Earth
  - there was insufficient evidence at the time the theory was published to convince many scientists
  - the mechanism of inheritance and variation was not known until 50 years after the theory was published.
- Other theories, including that of Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, are based mainly on the idea that changes that occur in an organism during its lifetime can be inherited. We now know that in the vast majority of cases this type of inheritance cannot occur.

#### Explained

Lamarck's idea, using a giraffe: a giraffe stretches its neck to reach leaves; its neck gets longer; it passes the longer neck to its offspring. This is wrong, because changes to the body during life do not change the genes in the gametes.

Darwin's idea, using a giraffe: giraffes vary in neck length; those with longer necks reach more food, survive and breed; their offspring inherit the alleles for longer necks; over generations, the population has longer necks.

### 4.6.3.2 Speciation (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Alfred Russel Wallace independently proposed the theory of evolution by natural selection. He published joint writings with Darwin in 1858, which prompted Darwin to publish "On the Origin of Species" the following year.
- Wallace worked worldwide gathering evidence for evolutionary theory. He is best known for his work on warning colouration in some species of animals and his theory of speciation.
- Alfred Wallace did much pioneering work on speciation, but more evidence over time has led to our current understanding of the theory of speciation.
- You should be able to describe the steps that give rise to new species.

#### Explained

Steps in speciation:

1. Two populations of one species become isolated, for example by a river, a mountain range or the sea.
2. Each population has genetic variation.
3. The environments on each side differ, so different characteristics are selected in each population by natural selection.
4. Over many generations, the populations become so different that they can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
5. They are now two separate species.

### 4.6.3.3 The understanding of genetics (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- In the mid-19th century Gregor Mendel carried out breeding experiments on plants. One of his observations was that the inheritance of each characteristic is determined by "units" that are passed on to descendants unchanged.
- In the late 19th century the behaviour of chromosomes during cell division was observed.
- In the early 20th century it was observed that chromosomes and Mendel's "units" behaved in similar ways. This led to the idea that the "units", now called genes, were located on chromosomes.
- In the mid-20th century the structure of DNA was determined and the mechanism of gene function worked out.
- This scientific work by many scientists led to the gene theory being developed.
- You should be able to explain why the importance of Mendel's discovery was not recognised until after his death.

#### Explained

Mendel bred pea plants and found that characteristics such as tall or short were inherited in fixed ratios, such as 3 : 1. His work was not recognised in his lifetime because:

- chromosomes, genes and DNA had not yet been discovered, so there was no mechanism to explain his "units"
- he published in a little-read journal
- he was a monk, not part of the main scientific community, and many scientists did not understand the mathematics he used.

### 4.6.3.4 Evidence for evolution

#### What you need to know

- The theory of evolution by natural selection is now widely accepted.
- Evidence for Darwin's theory is now available, as it has been shown that characteristics are passed on to offspring in genes. There is further evidence in the fossil record and the knowledge of how resistance to antibiotics evolves in bacteria.

### 4.6.3.5 Fossils

#### What you need to know

- Fossils are the "remains" of organisms from millions of years ago, which are found in rocks.
- Fossils may be formed:
  - from parts of organisms that have not decayed because one or more of the conditions needed for decay are absent
  - when parts of the organism are replaced by minerals as they decay
  - as preserved traces of organisms, such as footprints, burrows and rootlet traces.
- Many early forms of life were soft-bodied, which means they have left few traces behind. What traces there were have been mainly destroyed by geological activity. This is why scientists cannot be certain about how life began on Earth.
- We can learn from fossils how much or how little different organisms have changed as life developed on Earth.

#### Explained

Conditions where decay does not happen include very cold places (ice), places with no oxygen (peat bogs) and too acidic or too dry conditions. Hard parts such as bones, teeth and shells are most often fossilised by mineral replacement.

The fossil record is incomplete: soft-bodied organisms rarely fossilise, fossils are destroyed by movements of the Earth, and many have not been found yet.

### 4.6.3.6 Extinction

#### What you need to know

- Extinctions occur when there are no remaining individuals of a species still alive.
- You should be able to describe factors which may contribute to the extinction of a species.

#### Explained

| Factor | Example |
| --- | --- |
| A new predator | Animals introduced to islands that hunt native birds |
| A new disease | A disease that kills most of a population |
| A more successful competitor | A species that takes the food or space |
| A change in the environment | Climate change; habitat destruction |
| A single catastrophic event | An asteroid impact or huge volcanic eruption |

### 4.6.3.7 Resistant bacteria

#### What you need to know

- Bacteria can evolve rapidly because they reproduce at a fast rate.
- Mutations of bacterial pathogens produce new strains. Some strains might be resistant to antibiotics, and so are not killed. They survive and reproduce, so the population of the resistant strain rises. The resistant strain will then spread because people are not immune to it and there is no effective treatment.
- MRSA is resistant to antibiotics.
- To reduce the rate of development of antibiotic-resistant strains:
  - doctors should not prescribe antibiotics inappropriately, such as for non-serious or viral infections
  - patients should complete their course of antibiotics so all bacteria are killed and none survive to mutate and form resistant strains
  - the agricultural use of antibiotics should be restricted.
- The development of new antibiotics is costly and slow. It is unlikely to keep up with the emergence of new resistant strains.

#### Explained

This is natural selection happening quickly enough to watch. The antibiotic is the selection pressure: it kills the bacteria that are not resistant and leaves the resistant ones to multiply.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the antibiotic causes the mutation. The mutation happens by chance; the antibiotic selects the resistant bacteria.
- Saying people become resistant to antibiotics. The bacteria become resistant.

### 4.6.4 Classification of living organisms

#### What you need to know

- Traditionally living things have been classified into groups depending on their structure and characteristics, in a system first developed by Carl Linnaeus.
- Linnaeus classified living things into kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species. Organisms are named by the binomial system of genus and species.
- You should be able to use information given to show understanding of the Linnaean system.
- As evidence of internal structures became more developed due to improvements in microscopes, and the understanding of biochemical processes progressed, new models of classification were proposed.
- Due to evidence available from chemical analysis, there is now a "three-domain system" developed by Carl Woese. In this system organisms are divided into:
  - archaea (primitive bacteria usually living in extreme environments)
  - bacteria (true bacteria)
  - eukaryota (which includes protists, fungi, plants and animals).
- Evolutionary trees are a method used by scientists to show how they believe organisms are related. They use current classification data for living organisms and fossil data for extinct organisms.

#### Explained

A way to remember the order: Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species ("King Philip Came Over For Good Soup").

Binomial name: the genus (capital letter) then the species (small letter), in italics when typed. Humans are *Homo sapiens*. The binomial system gives every species one name used by scientists everywhere.

On an evolutionary tree, two species that share a more recent branch point have a more recent common ancestor, so they are more closely related.

## 4.7 Ecology

Living things depend on each other and on their surroundings. This topic covers communities, adaptations, food chains, sampling, the carbon and water cycles, decay, human impact on biodiversity, and (Biology only) trophic levels, biomass and food production.

### 4.7.1.1 Communities

#### What you need to know

- An ecosystem is the interaction of a community of living organisms (biotic) with the non-living (abiotic) parts of their environment.
- To survive and reproduce, organisms require a supply of materials from their surroundings and from the other living organisms there.
- Plants in a community or habitat often compete with each other for light and space, and for water and mineral ions from the soil.
- Animals often compete with each other for food, mates and territory.
- Within a community each species depends on other species for food, shelter, pollination, seed dispersal and so on. If one species is removed it can affect the whole community. This is called interdependence.
- A stable community is one where all the species and environmental factors are in balance, so that population sizes remain fairly constant.
- You should be able to extract and interpret information from charts, graphs and tables relating to the interaction of organisms within a community.

#### Explained

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Habitat | The place where an organism lives |
| Population | All the organisms of one species in a habitat |
| Community | All the populations of different species in a habitat |
| Ecosystem | A community plus its non-living environment |

Example of interdependence: if bees die out, plants they pollinate produce fewer seeds; animals that eat those seeds or fruits have less food; their predators have less food too.

Tropical rainforests and ancient oak woodland are examples of stable communities.

### 4.7.1.2 Abiotic factors

#### What you need to know

Abiotic (non-living) factors which can affect a community are:

- light intensity
- temperature
- moisture levels
- soil pH and mineral content
- wind intensity and direction
- carbon dioxide levels for plants
- oxygen levels for aquatic animals.

You should be able to explain how a change in an abiotic factor would affect a given community.

#### Explained

Example: if light intensity falls (for example, under a thick tree canopy), plants photosynthesise less and grow less; animals that eat them have less food; so populations through the food chain may fall.

### 4.7.1.3 Biotic factors

#### What you need to know

Biotic (living) factors which can affect a community are:

- availability of food
- new predators arriving
- new pathogens
- one species outcompeting another so the numbers are no longer sufficient to breed.

You should be able to explain how a change in a biotic factor might affect a given community.

#### Explained

Example: grey squirrels were introduced to the UK. They outcompete red squirrels for food and carry a virus that kills red squirrels. Red squirrel numbers have fallen sharply.

### 4.7.1.4 Adaptations

#### What you need to know

- Organisms have features (adaptations) that enable them to survive in the conditions in which they normally live. These adaptations may be structural, behavioural or functional.
- Some organisms live in environments that are very extreme, such as at high temperature, pressure or salt concentration. These organisms are called extremophiles. Bacteria living in deep sea vents are extremophiles.
- You should be able to explain how organisms are adapted to live in their natural environment, given appropriate information.

#### Explained

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structural | Features of the body | Thick fur and small ears in Arctic animals (less energy lost); large ears in desert animals (more energy lost); camouflage; cacti with spines instead of leaves (less water lost) |
| Behavioural | The way an organism acts | Migrating to warmer places; hunting at night in hot deserts; huddling together for warmth |
| Functional | Processes inside the body | Desert animals producing very concentrated urine; hibernating animals lowering their metabolism |

An exam answer should name the feature and explain how it helps survival in that environment.

### 4.7.2.1 Levels of organisation

#### What you need to know

- Feeding relationships within a community can be represented by food chains. All food chains begin with a producer which synthesises molecules. This is usually a green plant or alga which makes glucose by photosynthesis.
- A range of experimental methods using transects and quadrats are used by ecologists to determine the distribution and abundance of species in an ecosystem.
- In relation to abundance of organisms, you should be able to understand the terms mean, mode and median, calculate arithmetic means, and plot and draw appropriate graphs selecting appropriate scales for the axes.
- Producers are eaten by primary consumers, which in turn may be eaten by secondary consumers and then tertiary consumers.
- Consumers that kill and eat other animals are predators, and those eaten are prey. In a stable community the numbers of predators and prey rise and fall in cycles.
- You should be able to interpret graphs used to model these cycles.

#### Explained

Example food chain: grass → rabbit → fox. Grass is the producer; the rabbit is the primary consumer (and prey); the fox is the secondary consumer (and predator). The arrows show the direction the biomass moves: from what is eaten to what eats it.

**Predator–prey cycles.** When prey numbers rise, predators have more food, so predator numbers rise. More predators eat more prey, so prey numbers fall. With less food, predator numbers then fall. The predator peak comes a little after the prey peak.

#### Worked example

A student counts daisies in 5 quadrats, each 1 m²: 4, 7, 3, 7, 9. The field is 2000 m². Estimate the daisy population.

1. Mean per quadrat = (4 + 7 + 3 + 7 + 9) ÷ 5 = 30 ÷ 5 = 6 per m².
2. Estimated population = 6 × 2000 = 12 000 daisies.

For the same data: the mode is 7 (most common) and the median is 7 (the middle value of 3, 4, 7, 7, 9).

#### Common mistakes

- Drawing food chain arrows the wrong way. The arrow points to the eater.
- Saying the predator peak happens at the same time as the prey peak.

#### Practical

**Required practical: measure the population size of a common species in a habitat, and use sampling techniques to investigate the effect of a factor on the distribution of this species.**

Estimating population size (random sampling with quadrats):

1. Lay out two tape measures at right angles to form a grid over the area.
2. Use a random number generator to pick coordinates, and place a quadrat there.
3. Count the number of the chosen species in the quadrat.
4. Repeat for at least 10 quadrats and calculate a mean per quadrat.
5. Scale up: population = mean per m² × total area in m².

Random placement avoids bias in choosing where to sample.

Investigating distribution (a transect):

1. Lay a tape measure from one area to another across a change, for example from the shade of a tree into open grass.
2. Place a quadrat at regular intervals along the line, such as every 2 m.
3. Count the species, or estimate percentage cover, in each quadrat.
4. Measure the factor at each point, such as light intensity with a light meter.

Plot the number of organisms against distance or against the factor.

### 4.7.2.2 How materials are cycled

#### What you need to know

- All materials in the living world are recycled to provide the building blocks for future organisms.
- The carbon cycle returns carbon from organisms to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide, to be used by plants in photosynthesis.
- The water cycle provides fresh water for plants and animals on land before draining into the seas. Water is continuously evaporated and precipitated.
- You should be able to explain the importance of the carbon and water cycles to living organisms.
- Decay by microorganisms returns carbon to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide, and mineral ions to the soil.

#### Explained

**The carbon cycle.**

| Process | What happens to carbon |
| --- | --- |
| Photosynthesis | Plants and algae take carbon dioxide out of the air and make glucose |
| Feeding | Carbon in plants passes to animals as they eat |
| Respiration | Plants, animals and microorganisms release carbon dioxide into the air |
| Death and decay | Microorganisms feed on dead organisms and waste, and release carbon dioxide by respiration |
| Combustion | Burning fossil fuels and wood releases carbon dioxide |
| Fossilisation | Some dead organisms do not decay and form fossil fuels over millions of years |

**The water cycle.** The Sun heats water in seas and lakes, which evaporates. Plants lose water vapour by transpiration. Water vapour cools and condenses into clouds, then falls as precipitation (rain, snow). It runs off into rivers, soaks into the ground, and drains back to the sea. Living things take in water to drink or through their roots.

### 4.7.2.3 Decomposition (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- You should be able to explain how temperature, water and availability of oxygen affect the rate of decay of biological material.
- Gardeners and farmers try to provide optimum conditions for rapid decay of waste biological material. The compost produced is used as a natural fertiliser for growing garden plants or crops.
- Anaerobic decay produces methane gas. Biogas generators can be used to produce methane gas as a fuel.
- You should be able to calculate rate changes in the decay of biological material, and translate information between numerical and graphical forms.

#### Explained

| Factor | Effect | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | Warm speeds up decay; too hot slows it | Microorganisms' enzymes work faster in warmth, but are denatured if it is too hot |
| Water | Moist conditions speed up decay | Microorganisms need water to grow and for their reactions |
| Oxygen | More oxygen speeds up decay | Most decomposers respire aerobically |

A compost heap is turned to let oxygen in, kept moist, and heats up as the microorganisms respire.

A biogas generator holds plant waste or animal dung without oxygen. Anaerobic bacteria break it down and give off methane, which can be burned for heating or cooking.

#### Practical

**Required practical (Biology only): investigate the effect of temperature on the rate of decay of fresh milk by measuring pH change.**

1. Put milk and sodium carbonate solution (which makes it alkaline) in a test tube with a few drops of phenolphthalein indicator, which is pink in alkaline conditions.
2. Put the tube and a separate tube of lipase solution in a water bath at a set temperature.
3. Add the lipase, start a stopwatch, and stir.
4. Lipase breaks down the fat in milk into fatty acids, so the pH falls. Stop the clock when the pink colour disappears.
5. Repeat at different temperatures.

Rate of decay = 1 ÷ time. A pH probe can be used instead of the indicator for more precise readings.

### 4.7.2.4 Impact of environmental change (Biology only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Environmental changes affect the distribution of species in an ecosystem. These changes include:
  - temperature
  - availability of water
  - composition of atmospheric gases.
- The changes may be seasonal, geographic or caused by human interaction.
- You should be able to evaluate the impact of environmental changes on the distribution of species in an ecosystem, given appropriate information.

#### Explained

| Change | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Seasonal | Birds such as swallows migrate south in winter as insects become scarce |
| Geographic | Fewer plant species grow on high mountains, where it is colder and windier |
| Human | Rising average temperatures let some species spread further north; air pollution with sulfur dioxide reduces lichen growth near cities |

### 4.7.3.1 Biodiversity

#### What you need to know

- Biodiversity is the variety of all the different species of organisms on Earth, or within an ecosystem.
- A great biodiversity ensures the stability of ecosystems by reducing the dependence of one species on another for food, shelter and the maintenance of the physical environment.
- The future of the human species on Earth relies on us maintaining a good level of biodiversity. Many human activities are reducing biodiversity, and only recently have measures been taken to try to stop this reduction.

#### Explained

In a diverse ecosystem, if one food source is lost, consumers have others, so the community stays stable. Humans depend on biodiversity for food, medicines, clean water and air, and materials.

### 4.7.3.2 Waste management

#### What you need to know

- Rapid growth in the human population and an increase in the standard of living mean that increasingly more resources are used and more waste is produced. Unless waste and chemical materials are properly handled, more pollution will be caused.
- Pollution can occur:
  - in water, from sewage, fertiliser or toxic chemicals
  - in air, from smoke and acidic gases
  - on land, from landfill and from toxic chemicals.
- Pollution kills plants and animals, which can reduce biodiversity.

#### Explained

Fertiliser and sewage in water cause algae to grow fast and block light. Plants below die, and bacteria decaying them use up the oxygen, so fish die. Acidic gases such as sulfur dioxide form acid rain, which damages trees and makes lakes too acidic for some species.

### 4.7.3.3 Land use

#### What you need to know

- Humans reduce the amount of land available for other animals and plants by building, quarrying, farming and dumping waste.
- The destruction of peat bogs, and other areas of peat, to produce garden compost reduces the area of this habitat and thus the variety of different plant, animal and microorganism species that live there (biodiversity).
- The decay or burning of the peat releases carbon dioxide into the atmosphere.

#### Explained

Peat forms over thousands of years from plant material that does not fully decay in waterlogged, acidic bogs. It stores a lot of carbon. Using it for compost destroys a rare habitat and releases that carbon as carbon dioxide. Peat-free compost is an alternative.

### 4.7.3.4 Deforestation

#### What you need to know

- Large-scale deforestation in tropical areas has occurred to:
  - provide land for cattle and rice fields
  - grow crops for biofuels.

#### Explained

Effects of deforestation:

- Less carbon dioxide is taken out of the air by photosynthesis.
- Burning trees to clear land releases carbon dioxide; decaying wood releases it too, through microorganisms' respiration.
- Habitats are destroyed, so biodiversity falls.
- Cattle and rice fields release methane.

### 4.7.3.5 Global warming

#### What you need to know

- Levels of carbon dioxide and methane in the atmosphere are increasing, and contribute to "global warming".
- You should be able to describe some of the biological consequences of global warming.
- You should understand that the scientific consensus about global warming and climate change is based on systematic reviews of thousands of peer-reviewed publications.

#### Explained

Biological consequences of global warming:

- loss of habitats, for example as sea levels rise and flood low-lying land
- changes in the distribution of species, as some move to cooler areas and others spread into new ones
- changes in migration patterns
- reduced biodiversity, as species that cannot move or adapt become extinct.

### 4.7.3.6 Maintaining biodiversity

#### What you need to know

Scientists and concerned citizens have put in place programmes to reduce the negative effects of humans on ecosystems and biodiversity. These include:

- breeding programmes for endangered species
- protection and regeneration of rare habitats
- reintroduction of field margins and hedgerows in agricultural areas where farmers grow only one type of crop
- reduction of deforestation and carbon dioxide emissions by some governments
- recycling resources rather than dumping waste in landfill.

You should be able to evaluate given information about methods that can be used to tackle problems caused by human impacts on the environment, and explain and evaluate the conflicting pressures on maintaining biodiversity.

#### Explained

Conflicting pressures: protecting biodiversity can cost money and jobs. A farmer may lose income by leaving field margins; a developing country may need to clear forest to grow food or earn money from timber; protecting a habitat may stop new houses being built. An evaluation weighs the benefits to biodiversity against these costs.

Hedgerows and field margins give habitats and food for wild flowers, insects and birds in land otherwise covered by one crop (a monoculture).

### 4.7.4.1 Trophic levels (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Trophic levels can be represented by numbers, starting at level 1 with plants and algae. Further trophic levels are numbered after this, depending on the order in which the organism feeds.
  - Level 1: plants and algae make their own food and are called producers.
  - Level 2: herbivores eat plants and algae and are called primary consumers.
  - Level 3: carnivores that eat herbivores are called secondary consumers.
  - Level 4: carnivores that eat other carnivores are called tertiary consumers.
- Apex predators are carnivores with no predators.
- Decomposers break down dead plant and animal matter by secreting enzymes into the environment. Small soluble food molecules then diffuse into the microorganism.

#### Explained

Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) digest food outside their bodies. That is why decay depends on conditions that suit enzymes: warmth, moisture and oxygen.

### 4.7.4.2 Pyramids of biomass (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Pyramids of biomass can be constructed to represent the relative amount of biomass in each trophic level of a food chain. Trophic level 1 is at the bottom of the pyramid.
- You should be able to construct accurate pyramids of biomass from appropriate data.

#### Explained

Each bar's width is drawn to scale for the dry mass of all the organisms at that level, in the same unit (such as g/m²). Pyramids of biomass are almost always pyramid-shaped, because biomass is lost at each level.

### 4.7.4.3 Transfer of biomass (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Producers are mostly plants and algae, which transfer about 1% of the incident energy from light for photosynthesis.
- Only approximately 10% of the biomass from each trophic level is transferred to the level above it.
- Losses of biomass are due to:
  - not all the ingested material is absorbed; some is egested as faeces
  - some absorbed material is lost as waste, such as carbon dioxide and water in respiration, and water and urea in urine.
- Large amounts of the glucose is used in respiration.
- You should be able to describe the differences between the trophic levels of organisms within an ecosystem, and calculate the efficiency of biomass transfers between trophic levels by percentages or fractions of mass.

```latex
\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{biomass transferred to the next level}}{\text{biomass available at the previous level}} \times 100
```

#### Worked example

Plants in a field have 5000 g/m² of biomass. The rabbits that eat them have 400 g/m². Calculate the efficiency of transfer.

400 ÷ 5000 × 100 = 8%.

Food chains are short (rarely more than 4 or 5 levels) because so little biomass reaches the top.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying energy is "used up". Biomass is lost from the chain as waste and through respiration.

### 4.7.5.1 Factors affecting food security (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Food security means having enough food to feed a population.
- Biological factors which are threatening food security include:
  - the increasing birth rate has threatened food security in some countries
  - changing diets in developed countries means scarce food resources are transported around the world
  - new pests and pathogens that affect farming
  - environmental changes that affect food production, such as widespread famine occurring in some countries if rains fail
  - the cost of agricultural inputs
  - conflicts that have arisen in some parts of the world, which affect the availability of water or food.
- Sustainable methods must be found to feed all people on Earth.

### 4.7.5.2 Farming techniques (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- The efficiency of food production can be improved by restricting energy transfer from food animals to the environment. This can be done by limiting their movement and by controlling the temperature of their surroundings.
- Some animals are fed high protein foods to increase growth.
- You should be able to understand and discuss the ethical objections to some modern intensive animal farming methods.

#### Explained

Animals kept in small, warm pens move less and lose less energy keeping warm, so more of their food becomes biomass (meat). This is intensive farming.

Ethical objections: the animals have little space and a poor quality of life; disease spreads easily in crowded conditions, so antibiotics are often used, which can increase antibiotic resistance; and it uses a lot of energy for heating.

### 4.7.5.3 Sustainable fisheries (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Fish stocks in the oceans are declining. It is important to maintain fish stocks at a level where breeding continues, or certain species may disappear altogether in some areas.
- Control of net size and the introduction of fishing quotas play important roles in conservation of fish stocks at a sustainable level.

#### Explained

| Method | How it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Larger mesh size in nets | Young, small fish escape and can grow and breed |
| Fishing quotas | Limits how many fish of each species can be caught, so enough remain to breed |

### 4.7.5.4 Role of biotechnology (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

- Modern biotechnology techniques enable large quantities of microorganisms to be cultured for food.
- The fungus Fusarium is useful for producing mycoprotein, a protein-rich food suitable for vegetarians. The fungus is grown on glucose syrup in aerobic conditions, and the biomass is harvested and purified.
- A genetically modified bacterium produces human insulin. When harvested and purified, this is used to treat people with diabetes.
- GM crops could provide more food, or food with an improved nutritional value, such as golden rice.

#### Explained

Mycoprotein is made in large vessels called fermenters, where the temperature, pH and oxygen are controlled so the fungus grows quickly. Golden rice is genetically modified to contain beta-carotene, which the body uses to make vitamin A; it could reduce vitamin A deficiency in countries where rice is the main food.

## 4.3 Infection and response

Pathogens cause communicable disease. This topic covers the main pathogens and diseases, how the body defends itself, vaccines, drugs and how new drugs are tested, plus monoclonal antibodies and plant disease (Biology only).

### 4.3.1.1 Communicable (infectious) diseases

#### What you need to know

- Pathogens are microorganisms that cause infectious disease. They may be viruses, bacteria, protists or fungi. They may infect plants or animals.
- They can be spread by direct contact, by water or by air.
- Bacteria and viruses may reproduce rapidly inside the body.
- Bacteria may produce poisons (toxins) that damage tissues and make us feel ill.
- Viruses live and reproduce inside cells, causing cell damage.
- You should be able to explain how the spread of diseases can be reduced or prevented.

#### Explained

| Pathogen | Size and nature | How it makes you ill |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Virus | Much smaller than bacteria; not a cell | Gets inside cells and makes copies of itself; the cell bursts, releasing new viruses, which damages tissue |
| Bacterium | Single prokaryotic cell | Reproduces quickly; releases toxins that damage tissues |
| Protist | Single eukaryotic cell | Often carried by a vector (an animal that spreads the disease) |
| Fungus | Some single cells; some grow as threads called hyphae | Hyphae grow into tissue; spores spread the disease |

Ways to reduce or prevent spread:

| Method | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Hygiene | Washing hands; cleaning kitchen surfaces; coughing into a tissue |
| Destroying or controlling vectors | Killing mosquitoes; using nets |
| Isolating infected individuals | Keeping someone with measles at home |
| Vaccination | Measles vaccine for children |

#### Common mistakes

- Calling a virus a cell.
- Saying all microorganisms are pathogens. Only those that cause disease are.

### 4.3.1.2 Viral diseases

#### What you need to know

- **Measles** is a viral disease showing symptoms of fever and a red skin rash. It is a serious illness that can be fatal if complications arise. Most young children are vaccinated against it. The measles virus is spread by inhalation of droplets from sneezes and coughs.
- **HIV** initially causes a flu-like illness. Unless successfully controlled with antiretroviral drugs, the virus attacks the body's immune cells. Late-stage HIV infection, or AIDS, occurs when the body's immune system becomes so badly damaged it can no longer deal with other infections or cancers. HIV is spread by sexual contact or exchange of body fluids, such as blood, which occurs when drug users share needles.
- **Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV)** is a widespread plant pathogen affecting many species of plants, including tomatoes. It gives a distinctive "mosaic" pattern of discolouration on the leaves, which affects the growth of the plant due to lack of photosynthesis.

#### Explained

| Disease | Host | Symptoms | Spread | Control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Measles | Humans | Fever; red skin rash | Droplets from coughs and sneezes | Vaccination; isolating patients |
| HIV | Humans | Flu-like illness at first; later the immune system is damaged (AIDS) | Sexual contact; exchange of body fluids such as blood (shared needles) | Antiretroviral drugs; condoms; not sharing needles |
| TMV | Plants, such as tomatoes | Mosaic pattern of discoloured leaves; less photosynthesis, so poor growth | Contact between plants; on hands and tools | Removing infected plants; good hygiene; resistant varieties |

TMV slows growth because the discoloured parts of the leaf have less chlorophyll, so the plant makes less glucose by photosynthesis.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying antibiotics treat HIV. HIV is a virus; antiretroviral drugs are used.
- Saying HIV and AIDS are the same. AIDS is the late stage of HIV infection.

### 4.3.1.3 Bacterial diseases

#### What you need to know

- **Salmonella** food poisoning is spread by bacteria ingested in food, or on food prepared in unhygienic conditions. In the UK, poultry are vaccinated against Salmonella to control the spread. Fever, abdominal cramps, vomiting and diarrhoea are caused by the bacteria and the toxins they secrete.
- **Gonorrhoea** is a sexually transmitted disease (STD) with symptoms of a thick yellow or green discharge from the vagina or penis and pain on urinating. It is caused by a bacterium and was easily treated with the antibiotic penicillin until many resistant strains appeared. Gonorrhoea is spread by sexual contact. Its spread can be controlled by treatment with antibiotics or the use of a barrier method of contraception, such as a condom.

#### Explained

| Disease | Symptoms | Spread | Control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Salmonella food poisoning | Fever, abdominal cramps, vomiting, diarrhoea | Eating contaminated food, such as undercooked chicken | Vaccinating poultry; cooking food thoroughly; keeping raw and cooked food apart; washing hands |
| Gonorrhoea | Thick yellow or green discharge; pain when urinating | Sexual contact | Antibiotics (some strains are now resistant to penicillin); condoms; tracing and testing sexual partners |

#### Common mistakes

- Saying gonorrhoea is caused by a virus.

### 4.3.1.4 Fungal diseases

#### What you need to know

- **Rose black spot** is a fungal disease where purple or black spots develop on leaves, which often turn yellow and drop early. It affects the growth of the plant as photosynthesis is reduced. It is spread in the environment by water or wind. Rose black spot can be treated by using fungicides and/or removing and destroying the affected leaves.

#### Explained

Fewer leaves, and leaves with less green area, mean less photosynthesis, so less glucose for growth. Spores from the spots are carried to other plants by wind or splashed by rain.

### 4.3.1.5 Protist diseases

#### What you need to know

- The pathogens that cause **malaria** are protists. The malarial protist has a life cycle that includes the mosquito. Malaria causes recurrent episodes of fever and can be fatal.
- The spread of malaria is controlled by preventing the vectors, mosquitoes, from breeding, and by using mosquito nets to avoid being bitten.

#### Explained

The mosquito is a vector: it carries the protist from one person to another when it feeds on blood. Ways to control malaria:

- Remove standing water where mosquitoes lay eggs.
- Spray insecticide to kill mosquitoes.
- Sleep under nets treated with insecticide.
- Take antimalarial drugs when travelling to affected areas.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying malaria is caused by the mosquito. It is caused by a protist; the mosquito is the vector.

### 4.3.1.6 Human defence systems

#### What you need to know

Non-specific defence systems of the human body against pathogens:

- the skin
- the nose
- the trachea and bronchi
- the stomach.

The role of the immune system in defending against disease. If a pathogen enters the body, the immune system tries to destroy it. White blood cells help to defend against pathogens by:

- phagocytosis
- antibody production
- antitoxin production.

#### Explained

**First line: stopping pathogens getting in.**

| Barrier | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Skin | A physical barrier; releases antimicrobial secretions; scabs seal cuts |
| Nose | Hairs and mucus trap particles that may contain pathogens |
| Trachea and bronchi | Cells make sticky mucus that traps pathogens; cells with cilia waft the mucus up to the throat to be swallowed |
| Stomach | Hydrochloric acid kills pathogens in food and drink |

**Second line: white blood cells.**

1. **Phagocytosis.** Some white blood cells (phagocytes) surround a pathogen, engulf it, and digest it.
2. **Antibodies.** Some white blood cells (lymphocytes) make antibodies. Each pathogen has antigens on its surface. An antibody has a shape that fits one particular antigen. Antibodies bind to the pathogen, clump pathogens together, and mark them to be destroyed.
3. **Antitoxins.** Some white blood cells make antitoxins, which bind to toxins released by bacteria and neutralise them.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying white blood cells "eat" bacteria. Use "engulf" and "digest".
- Saying antibodies kill pathogens directly. They bind to them, so other white blood cells can destroy them.
- Saying antitoxins kill bacteria. They neutralise the toxins.

### 4.3.1.7 Vaccination

#### What you need to know

- Vaccination involves introducing small quantities of dead or inactive forms of a pathogen into the body to stimulate the white blood cells to produce antibodies.
- If the same pathogen re-enters the body, the white blood cells respond quickly to produce the correct antibodies, preventing infection.
- You should be able to explain how vaccination will prevent illness in an individual, and how the spread of the pathogen can be reduced by immunising a large proportion of the population.

#### Explained

1. The vaccine contains dead or inactive pathogens, carrying the pathogen's antigens.
2. White blood cells detect the antigens and make the matching antibodies.
3. Memory cells remain in the blood.
4. If the live pathogen enters the body later, the white blood cells make the right antibodies much faster and in greater numbers.
5. The pathogen is destroyed before it can cause illness.

If most of a population is vaccinated, the pathogen cannot spread easily, because most people it meets are immune. This protects the few who cannot be vaccinated, such as very young babies. This is called herd immunity.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a vaccine contains antibodies. It contains antigens (on dead or inactive pathogens); the body makes the antibodies.
- Saying a vaccine cures a disease. It prevents it.

### 4.3.1.8 Antibiotics and painkillers

#### What you need to know

- Antibiotics, such as penicillin, are medicines that help to cure bacterial disease by killing infective bacteria inside the body.
- It is important that specific bacteria should be treated by specific antibiotics.
- The use of antibiotics has greatly reduced deaths from infectious bacterial diseases. However, the emergence of strains resistant to antibiotics is of great concern.
- Antibiotics cannot kill viral pathogens.
- Painkillers and other medicines are used to treat the symptoms of disease but do not kill pathogens.
- It is difficult to develop drugs that kill viruses without also damaging the body's tissues.

#### Explained

Viruses live inside the body's own cells, so a drug that destroys them would also destroy the cells. That is why antibiotics do not work on colds or flu, and why there are few antiviral drugs.

Doctors should not prescribe antibiotics for viral infections, and patients should finish the course. Both reduce the chance of resistant bacteria developing (see 4.6.3.7).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying antibiotics kill viruses.
- Saying painkillers cure a disease.

### 4.3.1.9 Discovery and development of drugs

#### What you need to know

- Traditionally drugs were extracted from plants and microorganisms.
  - The heart drug digitalis originates from foxgloves.
  - The painkiller aspirin originates from willow.
  - Penicillin was discovered by Alexander Fleming from the Penicillium mould.
- Most new drugs are synthesised by chemists in the pharmaceutical industry. However, the starting point may still be a chemical extracted from a plant.
- New medical drugs have to be tested and trialled before being used to check that they are safe and effective.
- New drugs are extensively tested for toxicity, efficacy and dose.
- Preclinical testing is done in a laboratory using cells, tissues and live animals.
- Clinical trials use healthy volunteers and patients.
  - Very low doses of the drug are given at the start of the clinical trial.
  - If the drug is found to be safe, further clinical trials are carried out to find the optimum dose for the drug.
  - In double blind trials, some patients are given a placebo.

#### Explained

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Toxicity | Whether the drug is harmful |
| Efficacy | Whether the drug works |
| Dose | How much should be given, and how often |
| Placebo | A dummy treatment that looks like the drug but contains no drug |
| Double blind trial | Neither the patients nor the doctors know who has the drug and who has the placebo, so the results are not biased |

The stages, in order:

1. Discovery: a possible drug is found or designed.
2. Preclinical testing on cells and tissues in a lab, then on live animals.
3. Clinical trials, first on a small number of healthy volunteers at a very low dose, to check safety.
4. Then on patients with the disease, to find the optimum dose and check that it works, usually double blind with a placebo.
5. Results are checked by other scientists (peer review) before they are published, to make sure the work is valid and not biased.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying healthy volunteers are used to find out whether the drug works. They are used to check safety; patients show whether it works.
- Confusing a placebo with a vaccine.

### 4.3.2.1 Producing monoclonal antibodies (Biology only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Monoclonal antibodies are produced from a single clone of cells. The antibodies are specific to one binding site on one protein antigen, so they can target a specific chemical or specific cells in the body.
- They are produced by stimulating mouse lymphocytes to make a particular antibody. The lymphocytes are combined with a particular kind of tumour cell to make a cell called a hybridoma cell.
- The hybridoma cell can both divide and make the antibody. Single hybridoma cells are cloned to produce many identical cells that all produce the same antibody.
- A large amount of the antibody can be collected and purified.

#### Explained

Why combine two cells: a lymphocyte makes the antibody but does not divide well; a tumour cell divides fast but makes no antibody. The hybridoma does both.

1. Inject a mouse with the chosen antigen.
2. The mouse's lymphocytes make antibodies against that antigen.
3. Collect the lymphocytes and fuse them with tumour cells to make hybridoma cells.
4. Select a hybridoma that makes the right antibody and clone it.
5. Collect and purify the identical antibodies.

### 4.3.2.2 Uses of monoclonal antibodies (Biology only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

Examples include:

- for diagnosis, such as in pregnancy tests
- in laboratories to measure the levels of hormones and other chemicals in blood, or to detect pathogens
- in research to locate or identify specific molecules in a cell or tissue, by binding to them with a fluorescent dye
- to treat some diseases. For cancer, the monoclonal antibody can be bound to a radioactive substance, a toxic drug or a chemical that stops cells growing and dividing. It delivers the substance to the cancer cells without harming other cells in the body.

Monoclonal antibodies create more side effects than expected. They are not yet as widely used as everyone hoped when they were first developed.

#### Explained

**Pregnancy test.** A pregnant woman's urine contains the hormone hCG.

1. Urine is placed on the stick.
2. In the first zone, mobile antibodies attached to a blue bead bind to any hCG.
3. In the test zone, fixed antibodies bind to the hCG–bead complex, so a blue line appears: pregnant.
4. In the control zone, fixed antibodies bind to the free mobile antibodies, so a blue line always appears if the test has worked.

**Cancer treatment.** Cancer cells have particular antigens on their surface. Antibodies made against those antigens carry a drug or radioactive substance directly to the cancer cells, so fewer healthy cells are harmed than with normal chemotherapy.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Bind only to specific cells, so healthy cells are less affected | More side effects than expected, such as fever, vomiting and low blood pressure |
| Can be used to treat a range of conditions | Expensive to develop |
| | Producing them uses animals (mice) |

### 4.3.3.1 Detection and identification of plant diseases (Biology only)

#### What you need to know

Plant diseases can be detected by:

- areas of decay (rot)
- growths
- malformed stems or leaves
- discolouration
- the presence of pests.

(HT only) Identification can be made by:

## 4.4 Bioenergetics

Photosynthesis stores energy in glucose; respiration releases it. This topic covers both reactions, what limits the rate of photosynthesis, what happens in muscles during exercise, and metabolism.

### 4.4.1.1 Photosynthetic reaction

#### What you need to know

- Photosynthesis is an endothermic reaction in which energy is transferred from the environment to the chloroplasts by light.
- You should know the word equation and recognise the chemical symbols CO₂, H₂O, O₂ and C₆H₁₂O₆.

```latex
\text{carbon dioxide} + \text{water} \xrightarrow{\text{light}} \text{glucose} + \text{oxygen}
```

```latex
6\,CO_2 + 6\,H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{light}} C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6\,O_2
```

#### Explained

Endothermic means energy is taken in from the surroundings. In photosynthesis, the energy comes from light, absorbed by the green pigment chlorophyll inside chloroplasts.

Where the reactants come from: carbon dioxide diffuses into the leaf through the stomata; water is taken up by the roots and carried to the leaves in the xylem. Oxygen is released through the stomata or used in respiration.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling photosynthesis exothermic.
- Saying plants get their food from the soil. They make glucose; they take only water and mineral ions from the soil.

### 4.4.1.2 Rate of photosynthesis

#### What you need to know

Factors affecting the rate of photosynthesis:

- temperature
- light intensity
- carbon dioxide concentration
- amount of chlorophyll.

- You should be able to measure and calculate rates of photosynthesis, extract and interpret graphs of photosynthesis rate involving one limiting factor, and plot and draw appropriate graphs selecting appropriate scale for axes.
- (HT only) Limiting factors are important in the economics of enhancing the conditions in greenhouses to gain the maximum rate of photosynthesis while still maintaining profit.
- (HT only) You should be able to explain graphs of photosynthesis rate involving two or three factors, and decide which is the limiting factor.
- (HT only) You should understand and use inverse proportion: the inverse square law and light intensity in the context of photosynthesis.

#### Explained

A **limiting factor** is the factor in shortest supply, which stops the rate going any higher.

| Factor | Effect on rate |
| --- | --- |
| Light intensity | Rate rises as light increases, until another factor becomes limiting; then the graph levels off |
| Carbon dioxide concentration | Rate rises as CO₂ increases, until another factor becomes limiting; then the graph levels off |
| Temperature | Rate rises as temperature increases, because enzymes work faster, up to an optimum; above it, the enzymes are denatured and the rate falls sharply |
| Amount of chlorophyll | Less chlorophyll (for example from disease or magnesium deficiency) means less light absorbed, so a lower rate |

Reading a graph: on the rising part, the factor on the x-axis is limiting. Where the line is flat, something else is limiting.

(HT only) **Two or three factors.** If a graph shows two lines, for example at two CO₂ concentrations, and the higher-CO₂ line levels off higher, then CO₂ was limiting the lower line on its flat part.

(HT only) **Inverse square law.** Light intensity is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from the light source.

```latex
\text{light intensity} \propto \frac{1}{d^2}
```

Doubling the distance makes the light intensity a quarter (1 ÷ 2² = ¼). Halving the distance makes it four times as bright.

(HT only) **Greenhouses.** Growers can raise the rate by adding heating, lighting and extra carbon dioxide (often from paraffin heaters, which give off CO₂ and heat). Each costs money, so a grower adds them only while the extra crop is worth more than the cost. Adding more of a factor that is not limiting wastes money.

#### Worked example

(HT only) A lamp is 10 cm from a plant. It is moved to 20 cm. By what factor does the light intensity change?

1. The distance doubles.
2. Intensity ∝ 1 ÷ d², so it changes by 1 ÷ 2² = ¼.

Answer: the light intensity falls to a quarter.

A pondweed releases 36 bubbles of oxygen in 3 minutes. Calculate the rate.

36 ÷ 3 = 12 bubbles per minute.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the rate keeps rising as light increases. It levels off when another factor limits it.
- Saying temperature makes the rate fall because of "too much heat". Say the enzymes are denatured.
- (HT) Saying doubling the distance halves the light intensity. It quarters it.

#### Practical

**Required practical: investigate the effect of light intensity on the rate of photosynthesis using an aquatic organism such as pondweed.**

1. Put a piece of pondweed in a boiling tube of sodium hydrogencarbonate solution (which supplies carbon dioxide).
2. Place a lamp at a measured distance, for example 10 cm, from the tube.
3. Leave it for a few minutes to adjust.
4. Count the oxygen bubbles released in one minute, or collect the gas in an upturned measuring cylinder or gas syringe and measure its volume.
5. Repeat at different distances, such as 20, 30 and 40 cm.

Variables: the independent variable is light intensity (distance from the lamp); the dependent variable is the rate of oxygen production; control variables include temperature, carbon dioxide concentration and the same piece of pondweed.

A clear tank of water between the lamp and the tube, or an LED lamp, stops the lamp heating the water, which would change the temperature as well as the light. Collecting the gas is more accurate than counting bubbles, because bubbles vary in size.

### 4.4.1.3 Uses of glucose from photosynthesis

#### What you need to know

The glucose produced in photosynthesis may be:

- used for respiration
- converted into insoluble starch for storage
- used to produce fat or oil for storage
- used to produce cellulose, which strengthens the cell wall
- used to produce amino acids for protein synthesis.

To produce proteins, plants also use nitrate ions that are absorbed from the soil.

#### Explained

Starch is used for storage because it is insoluble: it does not affect the water balance of the cells by osmosis, and it stays where it is stored. Seeds often store fats or oils, which hold a lot of energy in a small space.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying glucose is stored. Plants store it as starch.

### 4.4.2.1 Aerobic and anaerobic respiration

#### What you need to know

- Cellular respiration is an exothermic reaction which is continuously occurring in living cells.
- The energy transferred supplies all the energy needed for living processes.
- Respiration in cells can take place aerobically (using oxygen) or anaerobically (without oxygen), to transfer energy.
- Organisms need energy for:
  - chemical reactions to build larger molecules
  - movement
  - keeping warm.
- Aerobic respiration: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water.
- Anaerobic respiration in muscles: glucose → lactic acid.
- As the oxidation of glucose is incomplete in anaerobic respiration, much less energy is transferred than in aerobic respiration.
- Anaerobic respiration in plant and yeast cells: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide. Anaerobic respiration in yeast cells is called fermentation and has economic importance in the manufacture of bread and alcoholic drinks.
- You should be able to compare the processes of aerobic and anaerobic respiration with regard to the need for oxygen, the differing products and the relative amounts of energy transferred.

#### Explained

```latex
C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6\,O_2 \rightarrow 6\,CO_2 + 6\,H_2O
```

| | Aerobic | Anaerobic in muscles | Anaerobic in plants and yeast |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Oxygen needed? | Yes | No | No |
| Products | Carbon dioxide and water | Lactic acid | Ethanol and carbon dioxide |
| Energy transferred | A lot | Much less | Much less |
| Where in the cell | Mostly mitochondria | Cytoplasm | Cytoplasm |

Fermentation uses: in bread making, the carbon dioxide makes the dough rise; in brewing, the ethanol is the alcohol in drinks such as beer and wine.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying respiration is breathing. Respiration is a chemical reaction in cells; breathing is moving air in and out of the lungs.
- Saying respiration "makes energy". Energy cannot be made; respiration transfers (releases) energy.
- Saying anaerobic respiration in muscles makes carbon dioxide.

### 4.4.2.2 Response to exercise

#### What you need to know

- During exercise the human body reacts to the increased demand for energy.
- Heart rate, breathing rate and breath volume increase during exercise to supply the muscles with more oxygenated blood.
- If insufficient oxygen is supplied, anaerobic respiration takes place in muscles. The incomplete oxidation of glucose causes a build-up of lactic acid and creates an oxygen debt.
- During long periods of vigorous activity muscles become fatigued and stop contracting efficiently.
- (HT only) Blood flowing through the muscles transports the lactic acid to the liver, where it is converted back into glucose. Oxygen debt is the amount of extra oxygen the body needs after exercise to react with the accumulated lactic acid and remove it from the cells.

#### Explained

| Change during exercise | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Heart rate rises | Pumps blood faster, delivering more oxygen and glucose to the muscles and removing carbon dioxide faster |
| Breathing rate and breath volume rise | More oxygen enters the blood, and more carbon dioxide is removed |

After hard exercise you keep breathing heavily. This is paying back the oxygen debt: (HT) the extra oxygen is used to deal with the lactic acid, which the liver converts back into glucose.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying lactic acid causes the heart rate to rise. The rise is to deliver more oxygen.
- (HT) Saying lactic acid is broken down in the muscles. It is carried to the liver.

### 4.4.2.3 Metabolism

#### What you need to know

- Metabolism is the sum of all the reactions in a cell or the body.
- The energy transferred by respiration in cells is used by the organism for the continual enzyme-controlled processes of metabolism that synthesise new molecules.
- Metabolism includes:
  - conversion of glucose to starch, glycogen and cellulose
  - the formation of lipid molecules from a molecule of glycerol and three molecules of fatty acids
  - the use of glucose and nitrate ions to form amino acids, which in turn are used to synthesise proteins
  - respiration
  - breakdown of excess proteins to form urea for excretion.

#### Explained

Some reactions build big molecules from small ones (starch, glycogen and cellulose from glucose; lipids from glycerol and fatty acids; proteins from amino acids). These need energy from respiration. Others break molecules down (respiration itself; excess amino acids broken down into urea in the liver). Every one of these reactions is controlled by enzymes.

Glycogen is the storage carbohydrate in animals, made mainly in the liver and muscles. Starch is the storage carbohydrate in plants. Cellulose builds plant cell walls.

4. (HT only) A lamp is moved from 30 cm to 15 cm from a plant. Calculate the change in light intensity. [2 marks]
5. In the pondweed practical, explain why a tank of water is placed between the lamp and the pondweed. [2 marks]
6. Give three uses of glucose in a plant. [3 marks]

### Mark scheme

2. Energy is transferred from the environment (light) into the reaction (1).

## 4.10 Using resources

Chemistry turns the Earth's resources into useful products, and has to do it sustainably. This topic covers sustainable development, drinking water, waste water, new ways to extract metals (HT), life cycle assessment and recycling, then (Chemistry only) corrosion, alloys, materials, the Haber process and fertilisers.

### 4.10.1.1 Using the Earth's resources and sustainable development

#### What you need to know

- Humans use the Earth's resources to provide warmth, shelter, food and transport.
- Natural resources, supplemented by agriculture, provide food, timber, clothing and fuels.
- Finite resources from the Earth, oceans and atmosphere are processed to provide energy and materials.
- Chemistry plays an important role in improving agricultural and industrial processes to provide new products, and in sustainable development, which is development that meets the needs of current generations without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
- You should be able to state examples of natural products that are supplemented or replaced by agricultural and synthetic products, and distinguish between finite and renewable resources given appropriate information.

#### Explained

| Finite resources (will run out) | Renewable resources (can be replaced) |
| --- | --- |
| Crude oil, coal, natural gas | Timber from managed forests |
| Metal ores | Crops such as cotton |
| | Wind, solar energy |

Examples of natural products replaced by synthetic ones: wool and cotton by polyester; natural rubber by synthetic rubber; wood by plastics in some uses.

### 4.10.1.2 Potable water

#### What you need to know

- Water of appropriate quality is essential for life. For humans, drinking water should have sufficiently low levels of dissolved salts and microbes. Water that is safe to drink is called potable water. Potable water is not pure water in the chemical sense because it contains dissolved substances.
- The methods used to produce potable water depend on available supplies of water and local conditions.
- In the United Kingdom, rain provides water with low levels of dissolved substances (fresh water) that collects in the ground and in lakes and rivers, and most potable water is produced by:
  - choosing an appropriate source of fresh water
  - passing the water through filter beds
  - sterilising.
- Sterilising agents used for potable water include chlorine, ozone or ultraviolet light.
- If supplies of fresh water are limited, desalination of salty water or sea water may be required. Desalination can be done by distillation or by processes that use membranes, such as reverse osmosis. These processes require large amounts of energy.
- You should be able to distinguish between potable water and pure water, describe the differences in treatment of ground water and salty water, and give reasons for the steps used to produce potable water.

#### Explained

| Step | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Filter beds | Remove solid particles such as leaves and grit |
| Sterilising with chlorine, ozone or UV | Kills microbes |

Desalination is expensive because of the energy needed to boil water (distillation) or push it through membranes at high pressure (reverse osmosis). It is used where fresh water is scarce, such as in some Middle Eastern countries.

#### Practical

**Required practical: analysis and purification of water samples from different sources, including pH, dissolved solids and distillation.**

1. Test the pH of each sample with universal indicator or a pH probe.
2. Measure the mass of an empty evaporating basin. Add a measured volume of the sample, heat gently until all the water evaporates, and reweigh. The increase in mass is the dissolved solids.
3. Distil a sample of salt water: heat it in a flask, pass the steam through a condenser, and collect the pure water. Check the distilled water has pH 7 and leaves no solid when evaporated.

### 4.10.1.3 Waste water treatment

#### What you need to know

- Urban lifestyles and industrial processes produce large amounts of waste water that require treatment before being released into the environment. Sewage and agricultural waste water require removal of organic matter and harmful microbes. Industrial waste water may require removal of organic matter and harmful chemicals.
- Sewage treatment includes:
  - screening and grit removal
  - sedimentation to produce sewage sludge and effluent
  - anaerobic digestion of sewage sludge
  - aerobic biological treatment of effluent.
- You should be able to comment on the relative ease of obtaining potable water from waste, ground and salt water.

#### Explained

1. Screening removes large objects and grit.
2. In settlement tanks, heavy solids sink to form sludge; the liquid on top is effluent.
3. Sludge is broken down by anaerobic bacteria (anaerobic digestion), which produces methane that can be burned as fuel, and leftover material can be used as fertiliser.
4. Effluent has air bubbled through it so aerobic bacteria break down the remaining organic matter. It can then be released into rivers.

Ease of getting potable water: ground water is easiest (just filter and sterilise); waste water needs much more treatment; salt water needs desalination, which uses a lot of energy.

### 4.10.1.4 Alternative methods of extracting metals (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- The Earth's resources of metal ores are limited.
- Copper ores are becoming scarce and new ways of extracting copper from low-grade ores include phytomining and bioleaching. These methods avoid traditional mining methods of digging, moving and disposing of large amounts of rock.
- Phytomining uses plants to absorb metal compounds. The plants are harvested and then burned to produce ash that contains metal compounds.
- Bioleaching uses bacteria to produce leachate solutions that contain metal compounds.
- The metal compounds can be processed to obtain the metal. For example, copper can be obtained from solutions of copper compounds by displacement using scrap iron or by electrolysis.
- You should be able to evaluate alternative biological methods of metal extraction, given appropriate information.

#### Explained

| | Phytomining | Bioleaching |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How | Plants grown on low-grade ore absorb copper compounds; they are burned and the ash is processed | Bacteria break down the ore, releasing a solution of copper compounds (leachate) |
| Advantages | Less damage to the landscape than mining; uses low-grade ore; less waste rock | No high temperatures needed; uses low-grade ore |
| Disadvantages | Very slow | Slow; the leachate can be toxic if it escapes |

Getting copper from the solution: Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu (scrap iron is cheap), or electrolysis, which gives very pure copper.

### 4.10.2.1 Life cycle assessment

#### What you need to know

- Life cycle assessments (LCAs) are carried out to assess the environmental impact of products in each of these stages:
  - extracting and processing raw materials
  - manufacturing and packaging
  - use and operation during its lifetime
  - disposal at the end of its useful life, including transport and distribution at each stage.
- Use of water, resources, energy sources and production of some wastes can be fairly easily quantified. Allocating numerical values to pollutant effects is less straightforward and requires value judgements, so LCA is not a purely objective process.
- Selective or abbreviated LCAs can be devised to evaluate a product, but these can be misused to reach pre-determined conclusions, for example in support of claims for advertising purposes.
- You should be able to carry out simple comparative LCAs for shopping bags made from plastic and paper.

#### Explained

| Stage | Plastic bag | Paper bag |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raw materials | Crude oil (finite) | Trees (renewable) |
| Manufacture | Uses less energy and water | Uses more energy and water |
| Use | Strong; can be reused many times | Weaker; usually used once |
| Disposal | Not biodegradable; lasts in landfill for a long time; can be recycled | Biodegradable; easily recycled |

The conclusion depends on how many times each bag is reused and which impacts are weighted most, which is why LCAs need value judgements.

### 4.10.2.2 Ways of reducing the use of resources

#### What you need to know

- The reduction in use, reuse and recycling of materials by end users reduces the use of limited resources, use of energy sources, waste and environmental impacts.
- Metals, glass, building materials, clay ceramics and most plastics are produced from limited raw materials. Much of the energy for the processes comes from limited resources. Obtaining raw materials from the Earth by quarrying and mining causes environmental impacts.
- Some products, such as glass bottles, can be reused. Glass bottles can be crushed and melted to make different glass products. Other products cannot be reused and so are recycled for a different use.
- Metals can be recycled by melting and recasting or reforming into different products. The amount of separation required for recycling depends on the material and the properties required of the final product. For example, some scrap steel can be added to iron from a blast furnace to reduce the amount of iron that needs to be extracted from iron ore.
- You should be able to evaluate ways of reducing the use of limited resources, given appropriate information.

#### Explained

Recycling saves raw materials and usually uses less energy than extracting new material, produces less waste in landfill, and reduces mining. But collecting, transporting and sorting cost energy and money.

### 4.10.3.1 Corrosion and its prevention (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Corrosion is the destruction of materials by chemical reactions with substances in the environment. Rusting is an example of corrosion. Both air and water are necessary for iron to rust.
- Corrosion can be prevented by applying a coating that acts as a barrier, such as greasing, painting or electroplating. Aluminium has an oxide coating that protects the metal from further corrosion.
- Some coatings are reactive and contain a more reactive metal to provide sacrificial protection, such as zinc is used to galvanise iron.
- You should be able to describe experiments and interpret results to show that both air and water are necessary for rusting, and explain sacrificial protection in terms of relative reactivity.

#### Explained

Experiment with three iron nails:

| Tube | Conditions | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Air and water | Rusts |
| B | Boiled water (no dissolved air) with a layer of oil on top | Does not rust |
| C | Dry air, with calcium chloride to absorb water | Does not rust |

Only the nail with both air and water rusts.

**Sacrificial protection.** Zinc is more reactive than iron, so it reacts (oxidises) instead of the iron, even when the coating is scratched. In galvanising, iron is coated with zinc. Blocks of magnesium are bolted to ship hulls for the same reason.

### 4.10.3.2 Alloys as useful materials (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Most metals in everyday use are alloys.
- Bronze is an alloy of copper and tin. Brass is an alloy of copper and zinc.
- Gold used as jewellery is usually an alloy with silver, copper and zinc. The proportion of gold in the alloy is measured in carats. 24 carat is 100% (pure gold), and 18 carat gold is 75% gold.
- Steels are alloys of iron that contain specific amounts of carbon and other metals. High carbon steel is strong but brittle. Low carbon steel is softer and more easily shaped. Steels containing chromium and nickel (stainless steels) are hard and resistant to corrosion.
- Aluminium alloys are low density.
- You should be able to recall a use of each of the alloys specified, and interpret and evaluate the composition and uses of alloys other than those specified given appropriate information.

#### Explained

| Alloy | Made from | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bronze | Copper and tin | Statues, medals, bells |
| Brass | Copper and zinc | Door handles, musical instruments, plumbing fittings |
| Gold alloys | Gold with silver, copper, zinc | Jewellery (harder than pure gold) |
| High carbon steel | Iron with more carbon | Cutting tools |
| Low carbon steel | Iron with less carbon | Car bodies, wire |
| Stainless steel | Iron with chromium and nickel | Cutlery, sinks |
| Aluminium alloys | Aluminium with other metals | Aircraft |

#### Worked example

What percentage of gold is in 9 carat gold? 9 ÷ 24 × 100 = 37.5%.

### 4.10.3.3 Ceramics, polymers and composites (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Most of the glass we use is soda-lime glass, made by heating a mixture of sand, sodium carbonate and limestone. Borosilicate glass, made from sand and boron trioxide, melts at higher temperatures than soda-lime glass.
- Clay ceramics, including pottery and bricks, are made by shaping wet clay and then heating in a furnace.
- The properties of polymers depend on what monomers they are made from and the conditions under which they are made. For example, low density (LD) and high density (HD) poly(ethene) are produced from ethene.
- Thermosoftening polymers melt when they are heated. Thermosetting polymers do not melt when they are heated.
- Most composites are made of two materials, a matrix or binder surrounding and binding together fibres or fragments of the other material, which is called the reinforcement.
- You should be able to explain how low density and high density poly(ethene) are both produced from ethene, explain the difference between thermosoftening and thermosetting polymers in terms of their structures, and compare quantitatively the physical properties of glass and clay ceramics, polymers, composites and metals.

#### Explained

LD and HD poly(ethene): different conditions (temperature, pressure, catalyst) produce different chain structures. LD has branched chains, so it is flexible (carrier bags). HD has straighter chains packed closer together, so it is stronger and more rigid (bottles, pipes).

| | Thermosoftening | Thermosetting |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structure | Separate chains, with weak forces between them | Chains joined by cross-links (covalent bonds) |
| When heated | Softens and melts, so can be reshaped | Does not melt; chars at high temperature |
| Example | Poly(ethene) | Melamine (used in kitchen worktops) |

Composites: reinforced concrete (steel rods in concrete), fibreglass (glass fibres in a polymer), carbon fibre (carbon fibres in a polymer), and wood (cellulose fibres in lignin).

### 4.10.4.1 The Haber process (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- The Haber process is used to manufacture ammonia, which can be used to produce nitrogen-based fertilisers.
- The raw materials for the Haber process are nitrogen and hydrogen. Nitrogen is obtained from the air, and hydrogen may be obtained from natural gas or other sources.
- The purified gases are passed over a catalyst of iron at a high temperature (about 450 °C) and a high pressure (about 200 atmospheres). Some of the hydrogen and nitrogen reacts to form ammonia. The reaction is reversible, so some of the ammonia produced breaks down into nitrogen and hydrogen: nitrogen + hydrogen ⇌ ammonia (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃).
- On cooling, the ammonia liquefies and is removed. The remaining hydrogen and nitrogen are recycled.
- (HT only) You should be able to interpret graphs of reaction conditions versus rate, apply the principles of dynamic equilibrium to the Haber process, explain the trade-off between rate of production and position of equilibrium, and explain how the commercially used conditions relate to the availability and cost of raw materials and energy supplies, control of equilibrium position and rate.

#### Explained

(HT only) The forward reaction is exothermic, and there are 4 gas molecules on the left and 2 on the right.

| Condition | Effect on yield | Effect on rate | Compromise chosen |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | A lower temperature gives a higher yield | A lower temperature makes it too slow | 450 °C: a reasonable yield at a reasonable rate |
| Pressure | A higher pressure gives a higher yield (fewer molecules on the right) | Higher pressure also increases rate | 200 atmospheres: higher pressures cost too much to build and run, and are more dangerous |
| Catalyst (iron) | No effect | Faster | Used, because it saves time and so money |

Recycling the unreacted gases means almost nothing is wasted, even though only a small percentage reacts each time.

### 4.10.4.2 Production and uses of NPK fertilisers (Chemistry only)

#### What you need to know

- Compounds of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium are used as fertilisers to improve agricultural productivity. NPK fertilisers contain compounds of all three elements.
- Industrial production of NPK fertilisers can be achieved using a variety of raw materials in several integrated processes. NPK fertilisers are formulations of various salts containing appropriate percentages of the elements.
- Ammonia can be used to manufacture ammonium salts and nitric acid.
- Potassium chloride, potassium sulfate and phosphate rock are obtained by mining, but phosphate rock cannot be used directly as a fertiliser.
- Phosphate rock is treated with nitric acid or sulfuric acid to produce soluble salts that can be used as fertilisers.
- You should be able to recall the names of the salts produced when phosphate rock is treated with nitric acid, sulfuric acid and phosphoric acid, and compare the industrial production of fertilisers with laboratory preparations of the same compounds, given appropriate information.

#### Explained

| Source | Process | Product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ammonia + nitric acid | Neutralisation | Ammonium nitrate |
| Ammonia + sulfuric acid | Neutralisation | Ammonium sulfate |
| Phosphate rock + nitric acid | | Phosphoric acid and calcium nitrate |
| Phosphate rock + sulfuric acid | | Calcium sulfate and calcium phosphate (single superphosphate) |
| Phosphate rock + phosphoric acid | | Calcium phosphate (triple superphosphate) |

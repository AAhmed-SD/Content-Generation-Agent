### ATP and why respiration never stops

*Refs: OCR A J247/J250 B1.3a; OCR B J257/J260 B4.1.2.*

#### What you need to know

- Respiration is a chemical process that happens all the time in every living cell.
- The energy released by respiration is used to make a molecule called **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate).
- ATP is the cell's immediate energy supply. When it is broken down, it releases energy for processes the cell needs.
- Cells cannot store much ATP, so respiration has to continue all the time to keep making it.
- Aerobic respiration makes much more ATP from each glucose molecule than anaerobic respiration.
- Respiration is exothermic: some of the energy is transferred to the surroundings as heat.

#### Explained

Glucose holds a lot of energy, but in one large package. A cell needs energy in small, ready-to-use amounts, many times a second. Respiration releases the energy from glucose step by step and uses it to join a phosphate group onto ADP (adenosine diphosphate), making ATP.

ADP + phosphate + energy from respiration → ATP

When a process in the cell needs energy, ATP is broken back into ADP and phosphate, releasing the energy where it is needed. The ADP is then recycled and turned back into ATP by respiration.

Processes that use ATP include:

- muscle contraction, so animals can move
- active transport, such as root hair cells taking up mineral ions, or the gut absorbing glucose against a concentration gradient
- building large molecules, such as proteins from amino acids, and starch, glycogen and lipids
- nerve impulses and cell division.

Because a cell holds only a few seconds' supply of ATP, it must respire continuously. If respiration stops (for example, cyanide blocks it), ATP runs out within moments and the cell dies.

Aerobic respiration breaks glucose down completely to carbon dioxide and water, so it releases much more energy and makes far more ATP per glucose (roughly 30 or more) than anaerobic respiration (2). That is why muscles that are respiring anaerobically tire quickly, and why yeast fermenting without oxygen grows slowly.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying respiration "makes energy". Energy cannot be made. Respiration releases energy stored in glucose and transfers it to ATP.
- Confusing respiration with breathing. Breathing moves air in and out of the lungs; respiration is a reaction inside cells, and plants respire day and night.
- Thinking only animal cells respire, or that plants respire only at night. Plant cells respire all the time; in daylight photosynthesis is usually faster, so the plant takes in carbon dioxide overall.

### Field sampling methods

*Refs: OCR A J247/J250 B6.1a; OCR B J257/J260 B3.4.2.*

#### What you need to know

- **Distribution** is where organisms are found; **abundance** is how many there are.
- Plants and slow-moving animals are sampled with quadrats. Random quadrats estimate population size; a transect shows how distribution changes across a habitat. (These are in Biology 4.7.2.1.)
- Mobile animals are estimated by **capture–mark–release–recapture**.
- Animals are collected with pooters, sweep nets, pond nets and pitfall traps.
- **Identification keys** are used to name the organisms found.
- **Indicator species** show the level of pollution.
- Abiotic factors are measured with instruments such as light meters, thermometers and pH probes.
- Results from a sample are **scaled up** to estimate the population of the whole area.

#### Explained

**Capture–mark–release–recapture.** Animals that move about cannot be counted in a quadrat, so a sample is caught, marked harmlessly (for example a small dot of non-toxic paint on a beetle's shell) and released. Later a second sample is caught. Some of these will be marked. If the marked animals have mixed evenly back into the population, the fraction of marked animals in the second sample equals the fraction of the whole population that was marked:

estimated population = (number in first sample × number in second sample) ÷ number marked in second sample

The method only works if:

- no animals are born, die, arrive or leave between the two samples
- the marks do not wash or rub off
- the marks do not make the animals easier for predators to see
- the marked animals have time to mix back into the population.

**Collecting equipment.**

- Pooter: a jar with two tubes. Sucking on one tube draws small insects up the other into the jar.
- Sweep net: swept through long grass to catch flying and crawling insects.
- Pond net: swept through water to catch aquatic animals.
- Pitfall trap: a container sunk into the ground with its rim level with the soil, often with a cover raised above it to keep out rain. Animals walking across the ground fall in. Traps are checked often so the animals can be released.

**Identification keys.** A dichotomous key asks a series of questions, each with two answers (for example "Does it have six legs?"), until only one name is left.

**Indicator species.** Some species survive only in clean conditions; others are found only where there is pollution. For example, many lichens cannot grow where the air contains a lot of sulfur dioxide, and mayfly larvae need water with plenty of dissolved oxygen, while bloodworms and sludge worms tolerate polluted, low-oxygen water.

**Scaling up.** If a sample covers a known area, multiply the mean number per sample by the number of sample areas that fit into the whole habitat.

#### Worked example

A student catches 40 woodlice under logs in a garden, marks each with a dot of paint and releases them. Two days later she catches 50 woodlice, of which 8 are marked. Estimate the population.

estimated population = (40 × 50) ÷ 8 = 2000 ÷ 8 = 250 woodlice.

Ten quadrats of 0.25 m² in a 120 m² field contain a total of 30 daisies. Mean per quadrat = 3. Number of quadrats that fit in the field = 120 ÷ 0.25 = 480. Estimated population = 3 × 480 = 1440 daisies.

#### Common mistakes

- Dividing by the wrong number in capture–recapture. The number of marked animals recaptured always goes on the bottom.
- Leaving too long between samples, so births and deaths change the population, or too short, so the marked animals have not mixed back in.
- Forgetting to convert quadrat area and field area to the same units before scaling up.
- Placing quadrats where it looks interesting instead of using random coordinates, which biases the estimate.

### OCR's named diseases

*Refs: OCR A J247/J250 B6.3f; OCR B J257/J260 B2.1.4, B2.1.5.*

#### What you need to know

OCR A asks for at least one human infection, one plant disease and sexually transmitted infections including HIV/AIDS; its plant examples are tobacco mosaic virus, barley powdery mildew and crown gall. OCR B names influenza, Salmonella, athlete's foot, malaria and HIV/AIDS in humans, and tobacco mosaic virus, ash dieback and crown gall in plants. HIV, Salmonella, malaria and tobacco mosaic virus are in Biology 4.3.1.2 to 4.3.1.5. For each of the others, know the pathogen type, how it spreads, its effects and how it is controlled.

| Disease | Pathogen | Spread | Effects | Control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Influenza (flu) | Virus | Droplets from coughs and sneezes; touching contaminated surfaces | Fever, aches, tiredness, cough; can be fatal in elderly or ill people | Yearly vaccine, hand washing, staying at home when ill; antiviral drugs in some cases |
| Athlete's foot | Fungus | Direct contact; damp floors, towels and shoes | Itchy, flaking, cracked skin, usually between the toes | Antifungal creams or powders; dry feet well; do not share towels; wear sandals in communal showers |
| Barley powdery mildew | Fungus | Spores carried by wind | White powdery patches on leaves; less photosynthesis, lower grain yield | Fungicides, resistant varieties, crop rotation, removing infected stubble |
| Crown gall | Bacterium (*Agrobacterium tumefaciens*) | Soil bacteria enter through wounds in roots or stem base; contaminated tools | Swollen, tumour-like galls; weakened, stunted plant | No cure: remove and destroy infected plants, avoid wounding, clean tools, do not replant in infected soil |
| Ash dieback | Fungus | Spores from infected fallen leaves blown by wind; movement of infected young trees | Leaves wilt and fall early, dark patches on bark, crown of tree dies back; many trees die | No cure: restrict movement of ash plants, remove infected young trees, breed and plant tolerant trees |

#### Explained

**Influenza** viruses change their surface proteins slightly every year, so antibodies made after last year's infection or vaccine may not recognise this year's virus. That is why flu vaccines are offered every autumn, especially to older people and others at high risk.

**Athlete's foot** fungi feed on dead skin and grow best where it is warm and damp. Keeping feet dry removes the conditions they need.

**Barley powdery mildew** grows on the leaf surface and sends feeding structures into the leaf cells. Covered leaf area cannot absorb light, so less photosynthesis happens and less glucose is available for grain.

**Crown gall** is caused by a bacterium that transfers some of its own DNA into the plant's cells. The plant cells then divide out of control and form a gall. Scientists use this same ability of *Agrobacterium* to carry useful genes into crop plants in genetic engineering.

**Ash dieback** was first confirmed in Britain in 2012 and has since spread through most of the country. It is expected to kill a large majority of ash trees, which affects the many insects, lichens and birds that depend on ash. A small number of trees show tolerance, and these are being used to breed more resistant trees.

**STIs.** HIV (a virus) is in Biology 4.3.1.2 and gonorrhoea (a bacterium) in 4.3.1.3. Both are spread by sexual contact and their spread is reduced by using condoms, testing and treatment. Any correct STI can be given where OCR asks for "sexually transmitted infections".

#### Common mistakes

- Treating a viral disease with antibiotics. Influenza is a virus; antibiotics have no effect on it.
- Saying fungicides "cure" ash dieback. There is no practical cure for trees in woodland; control depends on stopping spread and breeding tolerant trees.
- Calling crown gall a fungal disease because it causes swellings. It is bacterial.

### Hydroponics and biological control

*Refs: OCR A J247 B6.2b; OCR B J257 B6.4.6.*

#### What you need to know

- **Hydroponics** is growing plants without soil, with their roots in a solution of mineral ions.
- **Biological control** uses a living organism (a predator, parasite or pathogen) to reduce the population of a pest.
- Know the advantages and disadvantages of each, compared with using soil, fertilisers and chemical pesticides. (Fertilisers, pesticides and genetic modification are in Biology 4.6.2.4 and 4.7.5.)

#### Explained

**Hydroponics.** The nutrient solution can be adjusted to give each crop exactly the mineral ions it needs, and it is pumped past the roots so they also get oxygen. Crops are usually grown in glasshouses, where temperature, light and carbon dioxide can also be controlled.

Advantages:

- high yields in a small space, all year round
- no soil, so no soil pests, diseases or weeds
- mineral ions are supplied exactly, with less waste
- crops can be grown where the soil is poor or there is little land.

Disadvantages:

- expensive to set up and to run (pumps, heating and lighting use energy)
- needs skilled monitoring of the solution
- a disease in the shared solution can spread quickly to every plant
- plants may need artificial support without soil.

**Biological control.** A natural enemy of the pest is released. Examples: ladybirds eat aphids; a tiny parasitic wasp lays its eggs in glasshouse whitefly larvae, killing them.

Advantages:

- no chemical residues on food or in the environment
- usually affects only the target pest, not other species
- the control organism can breed, so it may not need to be reapplied
- pests do not become resistant in the way they can to pesticides.

Disadvantages:

- slower than a chemical pesticide
- reduces the pest but does not wipe it out
- the introduced species may eat other species, or itself become a pest. For example, cane toads introduced to Australia to control beetles in sugar cane fields spread widely and harmed native wildlife.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying hydroponics needs no fertiliser. The nutrient solution is itself a fertiliser solution.
- Saying biological control "kills all the pests". The aim is to keep the pest population low; if all the pests died, the control organism would die out too.

### Check questions

2. Explain why muscle cells respiring anaerobically can work for only a short time. [2 marks]
3. A student catches 24 snails in a garden, marks them and releases them. A week later she catches 30 snails, of which 6 are marked. Estimate the snail population. [2 marks]
4. Give two conditions that must be true for the estimate in question 3 to be valid. [2 marks]
5. Describe how a pitfall trap is set up, and suggest why it should be checked every day. [3 marks]
6. Mayfly larvae are found upstream of a sewage outlet but not downstream. Explain what this suggests. [2 marks]
7. Explain why a person who had flu last winter can catch flu again this winter. [2 marks]
8. Barley powdery mildew covers the leaves of barley plants. Explain why it reduces the yield of grain. [3 marks]
9. Describe how crown gall disease enters a plant and how its spread can be reduced. [3 marks]
10. A grower changes from growing lettuces in soil to hydroponics. Evaluate this change. [4 marks]
11. Give one advantage and one risk of using biological control instead of a chemical pesticide. [2 marks]

### Mark scheme

1. Any one use: muscle contraction / active transport / building large molecules such as proteins (1). Cells store very little ATP (1); so respiration must continue to keep making ATP (1).
2. Anaerobic respiration makes far less ATP per glucose (1); and lactic acid builds up, causing muscle fatigue (1).

Everything else in these OCR outcomes is in the AQA Chemistry notes: relative formula mass in 4.3.1.2, paper chromatography and Rf values in 4.8.1.3, and electrolysis with inert electrodes in 4.4.3.

### Overview

#### Board differences

- OCR A (Gateway) J248 and J250: C2.1d (empirical formula), C2.1g (paper and thin layer chromatography), C3.4e (electrolysis with inert and non-inert electrodes).
- OCR B (Twenty First Century) J258 and J260: C3.4.6 and C3.4.7 (empirical formula, with ratio arithmetic), C5.1.6 (chromatographic methods, including aqueous and non-aqueous solvents and locating agents).
- AQA GCSE does not ask for empirical formulae, and its chromatography required practical uses paper only. AQA's electrolysis required practical uses inert electrodes.

### Empirical formulae

*Refs: OCR A J248/J250 C2.1d; OCR B J258/J260 C3.4.6, C3.4.7.*

#### What you need to know

- The **empirical formula** is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.
- The **molecular formula** is the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule.
- Deduce an empirical formula from the numbers of atoms in a model, a diagram or a molecular formula, and the other way round (what models fit a given empirical formula).
- Calculate an empirical formula from the masses (or percentages by mass) of each element, using relative atomic masses.

#### Explained

To get the empirical formula from a molecular formula, divide all the numbers by their highest common factor.

| Compound | Molecular formula | Empirical formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ethene | C₂H₄ | CH₂ |
| Glucose | C₆H₁₂O₆ | CH₂O |
| Hydrogen peroxide | H₂O₂ | HO |
| Water | H₂O | H₂O |

Ionic compounds and giant covalent structures (such as sodium chloride and silicon dioxide) do not form molecules, so their formula is always the empirical formula: NaCl, SiO₂.

From masses, the method is:

1. Write the mass (or percentage) of each element.
2. Divide each by its relative atomic mass. This gives the relative number of moles of atoms.
3. Divide every answer by the smallest one.
4. If the results are not close to whole numbers, multiply them all by the same small number (2, 3 or 4) to make them whole.
5. Write the formula with the whole numbers as subscripts.

If you know the relative formula mass of the compound, you can go on to find the molecular formula: divide Mr by the mass of one empirical formula unit, and multiply the empirical formula by the answer.

#### Worked example

**From a diagram.** A ball-and-stick model shows a molecule with 4 carbon atoms and 10 hydrogen atoms. Molecular formula C₄H₁₀. Divide by 2: empirical formula C₂H₅.

**From masses.** A 4.0 g sample of an oxide of copper contains 3.2 g of copper. Find its empirical formula. (Ar: Cu = 63.5, O = 16)

| | Cu | O |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mass (g) | 3.2 | 4.0 − 3.2 = 0.8 |
| ÷ Ar | 3.2 ÷ 63.5 = 0.0504 | 0.8 ÷ 16 = 0.0500 |
| ÷ smallest | 1.01 | 1 |

Ratio 1 : 1, so the empirical formula is CuO.

**Needing a multiplier.** A compound contains 52.9% aluminium and 47.1% oxygen by mass. (Ar: Al = 27, O = 16)

- Al: 52.9 ÷ 27 = 1.96; O: 47.1 ÷ 16 = 2.94
- Divide by 1.96: Al 1, O 1.5
- Multiply by 2: Al 2, O 3. Empirical formula Al₂O₃.

**Molecular formula.** A hydrocarbon has empirical formula CH₂ and Mr = 56. Mass of CH₂ = 12 + 2 = 14. 56 ÷ 14 = 4, so the molecular formula is C₄H₈.

#### Common mistakes

- Dividing the masses by the atomic number, or by the Mr of the element's molecule (O₂ = 32), instead of the relative atomic mass.
- Rounding too early. A ratio of 1 : 1.5 must be doubled to 2 : 3, not rounded to 1 : 2.
- Forgetting to work out the mass of oxygen by subtraction when only the metal and the oxide are weighed.
- Simplifying a molecular formula that cannot be simplified, or writing an empirical formula with fractions.

### Thin layer chromatography and choosing a solvent

*Refs: OCR A J248/J250 C2.1g; OCR B J258/J260 C5.1.6.*

#### What you need to know

- Describe paper chromatography and thin layer chromatography (TLC).
- In TLC the stationary phase is a thin layer of a solid such as silica gel or alumina, spread on a glass, plastic or aluminium sheet. The mobile phase is the solvent.
- Substances separate because they spend different amounts of time dissolved in the mobile phase and stuck to the stationary phase.
- The solvent can be water (aqueous) or another liquid such as ethanol or propanone (non-aqueous). Substances that do not dissolve in water need a non-aqueous solvent.
- Colourless spots are made visible with a **locating agent** or, on some TLC plates, ultraviolet light.
- Rf values (in Chemistry 4.8.1.3) are calculated the same way for TLC as for paper.

#### Explained

**Running a TLC plate.**

1. Draw a pencil line about 1 cm from the bottom of the plate. Pencil does not dissolve and move with the solvent; ink would.
2. Put a small, concentrated spot of each sample on the line, using a capillary tube.
3. Stand the plate in a beaker or tank with solvent below the pencil line, so the spots do not dissolve into the solvent pool.
4. Cover the container so the air inside is saturated with solvent vapour; this stops the solvent evaporating from the plate and gives even movement.
5. Remove the plate when the solvent is near the top, and mark the solvent front in pencil straight away, before it evaporates.
6. Locate the spots and measure the distances.

**Why TLC is often preferred to paper.** The solid layer is more even than paper fibres, so spots are sharper and better separated. TLC runs in a few minutes, needs only tiny samples, and the plate can be used with a wider range of solvents, including ones that would damage paper.

**Choosing the solvent.** A substance only moves if it dissolves in the mobile phase. Food colourings dissolve in water, so water works for them. The ink in a permanent marker does not dissolve in water, so it stays on the baseline in water but separates in ethanol or propanone. The same substance has a different Rf in different solvents, so Rf values are only compared when the solvent (and stationary phase) are the same. A pure substance gives a single spot in every solvent tried; if a sample gives one spot in one solvent, trying a second solvent is a stronger test of purity.

**Locating agents.** Many substances (such as amino acids and sugars) are colourless. After the plate dries, it is sprayed with or exposed to a chemical that reacts with them to give coloured spots. Examples:

- ninhydrin spray, which gives purple spots with amino acids
- iodine vapour, which makes many organic compounds show up as brown spots.

Some TLC plates contain a substance that glows under ultraviolet light; compounds on the plate block the glow and show as dark spots, which are circled in pencil.

#### Worked example

On a TLC plate run in ethanol, the solvent front is 6.0 cm from the baseline. An unknown dye gives spots at 1.5 cm and 4.2 cm. Reference dye X gives one spot at 4.2 cm.

Rf of the upper spot = 4.2 ÷ 6.0 = 0.70. Rf of X = 0.70. The unknown contains two substances; one may be X. To be more confident, run both samples again in a different solvent and check the spots still match.

#### Common mistakes

- Putting the baseline below the solvent level, so the samples wash off into the solvent.
- Measuring to the edge of a spot instead of its centre.
- Calling the solvent the stationary phase. The solvent moves: it is the mobile phase.
- Concluding two substances are the same from one solvent alone. Different substances can share an Rf value in one solvent.

### Electrolysis with non-inert electrodes

*Refs: OCR A J248/J250 C3.4e.*

#### What you need to know

- **Inert electrodes** (graphite or platinum) do not react; they only carry electrons. The products come from the ions in the electrolyte (Chemistry 4.4.3.4).
- **Non-inert (active) electrodes** take part in the reaction. In copper sulfate solution with copper electrodes, the copper anode dissolves and copper is deposited on the cathode.
- This is how copper is purified and how objects are electroplated.
- Compare the results of electrolysing copper sulfate solution with inert and with copper electrodes.

#### Explained

**Copper sulfate with graphite (inert) electrodes.**

- Cathode (−): Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. A pink-brown coating of copper forms.
- Anode (+): oxygen gas is given off (from water or hydroxide ions; sulfate ions are not discharged).
- The blue colour fades as Cu²⁺ ions are used up, and the solution becomes acidic because H⁺ ions and sulfate ions are left behind.

**Copper sulfate with copper (non-inert) electrodes.**

- Anode (+): Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻. Copper atoms lose electrons and go into solution, so the anode gets lighter. No gas is given off.
- Cathode (−): Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. The cathode gets heavier.
- Copper ions are added to the solution at the anode as fast as they are removed at the cathode, so the concentration and the blue colour stay the same.
- In a perfect cell the mass lost by the anode equals the mass gained by the cathode.

**Purifying copper.** The anode is a block of impure copper; the cathode is a thin sheet of pure copper. Copper from the anode dissolves and is deposited as pure copper on the cathode. Impurities that do not dissolve fall below the anode as a sludge, which can contain valuable metals such as silver and gold.

**Electroplating.** The object to be coated is made the cathode; the anode is the plating metal; the electrolyte contains ions of the plating metal. For example, to silver-plate a spoon, the spoon is the cathode, a silver bar is the anode and the solution contains silver ions. Plating is used to prevent corrosion or to improve appearance.

#### Worked example

A student electrolyses copper sulfate solution with copper electrodes for 20 minutes.

| | Start mass (g) | End mass (g) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anode | 5.42 | 5.15 |
| Cathode | 5.38 | 5.64 |

Anode change = 5.15 − 5.42 = −0.27 g. Cathode change = 5.64 − 5.38 = +0.26 g. The cathode gains almost exactly what the anode loses, as expected. The small difference can come from copper flaking off the anode or the cathode not being dried fully before weighing.

#### Practical

This is part of OCR's electrolysis practical group (PAG C2 in J248, PAG C1 in J250; see [practical-activity-groups.md](practical-activity-groups.md)). A typical investigation varies the current or the time and measures the change in mass of each copper electrode. Clean the electrodes with emery paper first; rinse the cathode with water and then propanone, and dry it before weighing so the mass of liquid is not included. Copper sulfate solution is harmful and an irritant, so wear eye protection.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying oxygen is given off at a copper anode. With a copper anode, the copper itself is oxidised instead.
- Writing the anode half equation with electrons on the wrong side. Oxidation is loss of electrons: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻.
- Saying the solution changes colour when copper electrodes are used. The concentration of Cu²⁺ stays the same.
- Making the object to be plated the anode. It must be the cathode, where metal ions gain electrons and form metal.

### Check questions

1. Give the empirical formula of: (a) C₃H₆ (b) C₂H₆O (c) N₂O₄. [3 marks]
2. 2.4 g of magnesium reacts completely with oxygen to form 4.0 g of magnesium oxide. Find the empirical formula of magnesium oxide. (Ar: Mg = 24, O = 16) [3 marks]
3. A compound is 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen and 53.3% oxygen by mass. Its Mr is 60. Find its empirical and molecular formulae. (Ar: C = 12, H = 1, O = 16) [4 marks]
4. Give two advantages of thin layer chromatography over paper chromatography. [2 marks]
5. A student runs a sample of permanent marker ink in water and sees no movement. Explain why, and suggest how to improve the experiment. [2 marks]
6. Explain why a locating agent is needed to see amino acids on a chromatogram. [2 marks]
7. Copper sulfate solution is electrolysed with copper electrodes. Write the half equation at each electrode. [2 marks]
8. Explain why the blue colour of the solution fades with graphite electrodes but not with copper electrodes. [3 marks]
9. Describe how electrolysis is used to purify copper. [4 marks]

### Mark scheme

1. (a) CH₂ (1) (b) C₂H₆O (it cannot be simplified) (1) (c) NO₂ (1).
2. Mass of O = 4.0 − 2.4 = 1.6 g (1). Mg: 2.4 ÷ 24 = 0.1; O: 1.6 ÷ 16 = 0.1 (1). Ratio 1 : 1, MgO (1).
3. C 40.0 ÷ 12 = 3.33; H 6.7 ÷ 1 = 6.7; O 53.3 ÷ 16 = 3.33 (1). Ratio 1 : 2 : 1, empirical formula CH₂O (1). Mass of CH₂O = 30; 60 ÷ 30 = 2 (1). Molecular formula C₂H₄O₂ (1).
4. Any two: faster; better / sharper separation; needs smaller samples; more solvents can be used; more sensitive.
5. The ink is insoluble in water, so it does not move with the mobile phase (1). Use a non-aqueous solvent such as ethanol or propanone (1).
6. Amino acids are colourless, so the spots cannot be seen (1). The locating agent (for example ninhydrin) reacts with them to form coloured spots (1).
7. Anode: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ (1). Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (1).
8. Blue colour comes from Cu²⁺ ions (1). With graphite, Cu²⁺ ions are removed at the cathode and not replaced, so the colour fades (1). With copper, the anode releases Cu²⁺ ions as fast as they are removed, so the concentration stays the same (1).
9. Impure copper is the anode (1); pure copper is the cathode (1); electrolyte is copper sulfate solution (1); copper dissolves from the anode and pure copper is deposited on the cathode, while impurities fall as sludge (1).

#### Explained

**PAG 6: Respiration in yeast with different substrates (OCR B B4.1.5).** Yeast respires sugars and releases carbon dioxide. The rate of carbon dioxide release shows the rate of respiration.

1. Make up a yeast suspension and equal-concentration solutions of different sugars (for example glucose, sucrose, fructose, lactose).
2. Mix equal volumes of yeast suspension and one sugar solution in a boiling tube. Stand it in a water bath at a fixed temperature (about 35 °C) for a few minutes so it reaches that temperature.
3. Fit a bung and delivery tube. Either collect the gas in an upturned measuring cylinder or gas syringe, or count bubbles through water in a set time.
4. Measure the volume of gas in a fixed time (for example 5 minutes). Repeat for each sugar, and do a control with water instead of sugar.

Control: the same mass of yeast, volume and concentration of sugar, temperature and time. Yeast usually respires glucose and fructose fastest, sucrose more slowly (it must first be broken down) and lactose hardly at all (yeast lacks the enzyme to digest it).

**PAG 6: Respirometers.** A respirometer measures oxygen uptake by small organisms such as woodlice or germinating seeds. The organisms sit on a gauze above a chemical that absorbs carbon dioxide (such as soda lime or potassium hydroxide). As they use oxygen, the gas volume in the tube falls, and a drop of coloured liquid in a capillary tube moves towards them. Distance moved per minute (or volume, from the tube's cross-section) gives the rate. A second tube with glass beads instead of organisms is the control, to correct for temperature and pressure changes.

**PAG 6: Exercise and pulse rate.** Measure resting pulse (beats in 15 seconds × 4), exercise for a fixed time (such as 2 minutes of step-ups), then measure pulse every minute until it returns to resting. The recovery time is a measure of fitness.

**Ethics with living organisms.** Use the smallest number of animals needed, keep them in their habitat conditions, handle them gently, return them to where they were found, and never use vertebrates for experiments that harm them. Students' own data (pulse, reaction time) are used only with their consent.

#### Common mistakes

- Not letting the tubes reach the water-bath temperature before starting timing.
- Forgetting the control tube in a respirometer, so temperature changes are mistaken for oxygen uptake.
- In yeast experiments, changing the mass of sugar instead of keeping the concentration the same.
- Counting bubbles when bubbles are different sizes; a gas syringe gives more accurate volumes.

### Chemistry PAGs

*Refs: OCR A J248 PAG C1 to C8; J250 PAG C1 to C5. OCR B J258 PAG 1 to 8; J260 PAG C1 to C5.*

#### What you need to know

| PAG | Techniques | Typical activity | AQA notes |

#### Explained

**PAG 1: Reactivity trend.** Two common ways to put metals in order of reactivity:

- *Temperature rise with acid.* Add the same mass of each metal powder to the same volume and concentration of dilute hydrochloric acid in an insulated cup, and record the maximum temperature rise. A more reactive metal gives a larger rise. Use powders of similar particle size, and only metals that react safely (magnesium, zinc, iron, copper; never sodium or potassium with acid).
- *Displacement.* Put a piece of each metal into solutions of the other metals' salts. If the metal is coated or the solution changes colour (or the temperature rises), the added metal is more reactive and has displaced the other. Record results in a grid and count the reactions for each metal.

Halogen displacement (chlorine, bromine and iodine solutions with halide solutions) is another reactivity trend: a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from its salt, shown by a colour change.

**PAG 4: Distillation.**

- *Simple distillation* separates a liquid from a dissolved solid (water from salty water). Heat the flask; the water boils, the vapour is cooled in a Liebig condenser (cold water in at the bottom, out at the top, so the jacket stays full), and the pure liquid drips into a beaker. The salt stays in the flask. The thermometer bulb sits at the side arm, to read the temperature of the vapour that is distilling.
- *Fractional distillation* separates liquids with close boiling points, such as ethanol (78 °C) and water (100 °C). A fractionating column packed with glass beads sits on the flask. Vapour condenses and re-boils many times up the column, so the vapour at the top is mostly the lower-boiling liquid. Collect the liquid while the thermometer reads near 78 °C; change the beaker when the temperature rises.
- Safety: use anti-bumping granules for smooth boiling, never heat a sealed apparatus, and use an electric heater or water bath for flammable liquids like ethanol.

**PAG 5: Identification of species.** Use clean nichrome wire (dipped in acid and heated until there is no colour) for flame tests; add sodium hydroxide solution drop by drop for metal hydroxide precipitates; acidify with dilute nitric acid before adding silver nitrate (halides) or with hydrochloric acid before adding barium chloride (sulfate), so carbonate ions do not give a false precipitate. Details are in Chemistry 4.8.3.

**PAG 6: Titration.** Rinse the burette with the solution it will hold, fill it with the funnel removed, and read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level. Add the alkali from a pipette (using a pipette filler) to a conical flask on a white tile, add a few drops of indicator, then add acid quickly for a rough titre, and then slowly, drop by drop near the end point, for accurate titres. Repeat until two titres agree within 0.10 cm³ (concordant) and take their mean.

#### Common mistakes

- Placing the thermometer bulb in the boiling liquid during distillation instead of at the side arm.
- Running condenser water in at the top, which leaves air in the jacket and poor cooling.
- Using a dirty flame-test wire, so colours from earlier tests contaminate the result.
- Including the rough titre in the mean.

## 4.4 Atomic structure

Atoms have a tiny, dense nucleus surrounded by electrons. Some nuclei are unstable and give out radiation as they decay. This topic covers the structure of the atom and how the model developed, isotopes, alpha, beta, gamma and neutron radiation, nuclear equations, half-life, contamination and irradiation, and (Physics only) background radiation, medical uses, fission and fusion.

### 4.4.1.1 The structure of an atom

#### What you need to know

- Atoms are very small, with a radius of about 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.
- The basic structure of an atom is a positively charged nucleus made of protons and neutrons, surrounded by negatively charged electrons.
- The radius of a nucleus is less than 1/10 000 of the radius of an atom. Most of the mass of an atom is concentrated in the nucleus.
- The electrons are arranged at different distances from the nucleus (different energy levels). The electron arrangements may change with the absorption of electromagnetic radiation (moving further from the nucleus to a higher energy level) or by the emission of electromagnetic radiation (moving closer to the nucleus to a lower energy level).

#### Explained

| Particle | Relative charge | Relative mass | Where |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Proton | +1 | 1 | Nucleus |
| Neutron | 0 | 1 | Nucleus |
| Electron | −1 | Very small (about 1/2000) | Energy levels around the nucleus |

Scale: if an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be about the size of a pea on the centre spot. Almost all of the atom is empty space, yet nearly all the mass is in that pea.

An electron that absorbs the right amount of electromagnetic radiation jumps to a higher energy level, further out. When it drops back, it gives out electromagnetic radiation.

#### Common mistakes

- Writing the size of the nucleus as 1/10 000 of the atom's mass. It is about its radius.

### 4.4.1.2 Mass number, atomic number and isotopes

#### What you need to know

- In an atom, the number of electrons is equal to the number of protons in the nucleus. Atoms have no overall electrical charge.
- All atoms of a particular element have the same number of protons. The number of protons in an atom of an element is called its atomic number.
- The total number of protons and neutrons in an atom is called its mass number.
- Atoms can be represented with the mass number written top left and the atomic number bottom left of the symbol, for example sodium-23 as ²³₁₁Na.
- Atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons; these atoms are called isotopes of that element.
- Atoms turn into positive ions if they lose one or more outer electrons.
- You should be able to relate differences between isotopes to differences in conventional representations of their identities, charges and masses.

#### Worked example

How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in an atom of ³⁷₁₇Cl?

1. Protons = atomic number = 17.
2. Neutrons = mass number − atomic number = 37 − 17 = 20.
3. Electrons = protons = 17 (it is a neutral atom).

Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are isotopes: both have 6 protons, but carbon-12 has 6 neutrons and carbon-14 has 8.

#### Common mistakes

- Thinking isotopes have different numbers of protons. Then they would be different elements.

### 4.4.1.3 The development of the model of the atom

#### What you need to know

- New experimental evidence may lead to a scientific model being changed or replaced.
- Before the discovery of the electron, atoms were thought to be tiny spheres that could not be divided.
- The discovery of the electron led to the plum pudding model of the atom: a ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it.
- The results from the alpha particle scattering experiment led to the conclusion that the mass of an atom was concentrated at the centre (nucleus) and that the nucleus was charged. This nuclear model replaced the plum pudding model.
- Niels Bohr adapted the nuclear model by suggesting that electrons orbit the nucleus at specific distances. Theoretical calculations of Bohr agreed with experimental observations.
- Later experiments led to the idea that the positive charge of any nucleus could be subdivided into a whole number of smaller particles, each particle having the same amount of positive charge. The name proton was given to these particles.
- The experimental work of James Chadwick provided the evidence to show the existence of neutrons within the nucleus. This was about 20 years after the nucleus became an accepted scientific idea.
- You should be able to describe why the new evidence from the scattering experiment led to a change in the atomic model, and the difference between the plum pudding model and the nuclear model.

#### Explained

The alpha scattering experiment: positive alpha particles were fired at a very thin sheet of gold foil.

| Observation | What it showed |
| --- | --- |
| Most alpha particles went straight through | Most of the atom is empty space |
| Some were deflected by small angles | The centre of the atom is positively charged (it repels the positive alpha particles) |
| A very few bounced almost straight back | The mass and charge are concentrated in a very small nucleus |

The plum pudding model could not explain the bouncing back, because its positive charge and mass were spread out thinly, so alpha particles would all pass through with only tiny deflections.

Timeline: solid sphere → plum pudding (electron discovered) → nuclear model (alpha scattering) → Bohr model (electrons in fixed shells) → protons → neutrons (Chadwick).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the scattering experiment discovered neutrons. It showed a small, dense, charged nucleus; neutrons came later.

### 4.4.2.1 Radioactive decay and nuclear radiation

#### What you need to know

- Some atomic nuclei are unstable. The nucleus gives out radiation as it changes to become more stable. This is a random process called radioactive decay.
- Activity is the rate at which a source of unstable nuclei decays. Activity is measured in becquerel (Bq).
- Count-rate is the number of decays recorded each second by a detector (for example a Geiger-Muller tube).
- The nuclear radiation emitted may be:
  - an alpha particle (α): two neutrons and two protons (the same as a helium nucleus)
  - a beta particle (β): a high speed electron ejected from the nucleus as a neutron turns into a proton
  - a gamma ray (γ): electromagnetic radiation from the nucleus
  - a neutron (n).
- You should be able to apply your knowledge to the uses of radiation and evaluate the best sources of radiation to use in a given situation.

#### Explained

| Radiation | What it is | Ionising power | Range in air | Stopped by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alpha | 2 protons + 2 neutrons | Strongly ionising | A few centimetres | A sheet of paper, or skin |
| Beta | Fast electron from the nucleus | Moderately ionising | About a metre | A few millimetres of aluminium |
| Gamma | Electromagnetic wave | Weakly ionising | Very far | Several centimetres of lead or metres of concrete (reduced, not fully stopped) |

Ionising means knocking electrons off atoms, turning them into ions.

Choosing a source:

- Smoke alarm: alpha, because it ionises air well but is stopped by smoke particles.
- Paper or foil thickness control: beta, because a small change in thickness changes how much gets through.
- Tracer in the body or sterilising equipment: gamma, because it passes out of the body or through packaging.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a beta particle is an electron from the shells. It comes from the nucleus.
- Saying 1 Bq is one decay per minute. It is one decay per second.

### 4.4.2.2 Nuclear equations

#### What you need to know

- Nuclear equations are used to represent radioactive decay.
- In a nuclear equation an alpha particle may be written as ⁴₂He and a beta particle as ⁰₋₁e.
- The emission of the different types of nuclear radiation may cause a change in the mass and/or the charge of the nucleus:
  - alpha decay causes both the mass and charge of the nucleus to decrease (mass number down 4, atomic number down 2)
  - beta decay does not cause the mass of the nucleus to change, but causes the charge of the nucleus to increase (atomic number up 1)
  - the emission of a gamma ray does not cause the mass or the charge of the nucleus to change.
- You should be able to use the names and symbols of common nuclei and particles to write balanced equations that show single alpha and beta decay.

#### Worked example

Radium-226 (atomic number 88) decays by alpha emission to radon (Rn). Write the equation.

²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He

Check: top numbers 226 = 222 + 4; bottom numbers 88 = 86 + 2.

Carbon-14 (atomic number 6) decays by beta emission to nitrogen (N).

¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e

Check: 14 = 14 + 0; 6 = 7 + (−1).

#### Common mistakes

- Making the atomic number go down in beta decay. A neutron becomes a proton, so it goes up by 1.
- Forgetting the element symbol changes whenever the atomic number changes.

### 4.4.2.3 Half-lives and the random nature of radioactive decay

#### What you need to know

- Radioactive decay is random. You cannot tell which nucleus will decay next or when.
- The half-life of a radioactive isotope is the time it takes for the number of nuclei of the isotope in a sample to halve, or the time it takes for the count rate (or activity) from a sample containing the isotope to fall to half its initial level.
- You should be able to explain the concept of half-life and how it is related to the random nature of radioactive decay.
- You should be able to determine the half-life of a radioactive isotope from given information.
- (HT only) You should be able to calculate the net decline, expressed as a ratio, in a radioactive emission after a given number of half-lives.

#### Explained

Each nucleus has the same chance of decaying in any second, like flipping coins. You cannot predict one coin, but with millions you can predict that about half land heads. That is why half-life is constant for a large sample, even though each decay is random.

To find half-life from a graph of activity against time: read the starting activity, halve it, go across to the curve and down to the time axis. Do it again from a different starting point to check.

#### Worked example

A sample has an activity of 800 Bq. Its half-life is 3 hours. What is its activity after 12 hours?

1. Number of half-lives = 12 ÷ 3 = 4.
2. 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 → 50 Bq.

(HT only) What is the net decline as a ratio after 4 half-lives?

1. After n half-lives the fraction remaining is (½)ⁿ.
2. (½)⁴ = 1/16. The ratio of final to initial activity is 1 : 16.
3. The net decline is 15/16 of the original activity.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the sample is gone after two half-lives. After two half-lives a quarter is still left.

### 4.4.2.4 Radioactive contamination

#### What you need to know

- Radioactive contamination is the unwanted presence of materials containing radioactive atoms on other materials. The hazard from contamination is due to the decay of the contaminating atoms. The type of radiation emitted affects the level of hazard.
- Irradiation is the process of exposing an object to nuclear radiation. The irradiated object does not become radioactive.
- Suitable precautions must be taken to protect against any hazard that the radioactive source used in the process of irradiation may present.
- You should be able to compare the hazards associated with contamination and irradiation.
- It is important for the findings of studies into the effects of radiation on humans to be published and shared with other scientists so that the findings can be checked by peer review.

#### Explained

| | Contamination | Irradiation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What happens | Radioactive material gets onto or into something | Something is exposed to radiation from a source outside it |
| Does the object become radioactive? | Yes, it carries the radioactive atoms | No |
| Does it stop when you move away? | No, it goes with you | Yes, it stops when the source is removed or shielded |
| Most dangerous type | Alpha inside the body (strongly ionising, nothing to stop it) | Gamma and beta from outside (they reach the body; alpha is stopped by skin) |

Precautions: use tongs, keep sources in lead-lined boxes, keep as far away as possible, keep exposure time short, wear gloves and film badges.

Peer review: sharing results lets other scientists check methods and repeat studies, so safety limits are based on reliable evidence.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying irradiated food is radioactive. It is not.

### 4.4.3.1 Background radiation (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- Background radiation is around us all of the time. It comes from:
  - natural sources such as rocks and cosmic rays from space
  - man-made sources such as the fallout from nuclear weapons testing and nuclear accidents.
- The level of background radiation and radiation dose may be affected by occupation and/or location.
- Radiation dose is measured in sieverts (Sv). 1000 millisieverts (mSv) = 1 sievert.

#### Explained

Radon gas from granite rock is the largest natural source in the UK, so people in areas such as Cornwall get a higher dose. Pilots and aircrew get more cosmic radiation because they spend time at high altitude. Medical staff who work with X-rays or radioactive tracers are monitored because of their work.

When measuring a source's count-rate, measure background first and subtract it.

#### Common mistakes

- Thinking most background radiation is man-made. Most is natural.

### 4.4.3.2 Different half-lives of radioactive isotopes (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- Radioactive isotopes have a very wide range of half-life values.
- You should be able to explain why the hazards associated with radioactive material differ according to the half-life involved.

#### Explained

A short half-life source decays quickly, so it is very active at first but soon falls to a safe level. A long half-life source is less active at any moment but stays radioactive for a very long time, so it stays a hazard for years (for example nuclear waste that must be stored for thousands of years).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a long half-life is always more dangerous. It depends on time scale: short half-life gives a high dose quickly; long half-life lasts.

### 4.4.3.3 Uses of nuclear radiation (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- Nuclear radiations are used in medicine for:
  - exploration of internal organs
  - control or destruction of unwanted tissue.
- You should be able to describe and evaluate the uses of nuclear radiations for exploration of internal organs, and for control or destruction of unwanted tissue.
- You should be able to evaluate the perceived risks of using nuclear radiations in relation to given data and consequences.

#### Explained

Tracers: a gamma emitter with a short half-life (hours) is injected or swallowed. Gamma passes out of the body to a camera outside, showing how an organ is working. A short half-life means it does not stay active in the body for long. Alpha would not get out and would damage cells.

Radiotherapy: a beam of gamma rays from outside the body is aimed at a tumour from several directions so the tumour gets a high dose and healthy tissue gets less. Sometimes a source is placed inside or next to the tumour.

Evaluating risk: weigh the benefit (finding or treating a disease) against the risk (cell damage or cancer from ionising radiation). Data such as dose in mSv compared with yearly background dose help decide.

#### Common mistakes

- Choosing an alpha source for a medical tracer. It cannot be detected outside the body and is very damaging inside.

### 4.4.4.1 Nuclear fission (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- Nuclear fission is the splitting of a large and unstable nucleus (for example uranium or plutonium).
- Spontaneous fission is rare. Usually, for fission to occur the unstable nucleus must first absorb a neutron.
- The nucleus undergoing fission splits into two smaller nuclei, roughly equal in size, and emits two or three neutrons plus gamma rays. Energy is released by the fission reaction.
- All of the fission products have kinetic energy.
- The neutrons may go on to start a chain reaction.
- The chain reaction is controlled in a nuclear reactor to control the energy released. The explosion caused by a nuclear weapon is caused by an uncontrolled chain reaction.
- You should be able to draw or interpret diagrams representing nuclear fission and how a chain reaction may occur.

#### Explained

A slow neutron is absorbed by a uranium-235 nucleus. The nucleus becomes unstable and splits into two daughter nuclei, releasing 2 or 3 neutrons and gamma rays. Each new neutron can be absorbed by another uranium nucleus, which splits and releases more neutrons, and so on.

In a reactor, control rods absorb some of the neutrons so that, on average, only one neutron from each fission causes another fission. A moderator slows neutrons down so they can be absorbed. The energy heats water to make steam for turbines.

A chain reaction diagram: one neutron → one nucleus splits → three neutrons → three nuclei split → nine neutrons, and so on.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying fission happens when a nucleus is hit and "smashed". The neutron is absorbed first.

### 4.4.4.2 Nuclear fusion (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- Nuclear fusion is the joining of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus. In this process some of the mass may be converted into the energy of radiation.

#### Explained

In the Sun, hydrogen nuclei fuse to make helium. The helium nucleus has slightly less mass than the nuclei that made it; the missing mass is released as energy. Fusion needs very high temperatures and pressures because the positive nuclei repel each other and must be moving very fast to get close enough to join. This is why fusion power stations are so hard to build.

| | Fission | Fusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What happens | Large nucleus splits | Light nuclei join |
| Fuel | Uranium or plutonium | Hydrogen isotopes |
| Where | Nuclear power stations today | Stars; experimental reactors |

#### Common mistakes

- Confusing the two. Fission is splitting, fusion is joining (think "fuse together").

## 4.3 Particle model of matter

Everything is made of tiny particles. How closely they are packed and how fast they move explain density, changes of state, heating and gas pressure. This topic covers density, internal energy, specific heat capacity, specific latent heat and (Physics only) the pressure and volume of gases.

### Equations for this topic

| Equation | Symbols and units | Recall or given |
| --- | --- | --- |
| density = mass ÷ volume | ρ = m/V (kg/m³, kg, m³) | Recall |
| change in thermal energy = mass × specific heat capacity × temperature change | ΔE = mcΔθ (J, kg, J/kg °C, °C) | Equation sheet |
| energy for a change of state = mass × specific latent heat | E = mL (J, kg, J/kg) | Equation sheet |
| pressure × volume = constant (fixed mass of gas at constant temperature) | pV = constant (Pa, m³) | Equation sheet (Physics only) |

### 4.3.1.1 Density of materials

#### What you need to know

- The density of a material is defined by:

```latex
\rho = \frac{m}{V}
```

- Density ρ in kilograms per metre cubed (kg/m³), mass m in kilograms (kg), volume V in metres cubed (m³). Grams per centimetre cubed (g/cm³) is also used; 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³.
- The particle model can be used to explain the different states of matter and the differences in density.
- You should be able to recognise and draw simple diagrams to model the difference between solids, liquids and gases.
- You should be able to explain the differences in density between the different states of matter in terms of the arrangement of atoms or molecules.

#### Explained

| State | Arrangement | Movement | Density |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Solid | Particles touching, in a regular pattern | Vibrate about fixed positions | High |
| Liquid | Particles touching, but in a random arrangement | Move around each other | High (usually a little less than the solid) |
| Gas | Particles far apart, random | Move fast in all directions | Very low |

In a diagram, draw solid particles as neat rows of touching circles, liquid particles as touching circles in a jumble, and gas particles as a few widely spaced circles.

Gases have low density because the same mass of particles is spread through a much bigger volume.

#### Worked example

A block of aluminium has a mass of 0.54 kg and a volume of 0.000 20 m³. Calculate its density.

1. ρ = 0.54 ÷ 0.000 20 = 2700 kg/m³.

A stone of density 2.5 g/cm³ has a volume of 40 cm³. Calculate its mass.

1. m = ρ × V = 2.5 × 40 = 100 g.

#### Common mistakes

- Mixing units, for example mass in grams with volume in m³. Keep g with cm³ and kg with m³.
- Converting cm³ to m³ by dividing by 100. It is 1 000 000 (1 m³ = 100 × 100 × 100 cm³).

#### Practical

Required practical 5: finding the density of solids and liquids.

Regular solid:

1. Measure its mass with a top-pan balance.
2. Measure its length, width and height with a ruler (or vernier callipers for small objects).
3. Volume = length × width × height. Density = mass ÷ volume.

Irregular solid:

1. Measure its mass with a balance.
2. Fill a displacement (eureka) can with water up to the spout. Wait for it to stop dripping and put a measuring cylinder under the spout.
3. Lower the object in gently on a thread. The volume of water collected equals the volume of the object.
4. Density = mass ÷ volume.

(Instead of a eureka can, you can read a measuring cylinder before and after the object is put in; the rise is the object's volume.)

Liquid:

1. Put an empty measuring cylinder on a balance and zero (tare) it.
2. Pour in a measured volume of liquid, for example 50 cm³, reading from the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
3. Record the mass. Density = mass ÷ volume.

Errors: air bubbles stuck to the object, water splashing out of the eureka can, and reading the meniscus from an angle.

### 4.3.1.2 Changes of state

#### What you need to know

- When substances change state (melt, freeze, boil, evaporate, condense or sublimate), mass is conserved.
- Changes of state are physical changes, which differ from chemical changes because the material recovers its original properties if the change is reversed.
- You should be able to describe how, when substances change state, mass is conserved.

#### Explained

| Change | From | To |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Melting | Solid | Liquid |
| Freezing | Liquid | Solid |
| Boiling or evaporating | Liquid | Gas |
| Condensing | Gas | Liquid |
| Sublimating | Solid | Gas (directly) |

When 100 g of ice melts, you get 100 g of water. The particles are the same; only their arrangement and energy have changed. If you freeze the water again, you get ice with the same properties as before.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling melting a chemical change. No new substance is made.

### 4.3.2.1 Internal energy

#### What you need to know

- Energy is stored inside a system by the particles (atoms and molecules) that make up the system. This is called internal energy.
- Internal energy is the total kinetic energy and potential energy of all the particles that make up a system.
- Heating changes the energy stored within the system by increasing the energy of the particles that make up the system. This either raises the temperature of the system or produces a change of state.

#### Explained

The kinetic energy part comes from the particles moving or vibrating. The potential energy part comes from the forces between particles and how far apart they are. Heating a solid makes its particles vibrate faster (more kinetic energy, higher temperature). When it melts, the extra energy goes into breaking bonds and moving particles apart (more potential energy) instead.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying internal energy is just kinetic energy. It is kinetic plus potential.

### 4.3.2.2 Temperature changes in a system and specific heat capacity

#### What you need to know

- If the temperature of the system increases, the increase in temperature depends on the mass of the substance heated, the type of material, and the energy input to the system.

```latex
\Delta E = m c \Delta\theta
```

- The specific heat capacity of a substance is the amount of energy required to raise the temperature of one kilogram of the substance by one degree Celsius.

#### Explained

This is the same equation as in 4.1.1.3, seen from the particle side: the energy supplied raises the kinetic energy of the particles, and temperature is a measure of their average kinetic energy.

#### Worked example

0.80 kg of water at 15 °C is given 50 400 J of energy. What is its final temperature? (c = 4200 J/kg °C)

1. Δθ = ΔE ÷ (m × c) = 50 400 ÷ (0.80 × 4200) = 50 400 ÷ 3360 = 15 °C.
2. Final temperature = 15 + 15 = 30 °C.

#### Common mistakes

- Giving the temperature change as the final answer when the question asks for the final temperature.

### 4.3.2.3 Changes of state and specific latent heat

#### What you need to know

- If a change of state happens, the energy supplied changes the energy stored (internal energy) but not the temperature.
- The specific latent heat of a substance is the amount of energy required to change the state of one kilogram of the substance with no change in temperature.

```latex
E = m L
```

- Energy E in joules (J), mass m in kilograms (kg), specific latent heat L in joules per kilogram (J/kg).
- Specific latent heat of fusion: change of state from solid to liquid.
- Specific latent heat of vaporisation: change of state from liquid to vapour.
- You should be able to interpret heating and cooling graphs that include changes of state.
- You should be able to distinguish between specific heat capacity and specific latent heat.

#### Explained

A heating graph (temperature against time, steady heating) for ice starting below 0 °C:

1. Sloping line: ice warms up to 0 °C.
2. Flat line at 0 °C: ice melts. Energy goes into breaking bonds, not raising temperature.
3. Sloping line: water warms to 100 °C.
4. Flat line at 100 °C (longer than the melting one): water boils. Vaporisation needs much more energy than melting, because the particles must be pulled fully apart.
5. Sloping line: steam warms up.

A cooling graph is the same shape in reverse; on the flat parts energy is released as bonds form.

| | Specific heat capacity | Specific latent heat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it measures | Energy to raise 1 kg by 1 °C | Energy to change the state of 1 kg |
| Temperature change? | Yes | No |
| Unit | J/kg °C | J/kg |

#### Worked example

How much energy is needed to melt 0.50 kg of ice at 0 °C? (Specific latent heat of fusion of water = 334 000 J/kg)

1. E = 0.50 × 334 000 = 167 000 J.

A kettle supplies 1 130 000 J to boil away water at 100 °C. The specific latent heat of vaporisation of water is 2 260 000 J/kg. What mass of water is turned to steam?

1. m = E ÷ L = 1 130 000 ÷ 2 260 000 = 0.50 kg.

#### Common mistakes

- Using mcΔθ during a change of state. When the temperature is not changing, use E = mL.
- Thinking temperature rises while a substance is boiling. It stays the same until all of it has changed state.

### 4.3.3.1 Particle motion in gases

#### What you need to know

- The molecules of a gas are in constant random motion. The temperature of the gas is related to the average kinetic energy of the molecules.
- Changing the temperature of a gas, held at constant volume, changes the pressure exerted by the gas.
- You should be able to explain how the motion of the molecules in a gas is related to both its temperature and its pressure.
- You should be able to explain qualitatively the relation between the temperature of a gas and its pressure at constant volume.

#### Explained

Gas molecules move fast in all directions and hit the walls of their container. Each collision pushes on the wall. Pressure is the total force of all these collisions on each square metre of wall.

Heat the gas in a sealed, rigid container: the molecules gain kinetic energy and move faster. They hit the walls more often and harder, so the pressure rises.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the molecules get bigger or expand when heated. The molecules stay the same size; they move faster.

### 4.3.3.2 Pressure in gases (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- A gas can be compressed or expanded by pressure changes. The pressure produces a net force at right angles to the wall of the gas container (or any surface).
- You should be able to use the particle model to explain how increasing the volume in which a gas is contained, at constant temperature, can lead to a decrease in pressure.
- For a fixed mass of gas held at a constant temperature:

```latex
p V = \text{constant}
```

- Pressure p in pascals (Pa), volume V in metres cubed (m³). This equation is on the equation sheet.
- You should be able to calculate the change in the pressure of a gas or the volume of a gas (a fixed mass held at constant temperature) when either the pressure or volume is increased or decreased.

#### Explained

If the same molecules have a bigger space to move in at the same speed, they hit each part of the wall less often. Fewer collisions per second on each square metre means lower pressure. Halve the volume and the pressure doubles; double the volume and the pressure halves.

Because pV is constant, you can write p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ for the before and after states.

#### Worked example

A gas has a volume of 0.060 m³ at a pressure of 100 000 Pa. It is compressed at constant temperature to 0.020 m³. Calculate the new pressure.

1. p₁V₁ = 100 000 × 0.060 = 6000.
2. p₂ = 6000 ÷ 0.020 = 300 000 Pa.

#### Common mistakes

- Using pV = constant when the temperature changes. It only works at constant temperature.
- Mixing units on the two sides. Any volume unit works as long as both volumes use the same one.

### 4.3.3.3 Increasing the pressure of a gas (Physics only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Work is the transfer of energy by a force.
- Doing work on a gas increases the internal energy of the gas and can cause an increase in the temperature of the gas.
- You should be able to explain how, in a given situation (for example a bicycle pump), doing work on an enclosed gas leads to an increase in the temperature of the gas.

#### Explained

When you push a bike pump handle in, you apply a force over a distance, so you do work on the gas. The piston moves towards the molecules, so molecules bounce off it faster than they hit it. Their kinetic energy goes up, so the internal energy and temperature of the gas rise. That is why the pump barrel feels warm after a few strokes.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the pump gets warm only because of friction. Friction adds a little, but the main reason is the work done on the gas.

## 4.5 Forces

Forces change how things move and change their shape. This topic covers scalars and vectors, weight, resultant forces, work, springs, (Physics only) moments and fluid pressure, motion graphs, Newton's laws, stopping distances and (HT only) momentum.

### 4.5.1.1 Scalar and vector quantities

#### What you need to know

- Scalar quantities have magnitude only.
- Vector quantities have magnitude and an associated direction.
- A vector quantity may be represented by an arrow. The length of the arrow represents the magnitude, and the direction of the arrow the direction of the vector quantity.

#### Explained

| Scalars | Vectors |
| --- | --- |
| Distance, speed, mass, time, energy, temperature | Displacement, velocity, force, weight, acceleration, momentum |

"20 m/s" is a speed. "20 m/s north" is a velocity.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling weight a scalar. It is a force, so it is a vector (it always acts downwards).

### 4.5.1.2 Contact and non-contact forces

#### What you need to know

- A force is a push or pull that acts on an object due to the interaction with another object.
- All forces between objects are either contact forces (the objects are physically touching) or non-contact forces (the objects are physically separated).
- Examples of contact forces include friction, air resistance, tension and normal contact force.
- Examples of non-contact forces are gravitational force, electrostatic force and magnetic force.
- Force is a vector quantity.
- You should be able to describe the interaction between pairs of objects which produce a force on each object, and represent such forces as vectors.

#### Explained

Forces always come from an interaction between two objects. A book on a table pushes down on the table, and the table pushes up on the book (normal contact force). The Earth pulls the book down, and the book pulls the Earth up (gravity, non-contact).

#### Common mistakes

- Listing weight as a contact force. Gravity acts without touching.

### 4.5.1.3 Gravity

#### What you need to know

- Weight is the force acting on an object due to gravity. The force of gravity close to the Earth is due to the gravitational field around the Earth.
- The weight of an object depends on the gravitational field strength at the point where the object is.

```latex
W = m g
```

- Weight W in newtons (N), mass m in kilograms (kg), gravitational field strength g in newtons per kilogram (N/kg). In calculations, g on Earth is taken as 9.8 N/kg unless told otherwise.
- The weight of an object may be considered to act at a single point referred to as the object's centre of mass.
- The weight of an object and the mass of an object are directly proportional.
- Weight is measured using a calibrated spring-balance (a newtonmeter).

#### Worked example

What is the weight of a 65 kg astronaut on the Moon, where g = 1.6 N/kg?

1. W = 65 × 1.6 = 104 N.

The astronaut's mass is still 65 kg; only the weight changes.

#### Common mistakes

- Giving weight in kilograms. Weight is a force, measured in newtons.

### 4.5.1.4 Resultant forces

#### What you need to know

- A number of forces acting on an object may be replaced by a single force that has the same effect as all the original forces acting together. This single force is called the resultant force.
- You should be able to calculate the resultant of two forces that act in a straight line.
- (HT only) You should be able to describe examples of the forces acting on an isolated object or system, and use free body diagrams to describe qualitatively examples where several forces lead to a resultant force on an object, including balanced forces when the resultant force is zero.
- (HT only) A single force can be resolved into two components acting at right angles to each other. The two component forces together have the same effect as the single force.
- (HT only) You should be able to use vector diagrams to illustrate resolution of forces, equilibrium situations, and determine the resultant of two forces, to include both magnitude and direction (scale drawings only).

#### Explained

Same direction: add. Opposite directions: subtract, and the resultant points the way of the bigger force.

(HT only) A free body diagram shows one object as a dot or box with an arrow for every force acting on it, each starting from the object.

(HT only) For two forces at an angle, draw a scale drawing: draw the first force as an arrow to scale, then draw the second starting from the tip of the first. The resultant is the arrow from the start of the first to the tip of the second. Measure its length and angle.

(HT only) Resolving: one force can be split into a horizontal part and a vertical part by drawing it as the diagonal of a rectangle to scale and measuring the two sides.

(HT only) If the forces drawn tip to tail make a closed shape, the resultant is zero and the object is in equilibrium.

#### Worked example

A box is pushed right with 50 N; friction acts left with 20 N. Resultant = 50 − 20 = 30 N to the right.

(HT only) Two tugs pull a ship: 3.0 kN east and 4.0 kN north. Scale 1 cm = 1 kN. Drawing them tip to tail gives a resultant arrow 5.0 cm long at about 53° north of east, so the resultant is 5.0 kN in that direction.

#### Common mistakes

- Adding forces that act in opposite directions.
- (HT only) Drawing vectors from the same point rather than tip to tail.

### 4.5.2 Work done and energy transfer

#### What you need to know

- When a force causes an object to move through a distance, work is done on the object. So a force does work on an object when the force causes a displacement of the object.

```latex
W = F s
```

- Work done W in joules (J), force F in newtons (N), distance s in metres (m), measured along the line of action of the force.
- One joule of work is done when a force of one newton causes a displacement of one metre: 1 joule = 1 newton-metre.
- You should be able to describe the energy transfer involved when work is done, and convert between newton-metres and joules.
- Work done against the frictional forces acting on an object causes a rise in the temperature of the object.

#### Worked example

A cyclist pushes a bike 40 m along a path with a steady force of 25 N. Work done = 25 × 40 = 1000 J (1000 N m).

#### Common mistakes

- Using the total distance travelled when the force acts at an angle. Only the distance in the direction of the force counts.

### 4.5.3 Forces and elasticity

#### What you need to know

- You should be able to give examples of the forces involved in stretching, bending or compressing an object.
- You should be able to explain why, to change the shape of an object (by stretching, bending or compressing), more than one force has to be applied (this is limited to stationary objects only).
- You should be able to describe the difference between elastic deformation and inelastic deformation caused by stretching forces.
- The extension of an elastic object, such as a spring, is directly proportional to the force applied, provided that the limit of proportionality is not exceeded.

```latex
F = k e
```

- Force F in newtons (N), spring constant k in newtons per metre (N/m), extension e in metres (m).
- This relationship also applies to the compression of an elastic object, where e is the compression.
- A force that stretches (or compresses) a spring does work and elastic potential energy is stored in the spring. Provided the spring is not inelastically deformed, the work done on the spring and the elastic potential energy stored are equal.
- You should be able to describe the difference between a linear and non-linear relationship between force and extension, and calculate a spring constant in linear cases.
- You should be able to interpret data from an investigation of the relationship between force and extension.
- You should be able to calculate work done in stretching (or compressing) a spring (up to the limit of proportionality) using Ee = ½ke².

#### Explained

To stretch a spring that hangs still, it must be pulled at both ends: the weight pulls down and the clamp pulls up. If only one force acted, the spring would just move, not change shape.

Elastic deformation: the object goes back to its original shape when the force is removed. Inelastic deformation: it stays permanently stretched or bent.

On a force–extension graph, the straight part through the origin is linear; the gradient is the spring constant. Past the limit of proportionality the line curves (non-linear). The area under the linear part is the energy stored.

#### Worked example

A spring stretches by 0.040 m when a 2.0 N weight is hung on it. Calculate the spring constant.

1. k = F ÷ e = 2.0 ÷ 0.040 = 50 N/m.

How much energy is stored when the same spring is stretched by 0.060 m?

1. Ee = ½ × 50 × 0.060² = 0.5 × 50 × 0.0036 = 0.090 J.

#### Common mistakes

- Using the total length instead of the extension. Extension = stretched length − original length.
- Leaving extension in cm.

#### Practical

Required practical 6: force and extension of a spring.

1. Hang a spring from a clamp stand. Clamp a metre ruler vertically beside it, with zero level with the bottom of the spring. Fix a pointer to the bottom of the spring.
2. Record the starting position of the pointer.
3. Hang a mass hanger (for example 1 N) on the spring. Read the new position of the pointer at eye level. Extension = new reading − starting reading.
4. Add masses one at a time, recording the total weight and extension each time.
5. Plot force (y-axis) against extension (x-axis). The straight part shows force is proportional to extension; its gradient is the spring constant.

Check for elastic behaviour by removing the masses and seeing if the spring goes back to its original length.

Safety: wear eye protection in case the spring snaps; put a tray or cushion under the masses and clamp the stand to the bench so it does not tip.

### 4.5.4 Moments, levers and gears (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- A force or a system of forces may cause an object to rotate.
- You should be able to describe examples in which forces cause rotation.
- The turning effect of a force is called the moment of the force.

```latex
M = F d
```

- Moment M in newton-metres (N m), force F in newtons (N), d is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force, in metres (m).
- If an object is balanced, the total clockwise moment about a pivot equals the total anticlockwise moment about that pivot.
- You should be able to calculate the size of a force, or its distance from a pivot, acting on an object that is balanced.
- A simple lever and a simple gear system can both be used to transmit the rotational effects of forces.
- You should be able to explain how levers and gears transmit the rotational effects of forces.

#### Explained

A longer spanner makes it easier to undo a nut, because the same force at a greater distance gives a bigger moment. A lever uses this: a small force far from the pivot can balance a large force close to it.

Gears: a small gear driving a larger gear makes the larger gear turn more slowly but with a bigger moment (good for climbing hills on a bike in a low gear). A large gear driving a small one makes the small gear turn faster with a smaller moment. Neighbouring gears turn in opposite directions.

#### Worked example

A seesaw is balanced. A 400 N child sits 1.5 m from the pivot. Where must a 600 N adult sit on the other side?

1. Anticlockwise moment = 400 × 1.5 = 600 N m.
2. Clockwise moment must equal 600 N m: 600 × d = 600.
3. d = 1.0 m.

#### Common mistakes

- Using the distance along a slanting bar rather than the perpendicular distance.
- Giving the unit of moment as joules. Use N m.

### 4.5.5.1.1 Pressure in a fluid 1 (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- A fluid can be either a liquid or a gas.
- The pressure in fluids causes a force normal (at right angles) to any surface.

```latex
p = \frac{F}{A}
```

- Pressure p in pascals (Pa), force F in newtons (N), area A in metres squared (m²). 1 Pa = 1 N/m².

#### Worked example

A box weighing 1200 N rests on a base of area 0.40 m². Pressure = 1200 ÷ 0.40 = 3000 Pa.

#### Common mistakes

- Converting cm² to m² by dividing by 100. Divide by 10 000.

### 4.5.5.1.2 Pressure in a fluid 2 (Physics only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- The pressure due to a column of liquid can be calculated:

```latex
p = h \rho g
```

- Pressure p in pascals (Pa), height of the column h in metres (m), density ρ in kilograms per metre cubed (kg/m³), g in newtons per kilogram (N/kg). This equation is on the equation sheet.
- You should be able to explain why, in a liquid, pressure at a point increases with the height of the column of liquid above that point and with the density of the liquid.
- A partially (or totally) submerged object experiences a greater pressure on the bottom surface than on the top surface. This creates a resultant force upwards. This force is called the upthrust.
- You should be able to describe the factors which influence floating and sinking.

#### Explained

The deeper you go, the more liquid is above you pressing down, so pressure increases with depth. A denser liquid has more mass (and weight) in the same column, so it presses harder.

The bottom of a submerged object is deeper than its top, so the upward push on the bottom is bigger than the downward push on the top. The difference is upthrust.

Floating and sinking: an object floats if it can displace a weight of fluid equal to its own weight before it is fully submerged, which happens if it is less dense than the fluid. If it is denser than the fluid, its weight is greater than the largest upthrust it can get, so it sinks.

#### Worked example

Calculate the pressure due to the water 12 m below the surface of a lake. (Density of water = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.8 N/kg)

1. p = 12 × 1000 × 9.8 = 117 600 Pa (about 118 kPa).

(This is the pressure from the water only; the atmosphere adds about 100 kPa more.)

#### Common mistakes

- Using the total depth of the container rather than the depth of the point below the surface.

### 4.5.5.2 Atmospheric pressure (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- The atmosphere is a thin layer (relative to the size of the Earth) of air round the Earth. The atmosphere gets less dense with increasing altitude.
- Air molecules colliding with a surface create atmospheric pressure. The number of air molecules (and so the weight of air) above a surface decreases as the height of the surface above ground level increases. So as height increases there is always less air above a surface than there is at a lower height. This means atmospheric pressure decreases with an increase in height.
- You should be able to describe a simple model of the Earth's atmosphere and of atmospheric pressure, and explain why atmospheric pressure varies with height above a surface.

#### Explained

At the top of a mountain there is less air above you and the air is less dense, so fewer molecules hit each square metre each second. That is why your ears pop on a plane and why a sealed crisp packet swells at altitude.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying pressure drops with height "because there is less gravity". The main reason is less air above and fewer collisions.

### 4.5.6.1.1 Distance and displacement

#### What you need to know

- Distance is how far an object moves. Distance does not involve direction. Distance is a scalar quantity.
- Displacement includes both the distance an object moves, measured in a straight line from the start point to the finish point, and the direction of that straight line. Displacement is a vector quantity.
- You should be able to express a displacement in terms of both the magnitude and direction.

#### Worked example

A runner goes once round a 400 m track and stops where they started. Distance = 400 m. Displacement = 0 m.

A walker goes 3 km north and then 4 km east. Distance = 7 km. Displacement = 5 km (from Pythagoras) on a bearing of about 053°.

### 4.5.6.1.2 Speed

#### What you need to know

- Speed does not involve direction. Speed is a scalar quantity.
- The speed of a moving object is rarely constant. When people walk, run or travel in a car their speed is constantly changing.
- The speed that a person can walk, run or cycle depends on many factors including age, terrain, fitness and distance travelled.
- Typical values: walking about 1.5 m/s, running about 3 m/s, cycling about 6 m/s.
- It is not only moving objects that have varying speed. The speed of sound and the speed of the wind also vary. A typical value for the speed of sound in air is 330 m/s.
- You should be able to recall typical values of speed for a person walking, running and cycling as well as the typical values of speed for different types of transportation systems.
- You should be able to make measurements of distance and time and then calculate speeds of objects.

```latex
s = v t
```

- Distance s in metres (m), speed v in metres per second (m/s), time t in seconds (s).
- You should be able to calculate average speed for non-uniform motion.

#### Explained

Typical speeds of transport: car in town about 13 m/s (30 mph), car on a motorway about 31 m/s (70 mph), train up to about 55 m/s, plane about 250 m/s.

#### Worked example

A train travels 180 km in 1.5 hours. Calculate its average speed in m/s.

1. Distance = 180 000 m; time = 1.5 × 3600 = 5400 s.
2. v = 180 000 ÷ 5400 = 33 m/s.

### 4.5.6.1.3 Velocity

#### What you need to know

- The velocity of an object is its speed in a given direction. Velocity is a vector quantity.
- (HT only) You should be able to explain the vector–scalar distinction as it applies to displacement, distance, velocity and speed.
- (HT only) You should be able to explain qualitatively, with examples, that motion in a circle involves constant speed but changing velocity.

#### Explained

(HT only) A car driving round a roundabout at a steady 10 m/s has a constant speed, but its direction keeps changing, so its velocity keeps changing. A changing velocity means it is accelerating, so there must be a resultant force, which points towards the centre of the circle.

### 4.5.6.1.4 The distance–time relationship

#### What you need to know

- If an object moves along a straight line, the distance travelled can be represented by a distance–time graph.
- The speed of an object can be calculated from the gradient of its distance–time graph.
- (HT only) If an object is accelerating, its speed at any particular time can be found by drawing a tangent and measuring the gradient of the distance–time graph at that time.
- You should be able to draw distance–time graphs from measurements and extract and interpret lines and slopes of distance–time graphs, translating information between graphical and numerical form.

#### Explained

| Shape of line | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Horizontal | Stationary |
| Straight sloping line | Constant speed |
| Steeper straight line | Faster constant speed |
| Curve getting steeper | Accelerating |
| Curve getting less steep | Decelerating |

#### Worked example

A distance–time graph is a straight line from (0 s, 0 m) to (20 s, 100 m). Speed = gradient = 100 ÷ 20 = 5.0 m/s.

(HT only) For a curve, draw a straight tangent touching the curve at the time you want. Pick two points far apart on the tangent, for example (4 s, 6 m) and (10 s, 30 m). Speed = (30 − 6) ÷ (10 − 4) = 24 ÷ 6 = 4.0 m/s.

### 4.5.6.1.5 Acceleration

#### What you need to know

- The average acceleration of an object can be calculated:

```latex
a = \frac{\Delta v}{t}
```

- Acceleration a in metres per second squared (m/s²), change in velocity Δv in metres per second (m/s), time t in seconds (s).
- An object that slows down is decelerating.
- You should be able to estimate the magnitude of everyday accelerations.
- The acceleration of an object can be calculated from the gradient of a velocity–time graph.
- (HT only) The distance travelled by an object (or displacement of an object) can be calculated from the area under a velocity–time graph.
- You should be able to draw velocity–time graphs from measurements and interpret lines and slopes to determine acceleration; (HT only) interpret enclosed areas in velocity–time graphs to determine distance travelled (or displacement); (HT only) measure, when appropriate, the area under a velocity–time graph by counting squares.
- The following equation applies to uniform acceleration:

```latex
v^2 - u^2 = 2 a s
```

- Final velocity v and initial velocity u in m/s, acceleration a in m/s², distance s in m. This equation is on the equation sheet.
- Near the Earth's surface any object falling freely under gravity has an acceleration of about 9.8 m/s².
- An object falling through a fluid initially accelerates due to the force of gravity. Eventually the resultant force will be zero and the object will move at its terminal velocity.
- (Physics only) You should be able to draw and interpret velocity–time graphs for objects that reach terminal velocity, and interpret the changing motion in terms of the forces acting.

#### Explained

On a velocity–time graph: horizontal line = constant velocity; straight line going up = constant acceleration; steeper = bigger acceleration; line going down = deceleration.

Everyday accelerations: a car pulling away about 2–3 m/s²; a sprinter at the start about 5 m/s²; free fall 9.8 m/s².

Terminal velocity of a skydiver:

1. Just after jumping: weight is much bigger than air resistance, so there is a large resultant force down and the skydiver accelerates at nearly 9.8 m/s².
2. As speed rises, air resistance grows, so the resultant force and acceleration get smaller.
3. Eventually air resistance equals weight. Resultant force is zero, so the speed is constant: terminal velocity.
4. When the parachute opens, the area and air resistance increase suddenly. Air resistance is now bigger than weight, so the skydiver decelerates.
5. As the speed drops, air resistance falls until it equals weight again, giving a new, lower terminal velocity.

(Physics only) On the velocity–time graph this shows as a curve that rises and flattens off, a sudden drop when the parachute opens, then a flattening to a lower horizontal line.

#### Worked example

A car speeds up from 8 m/s to 20 m/s in 4.0 s. Calculate its acceleration.

1. Δv = 20 − 8 = 12 m/s.
2. a = 12 ÷ 4.0 = 3.0 m/s².

A ball is dropped from rest and falls 20 m. Use a = 9.8 m/s² and ignore air resistance. Calculate its final speed.

1. v² − 0² = 2 × 9.8 × 20 = 392.
2. v = √392 = 19.8 m/s.

(HT only) A velocity–time graph rises in a straight line from 0 to 10 m/s in 5 s, then is flat at 10 m/s for 10 s. Distance = area under the line = (½ × 5 × 10) + (10 × 10) = 25 + 100 = 125 m.

#### Common mistakes

- Using final velocity instead of change in velocity.
- Forgetting to square root at the end of a v² − u² = 2as calculation.
- Reading the gradient of a velocity–time graph as speed. It is acceleration.

### 4.5.6.2.1 Newton's First Law

#### What you need to know

- If the resultant force acting on an object is zero and the object is stationary, the object remains stationary. If the object is moving, it continues to move at the same speed and in the same direction, so its velocity stays the same.
- So when a vehicle travels at a steady speed the resistive forces balance the driving force.
- So the velocity (speed and/or direction) of an object will only change if a resultant force is acting on the object.
- You should be able to apply Newton's First Law to explain the motion of objects moving with a uniform velocity and objects where the speed and/or direction changes.
- (HT only) The tendency of objects to continue in their state of rest or of uniform motion is called inertia.

#### Explained

A hockey puck on ice keeps sliding because there is almost no friction to stop it. A car at a steady 30 m/s on a flat road has a driving force exactly matching drag plus friction. If the driving force rises, there is a resultant force forwards and the car speeds up.

### 4.5.6.2.2 Newton's Second Law

#### What you need to know

- The acceleration of an object is proportional to the resultant force acting on the object, and inversely proportional to the mass of the object.

```latex
F = m a
```

- Resultant force F in newtons (N), mass m in kilograms (kg), acceleration a in metres per second squared (m/s²).
- You should be able to estimate the speed, accelerations and forces involved in large accelerations for everyday road transport.
- You should recognise and be able to use the symbol that indicates an approximate value or approximate answer, ~.
- (HT only) You should be able to explain that inertial mass is a measure of how difficult it is to change the velocity of an object. Inertial mass is defined as the ratio of force over acceleration.

#### Worked example

A 900 kg car has a driving force of 3000 N and a total resistive force of 600 N. Calculate its acceleration.

1. Resultant force = 3000 − 600 = 2400 N.
2. a = F ÷ m = 2400 ÷ 900 = 2.7 m/s².

Estimate: a car (~1000 kg) braking hard from ~30 m/s to rest in ~5 s has a ≈ 6 m/s², so the braking force is ~6000 N.

#### Common mistakes

- Using the driving force instead of the resultant force.

#### Practical

Required practical 7: investigating the effect of force and mass on acceleration.

1. Put a trolley on a flat bench. Tie a string to the trolley, pass it over a pulley at the end of the bench and hang a mass hanger on the end.
2. Mark lines at equal distances along the bench (or use light gates).
3. Release the trolley. The weight of the hanging masses is the accelerating force.
4. Measure the time to pass each line, or use light gates to get the speed at two points, and work out the acceleration.
5. To change the force but keep the mass of the system constant, move masses from the trolley to the hanger one at a time.
6. To change the mass at constant force, keep the hanging mass the same and add masses to the trolley.

Results: acceleration is proportional to force (straight line through the origin on an a–F graph) and gets smaller as mass increases.

Errors: friction (tilt the bench slightly to compensate), and human reaction time with a stopwatch (light gates reduce this).

Safety: put a soft landing under the hanging masses and stop the trolley before it falls off the bench.

### 4.5.6.2.3 Newton's Third Law

#### What you need to know

- Whenever two objects interact, the forces they exert on each other are equal and opposite.
- You should be able to apply Newton's Third Law to examples of equilibrium situations.

#### Explained

A swimmer pushes water backwards; the water pushes the swimmer forwards with an equal force. The two forces act on different objects, are the same type of force, and are equal in size and opposite in direction.

A book resting on a table is in equilibrium because its weight and the table's upward push balance, but that pair is not a Third Law pair (both act on the book, and they are different types of force). The Third Law partner of the book's weight is the book pulling the Earth upwards.

#### Common mistakes

- Picking two forces acting on the same object as a Third Law pair.

### 4.5.6.3.1 Stopping distance

#### What you need to know

- The stopping distance of a vehicle is the sum of the distance the vehicle travels during the driver's reaction time (thinking distance) and the distance it travels under the braking force (braking distance).
- For a given braking force, the greater the speed of the vehicle, the greater the stopping distance.
- (Physics only) You should be able to estimate how the distance for a vehicle to make an emergency stop varies over a range of speeds typical for that vehicle.
- (Physics only) You should be able to interpret graphs relating speed to stopping distance for a range of vehicles.

#### Explained

Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance.

(Physics only) Thinking distance is proportional to speed (double the speed, double the thinking distance). Braking distance rises much faster, roughly with the square of speed (double the speed, about four times the braking distance), because the kinetic energy to be removed depends on v². So a car at 30 mph stops in about 23 m, but at 70 mph it needs about 96 m. On a speed–stopping distance graph the line curves upwards; a heavier vehicle with the same braking force has a higher curve.

### 4.5.6.3.2 Reaction time

#### What you need to know

- Reaction times vary from person to person. Typical values range from 0.2 s to 0.9 s.
- A driver's reaction time can be affected by tiredness, drugs and alcohol. Distractions may also affect a driver's ability to react.
- You should be able to explain methods used to measure human reaction times and recall typical results, interpret and evaluate measurements from simple methods, and evaluate the effect of various factors on thinking distance based on given data.

#### Explained

Ruler drop test: a partner holds a ruler vertically with the zero between your open finger and thumb, then drops it without warning. You catch it. The further it falls, the longer your reaction time. A computer test where you click when the screen changes is more precise.

#### Worked example

A driver's reaction time is 0.6 s at 25 m/s. Thinking distance = 25 × 0.6 = 15 m. If tiredness raises the reaction time to 0.9 s, thinking distance = 25 × 0.9 = 22.5 m.

### 4.5.6.3.3 Factors affecting braking distance 1

#### What you need to know

- The braking distance of a vehicle can be affected by adverse road and weather conditions and poor condition of the vehicle.
- Adverse road conditions include wet or icy conditions. Poor condition of the vehicle is limited to the vehicle's brakes or tyres.
- You should be able to explain the factors which affect the distance required for road transport vehicles to come to rest in emergencies, and the implications for safety, and estimate how the distance required for road vehicles to stop in an emergency varies over a range of typical speeds.

#### Explained

Wet or icy roads and worn tyres reduce friction between the tyres and the road, so the maximum braking force is smaller and the car takes further to stop (it may skid). Worn brakes give a smaller braking force. Speed, mass of the vehicle, and these conditions all affect braking distance. Tiredness, alcohol and distractions affect thinking distance, not braking distance.

### 4.5.6.3.4 Factors affecting braking distance 2

#### What you need to know

- When a force is applied to the brakes of a vehicle, work done by the friction force between the brakes and the wheel reduces the kinetic energy of the vehicle and the temperature of the brakes increases.
- The greater the speed of a vehicle, the greater the braking force needed to stop the vehicle in a certain distance.
- The greater the braking force, the greater the deceleration of the vehicle. Large decelerations may lead to brakes overheating and/or loss of control.
- You should be able to explain the dangers caused by large decelerations.
- (HT only) You should be able to estimate the forces involved in the deceleration of road vehicles in typical situations on a public road.

#### Explained

Work done by brakes = kinetic energy removed, so F × d = ½mv². Large decelerations are dangerous because the brakes can overheat and fail, the tyres can skid, and the people inside feel large forces.

#### Worked example

(HT only) Estimate the braking force for a car (~1000 kg) stopping from 20 m/s in 40 m.

1. Kinetic energy = ½ × 1000 × 20² = 200 000 J.
2. F = 200 000 ÷ 40 = 5000 N (about 5 kN).

### 4.5.7.1 Momentum is a property of moving objects (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Momentum is defined by:

```latex
p = m v
```

- Momentum p in kilogram metres per second (kg m/s), mass m in kilograms (kg), velocity v in metres per second (m/s).

#### Worked example

A 0.16 kg cricket ball moves at 30 m/s. p = 0.16 × 30 = 4.8 kg m/s.

#### Common mistakes

- Forgetting direction. Momentum is a vector; in collision questions take one direction as positive and the other as negative.

### 4.5.7.2 Conservation of momentum (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- In a closed system, the total momentum before an event is equal to the total momentum after the event. This is called conservation of momentum.
- You should be able to use the concept of momentum as a model to describe and explain examples of momentum in an event, such as a collision.
- (Physics only) You should be able to complete calculations involving an event, such as the collision of two objects.

#### Explained

In an explosion (such as a gun firing or two skaters pushing apart), the total momentum before is zero, so afterwards the two parts move apart with equal and opposite momenta.

#### Worked example

(Physics only) A 2.0 kg trolley at 3.0 m/s hits a stationary 1.0 kg trolley and they stick together. Find their velocity after.

1. Momentum before = 2.0 × 3.0 + 1.0 × 0 = 6.0 kg m/s.
2. Momentum after = (2.0 + 1.0) × v = 3.0v.
3. 3.0v = 6.0, so v = 2.0 m/s in the original direction.

### 4.5.7.3 Changes in momentum (Physics only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- When a force acts on an object that is moving, or able to move, a change in momentum occurs.
- The equations F = ma and a = (v − u)/t combine to give:

```latex
F = \frac{m \Delta v}{\Delta t}
```

- where mΔv is the change in momentum. This equation is on the equation sheet. So force equals the rate of change of momentum.
- You should be able to explain safety features such as air bags, seat belts, gymnasium crash mats, cycle helmets and cushioned surfaces for playgrounds with reference to the concept of rate of change of momentum.
- You should be able to apply equations relating force, mass, velocity and acceleration to explain how the changes involved are inter-related.

#### Explained

In a crash your momentum has to drop to zero. If it happens over a longer time, the rate of change of momentum is smaller, so the force on you is smaller. Air bags, crumple zones, seat belts that stretch a little, crash mats and soft playground surfaces all increase the time of the impact.

#### Worked example

A 70 kg person moving at 15 m/s is stopped by an air bag in 0.20 s. Calculate the average force.

1. Change in momentum = 70 × 15 = 1050 kg m/s.
2. F = 1050 ÷ 0.20 = 5250 N.

Without the air bag the stop might take 0.02 s, giving 52 500 N, ten times larger.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying an air bag "reduces the change in momentum". The change is the same; the time is longer, so the force is smaller.

## 4.1 Energy

Energy is a way of keeping track of what can happen in a system. It is never made or destroyed; it moves between stores. This topic covers energy stores, the equations for kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic and thermal energy, power, efficiency, and the ways we generate electricity.

### Equations for this topic

| Equation | Symbols and units | Recall or given |
| --- | --- | --- |
| kinetic energy = 0.5 × mass × speed² | Ek = ½mv² (J, kg, m/s) | Recall |
| gravitational potential energy = mass × gravitational field strength × height | Ep = mgh (J, kg, N/kg, m) | Recall |
| elastic potential energy = 0.5 × spring constant × extension² | Ee = ½ke² (J, N/m, m) | Equation sheet |
| change in thermal energy = mass × specific heat capacity × temperature change | ΔE = mcΔθ (J, kg, J/kg °C, °C) | Equation sheet |
| power = energy transferred ÷ time | P = E/t (W, J, s) | Recall |
| power = work done ÷ time | P = W/t (W, J, s) | Recall |
| efficiency = useful output energy transfer ÷ total input energy transfer | no units (decimal or %) | Recall |
| efficiency = useful power output ÷ total power input | no units (decimal or %) | Recall |

### 4.1.1.1 Energy stores and systems

#### What you need to know

- A system is an object or a group of objects that you choose to study.
- When a system changes, the energy in its stores changes. The main stores are kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic potential, thermal (internal), chemical, magnetic, electrostatic and nuclear.
- Energy can be moved between stores by heating, by a force doing work (mechanical work), by an electric current doing work, and by waves (such as light and sound).
- You should be able to describe the changes in the way energy is stored when a system changes, for example:
  - a ball thrown upwards
  - a moving object hitting an obstacle
  - an object sped up by a constant force
  - a vehicle slowing down
  - water being brought to the boil in an electric kettle.
- You should be able to calculate the changes in energy involved when a system changes, using the equations in this topic.

#### Explained

Think of energy like money held in different accounts. The total never changes; it moves from one account to another. Physicists call the accounts stores and the ways money moves between them pathways.

| Change | Store that empties | Store that fills | Pathway |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ball thrown upwards (going up) | Kinetic store of the ball | Gravitational potential store of the ball | Work done by gravity (the weight acts against the motion) |
| Car hits a wall | Kinetic store of the car | Thermal stores of car, wall and air; some sound | Work done by contact forces; sound waves |
| Trolley pushed by a constant force | Chemical store of the person (or elastic store of a stretched band) | Kinetic store of the trolley | Work done by the force |
| Car braking | Kinetic store of the car | Thermal store of the brakes and surroundings | Work done by friction |
| Electric kettle | Chemical or nuclear store at the power station | Thermal store of the water | Electrical work, then heating |

A closed system is one where no energy enters or leaves. The total energy in a closed system stays the same, even though it may move between stores inside it.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling heat, light or sound a store. They are ways energy is transferred, not places it is kept. Use "thermal store" not "heat energy".
- Forgetting the surroundings. In almost every real change some energy ends up in the thermal store of the surroundings.

### 4.1.1.2 Changes in energy

#### What you need to know

- You should be able to calculate the energy in the kinetic store of a moving object:

```latex
E_k = \frac{1}{2} m v^2
```

- You should be able to calculate the energy in the gravitational potential store of an object raised above the ground:

```latex
E_p = m g h
```

- You should be able to calculate the energy in the elastic potential store of a stretched spring, as long as the limit of proportionality is not exceeded (the equation is on the equation sheet):

```latex
E_e = \frac{1}{2} k e^2
```

- Units: energy in joules (J), mass in kilograms (kg), speed in metres per second (m/s), gravitational field strength in newtons per kilogram (N/kg), height in metres (m), spring constant in newtons per metre (N/m), extension in metres (m).
- On Earth, g is about 9.8 N/kg. Questions will tell you which value to use.

#### Explained

Kinetic energy depends on the square of speed. Double the speed and the kinetic energy goes up four times. That is why a small rise in speed makes a big difference in a crash.

Gravitational potential energy is the energy an object gains when it is lifted against gravity. It depends on how high it is lifted (the change in height), not how high it is above sea level.

For a falling object with no air resistance, every joule lost from the gravitational store goes into the kinetic store, so you can write mgh = ½mv². The mass cancels, which is why a heavy and a light ball dropped together land at the same time if air resistance is small.

#### Worked example

A 1200 kg car moves at 15 m/s. Calculate the energy in its kinetic store.

1. Ek = ½ × 1200 × 15².
2. 15² = 225, so Ek = 0.5 × 1200 × 225 = 135 000 J (135 kJ).

A 0.40 kg ball is dropped from a height of 5.0 m. Use g = 9.8 N/kg. Ignore air resistance. Calculate its speed just before it hits the ground.

1. Energy lost from gravitational store: Ep = 0.40 × 9.8 × 5.0 = 19.6 J.
2. All of this goes to the kinetic store: 19.6 = ½ × 0.40 × v².
3. v² = 19.6 ÷ 0.20 = 98.
4. v = √98 = 9.9 m/s.

A spring with spring constant 40 N/m is stretched by 15 cm. Calculate the energy in its elastic store.

1. Convert: 15 cm = 0.15 m.
2. Ee = ½ × 40 × 0.15² = 0.5 × 40 × 0.0225 = 0.45 J.

#### Common mistakes

- Squaring the whole of ½mv instead of only v.
- Leaving extension or height in centimetres. Convert to metres first.
- Using grams in Ek or Ep. Mass must be in kilograms.
- When rearranging Ek = ½mv² for speed, forgetting to take the square root at the end.

### 4.1.1.3 Energy changes in systems

#### What you need to know

- The amount of energy needed to change the temperature of a substance, or released when it cools, depends on its mass, the material, and the temperature change.

```latex
\Delta E = m c \Delta\theta
```

- ΔE is the change in thermal energy in joules (J), m is mass in kg, c is specific heat capacity in joules per kilogram per degree Celsius (J/kg °C), and Δθ is the temperature change in °C. This equation is on the equation sheet.
- The specific heat capacity of a substance is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of it by 1 °C.

#### Explained

Different materials warm up at different rates for the same energy input. Water has a very high specific heat capacity (about 4200 J/kg °C), so it takes a lot of energy to heat and gives out a lot as it cools. That is why it is used in radiators and storage heaters. Metals such as copper have low values (around 390 J/kg °C), so they heat up quickly.

#### Worked example

How much energy is needed to heat 2.5 kg of water from 18 °C to 100 °C? (c of water = 4200 J/kg °C)

1. Δθ = 100 − 18 = 82 °C.
2. ΔE = 2.5 × 4200 × 82 = 861 000 J (861 kJ).

A 0.50 kg block of metal is heated with 9000 J of energy and its temperature rises by 20 °C. Calculate its specific heat capacity.

1. Rearrange: c = ΔE ÷ (m × Δθ).
2. c = 9000 ÷ (0.50 × 20) = 9000 ÷ 10 = 900 J/kg °C.

#### Common mistakes

- Using the final temperature instead of the change in temperature.
- Using grams rather than kilograms. 500 g = 0.50 kg.

#### Practical

Required practical 1: finding the specific heat capacity of a material.

1. Measure the mass of a metal block (for example 1 kg) with a balance. Wrap it in insulation.
2. Put an electric heater in one hole and a thermometer in the other. A drop of water in the thermometer hole improves contact.
3. Connect the heater to a power supply with an ammeter in series and a voltmeter in parallel (or use a joulemeter).
4. Record the starting temperature, switch on and start a stopwatch.
5. Record the current and potential difference, and the temperature every minute for about 10 minutes.
6. Energy transferred = potential difference × current × time (E = VIt), or read the joulemeter.
7. Plot temperature (y-axis) against energy transferred (x-axis). The gradient is 1 ÷ (m × c), so c = 1 ÷ (gradient × m).

Why the value comes out too high: some energy heats the surroundings, not the block, so more energy seems to be needed for each degree. Insulating the block reduces this. Thermal contact and the heater warming up before the block also add error, which is why you use the straight part of the graph.

Safety: the heater and block get hot; do not touch them and let them cool before packing away.

### 4.1.1.4 Power

#### What you need to know

- Power is the rate at which energy is transferred or the rate at which work is done.

```latex
P = \frac{E}{t} \qquad P = \frac{W}{t}
```

- Power in watts (W), energy transferred in joules (J), work done in joules (J), time in seconds (s).
- An energy transfer of 1 joule per second is 1 watt.
- You should be able to give examples that show what power means, for example two motors lifting the same load the same height in different times.

#### Explained

Two electric motors each lift a 10 kg box up 2 m. Both do the same work. The one that does it in 4 s has twice the power of the one that takes 8 s. Power tells you how fast, not how much.

#### Worked example

A motor does 4800 J of work in 1 minute. Calculate its power.

1. t = 60 s.
2. P = 4800 ÷ 60 = 80 W.

A 2.0 kW kettle is switched on for 3.5 minutes. How much energy does it transfer?

1. P = 2000 W, t = 3.5 × 60 = 210 s.
2. E = P × t = 2000 × 210 = 420 000 J.

#### Common mistakes

- Leaving time in minutes or hours. Convert to seconds.
- Leaving kW as kW. 2.0 kW = 2000 W.

### 4.1.2.1 Energy transfers in a system

#### What you need to know

- Energy can be transferred usefully, stored or dissipated, but it cannot be created or destroyed. This is the principle of conservation of energy.
- In a closed system there is no net change in the total energy when energy is transferred.
- In every system change some energy is dissipated, meaning it is stored in less useful ways. This energy is often described as wasted.
- You should be able to explain ways of reducing unwanted energy transfers, for example by lubrication and by thermal insulation.
- The higher the thermal conductivity of a material, the higher the rate of energy transfer through it by conduction.
- The rate of cooling of a building depends on the thickness and the thermal conductivity of its walls.
- You should be able to describe how the rate of cooling of a building is affected by the thickness and thermal conductivity of its walls.

#### Explained

Dissipated energy is spread out, usually into the thermal store of the surroundings, so it is hard to use again. A motor gets warm and makes a noise; the energy in those warm parts and in the sound is wasted.

Ways to reduce unwanted transfers:

- Lubrication (oil or grease) reduces friction between moving parts, so less work is done against friction and less energy goes to the thermal store.
- Thermal insulation slows heating of the surroundings. Materials with low thermal conductivity (such as trapped air in foam, fibreglass or double glazing) let energy through slowly.
- Thicker walls slow the rate of energy transfer. A house loses energy more slowly through thick walls made of a material with low thermal conductivity.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying energy is "lost" as if it disappears. It is still there, just spread out and not useful.
- Mixing up conductivity and conductor. A material with low thermal conductivity is a good insulator.

#### Practical

Required practical 2 (Physics only): investigating thermal insulators.

1. Put a small beaker inside a larger one. Fill the small beaker with a measured volume of hot water (for example 80 cm³ from a kettle).
2. Pack the gap between the beakers with the insulating material being tested. Put a lid of the same material on top with a hole for a thermometer.
3. Record the starting temperature and then the temperature every 2 minutes for 20 minutes.
4. Repeat with different materials, keeping the volume of water, starting temperature, beaker sizes and time the same.
5. Repeat with different thicknesses of one material (for example 1, 2, 3 and 4 layers of bubble wrap).

The best insulator gives the smallest temperature drop in the 20 minutes. More layers usually give a smaller drop, because the energy has further to travel through a poor conductor.

Safety: take care with hot water; stand beakers on a heatproof mat.

### 4.1.2.2 Efficiency

#### What you need to know

- The energy efficiency of any energy transfer can be calculated:

```latex
\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful output energy transfer}}{\text{total input energy transfer}}
```

```latex
\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful power output}}{\text{total power input}}
```

- Efficiency has no unit. It can be written as a decimal (0.35) or a percentage (35%). Multiply the decimal by 100 to get a percentage.
- No device can be more than 100% efficient.
- (HT only) You should be able to describe ways to increase the efficiency of an intended energy transfer.

#### Explained

A filament bulb might take in 60 J each second and give out only 6 J of light. Its efficiency is 6 ÷ 60 = 0.10, or 10%. The other 54 J heats the surroundings.

(HT only) To make a transfer more efficient you reduce the wasted energy: lubricate moving parts to cut friction, use streamlined shapes to cut air resistance, insulate to cut unwanted heating, use thicker wires or lower-resistance parts to cut heating in circuits, and swap filament bulbs for LEDs.

#### Worked example

An electric motor has an input power of 500 W and a useful output power of 350 W. Calculate its efficiency as a percentage.

1. Efficiency = 350 ÷ 500 = 0.70.
2. As a percentage: 0.70 × 100 = 70%.

A kettle is 85% efficient. It transfers 340 000 J usefully to the water. How much energy was put in?

1. Total input = useful output ÷ efficiency.
2. 340 000 ÷ 0.85 = 400 000 J.

#### Common mistakes

- Dividing the bigger number by the smaller and getting more than 100%. Useful output always goes on top.
- Giving efficiency a unit. It has none.

## 4.8 Space physics (Physics only)

The whole of this topic is Physics only. It covers the solar system and how it formed, the life cycle of stars and where the elements come from, orbits of planets and satellites, and red-shift as evidence for the Big Bang.

### 4.8.1.1 Our solar system

#### What you need to know

- Within our solar system there is one star, the Sun, plus the eight planets and the dwarf planets that orbit around the Sun. Natural satellites, the moons, orbit planets.
- Our solar system is a small part of the Milky Way galaxy.
- The Sun was formed from a cloud of dust and gas (nebula) pulled together by gravitational attraction.
- You should be able to explain how, at the start of a star's life cycle, the dust and gas drawn together by gravity causes fusion reactions.
- Fusion reactions lead to an equilibrium between the gravitational collapse of a star and the expansion of a star due to fusion energy.

#### Explained

Scale: the Earth is one planet in a solar system around one star; the Sun is one of billions of stars in the Milky Way; the Milky Way is one of billions of galaxies in the universe.

Formation of the Sun:

1. Gravity pulls a cloud of dust and gas (a nebula) together.
2. As it collapses, it gets denser and hotter, forming a protostar.
3. When the core is hot and dense enough, hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium, releasing energy. The star is born.

A stable star is a balance of two effects: gravity pulling inwards and the outward pressure from the energy released by fusion. While these are equal, the star stays the same size. This is the main sequence stage, which the Sun is in now.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Sun burns hydrogen like a fire. It fuses hydrogen nuclei; there is no chemical burning.
- Calling Pluto a planet. It is a dwarf planet.

### 4.8.1.2 The life cycle of a star

#### What you need to know

- A star goes through a life cycle. The life cycle is determined by the size of the star.
- Fusion processes in stars produce all of the naturally occurring elements. Elements heavier than iron are produced in a supernova.
- The explosion of a massive star (supernova) distributes the elements throughout the universe.
- You should be able to describe the life cycle of a star the size of the Sun, and of a star much more massive than the Sun.
- You should be able to explain how fusion processes lead to the formation of new elements.

#### Explained

Both paths start the same way: nebula → protostar → main sequence star.

| Star about the size of the Sun | Star much more massive than the Sun |
| --- | --- |
| Main sequence star | Main sequence star (shorter life, because it fuses its hydrogen much faster) |
| Red giant: when hydrogen in the core runs low, the star swells and cools on the surface; helium fuses into heavier elements such as carbon | Red supergiant: swells even more; fusion makes heavier and heavier elements up to iron |
| White dwarf: the outer layers drift away and the hot, dense core is left; it shrinks and cools | Supernova: the core collapses and the star explodes, forming elements heavier than iron and scattering all elements into space |
| Black dwarf: a white dwarf that has cooled so much it no longer gives out much energy | Neutron star (very dense core left behind) or, for the most massive stars, a black hole (gravity so strong not even light can escape) |

Making elements: in the main sequence, hydrogen fuses to helium. In a red giant or supergiant, helium fuses into carbon, then oxygen and so on. Fusion in normal stars stops at iron. Elements heavier than iron (such as gold and uranium) are only made in the extreme conditions of a supernova. The explosion scatters these elements into space, where they become part of new nebulas, new stars and planets. The atoms in your body were made in stars.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the Sun will become a supernova. Stars the size of the Sun end as white dwarfs.
- Saying elements heavier than iron are made on the main sequence.

### 4.8.1.3 Orbital motion, natural and artificial satellites

#### What you need to know

- Gravity provides the force that allows planets and satellites (both natural and artificial) to maintain their circular orbits.
- You should be able to describe the similarities and distinctions between the planets, their moons, and artificial satellites.
- (HT only) You should be able to explain qualitatively how, for circular orbits, the force of gravity can lead to changing velocity but unchanged speed.
- (HT only) You should be able to explain qualitatively how, for a stable orbit, the radius must change if the speed changes.

#### Explained

| | Planets | Moons | Artificial satellites |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Orbit around | The Sun | A planet | A planet (usually Earth) |
| Made by | Nature | Nature | People |
| Held in orbit by | Gravity of the Sun | Gravity of the planet | Gravity of the Earth |
| Examples | Earth, Mars, Jupiter | The Moon, moons of Jupiter | Weather, communication and GPS satellites; the International Space Station |

All orbits at GCSE are treated as nearly circular, and all are kept going by gravity.

(HT only) Constant speed, changing velocity: gravity pulls the satellite towards the centre of the orbit, at right angles to its motion. A force at right angles does not speed the satellite up or slow it down, but it does keep changing its direction. Velocity is a vector, so a change in direction is a change in velocity, which is an acceleration towards the centre.

(HT only) Speed and radius: closer to the planet, gravity is stronger, so to stay in a stable orbit a satellite must move faster (otherwise it would be pulled inwards). Further out, gravity is weaker, so a satellite must move more slowly (otherwise it would fly off). So if the speed of a satellite in a stable orbit changes, the radius of its orbit must change too: a smaller orbit needs a higher speed. Satellites in low orbits go round the Earth in about 90 minutes; geostationary satellites much further out take 24 hours.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying there is no gravity in orbit. Gravity is what keeps the satellite in orbit; astronauts float because they and their spacecraft are falling around the Earth together.
- (HT only) Saying a satellite is not accelerating because its speed is constant. Its direction is changing, so it is accelerating.

### 4.8.2 Red-shift

#### What you need to know

- There is an observed increase in the wavelength of light from most distant galaxies. The further away the galaxies, the faster they are moving and the bigger the observed increase in wavelength. This effect is called red-shift.
- The observed red-shift provides evidence that space itself (the universe) is expanding and supports the Big Bang theory.
- The Big Bang theory suggests that the universe began from a very small region that was extremely hot and dense.
- Since 1998 onwards, observations of supernovae suggest that distant galaxies are receding ever faster.
- You should be able to explain qualitatively the red-shift of light from galaxies that are receding, how the change of each galaxy's speed with distance is evidence of an expanding universe, how red-shift provides evidence for the Big Bang model, and how scientists are able to use observations to arrive at theories such as the Big Bang theory.
- You should be able to explain that there is still much about the universe that is not understood, for example dark mass and dark energy.

#### Explained

Each element absorbs light at particular wavelengths, giving dark lines in a spectrum. In light from a distant galaxy the same pattern of lines appears, but shifted towards the red (longer wavelength) end. This means the galaxy is moving away from us: as it recedes, the waves are stretched out.

Two observations:

1. Almost all distant galaxies are red-shifted, so they are moving away from us.
2. The further away a galaxy is, the bigger its red-shift, so the faster it is moving away.

This is what you would expect if space itself is expanding: think of dots on a balloon being blown up, where every dot moves away from every other, and the dots furthest apart separate fastest. Run the expansion backwards and everything comes together in a tiny, extremely hot, dense region: the Big Bang.

How scientists got there: observations (red-shift of many galaxies) were collected, a theory was proposed to explain them, and further evidence was tested against it. The Big Bang theory replaced the idea of a steady, unchanging universe because it explained the red-shift data better.

Open questions: since 1998, measurements of distant supernovae show that the expansion is speeding up. Scientists do not yet know why; the unknown cause is called dark energy. Galaxies also spin as if they contain much more mass than we can see; this unseen mass is called dark mass (dark matter). Neither is understood yet.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying red-shift means the light turns red. It means the wavelength gets longer, shifting the pattern of lines towards the red end.
- Saying galaxies are moving away from the Earth because we are at the centre. Every galaxy sees the others moving away; there is no centre.

### 4.2.1.2 Electrical charge and current

#### What you need to know

- For electrical charge to flow through a closed circuit, the circuit must include a source of potential difference.
- Electric current is a flow of electrical charge. The size of the current is the rate of flow of charge.

```latex
Q = I t
```

- Charge flow Q in coulombs (C), current I in amperes (A), time t in seconds (s).
- A current has the same value at any point in a single closed loop.

#### Explained

In metal wires the charge carriers are electrons. A current of 1 A means 1 coulomb of charge passes a point every second. In a single loop there is nowhere for the charge to pile up or leak away, so the current is the same everywhere in that loop.

#### Worked example

A current of 0.25 A flows through a lamp for 2 minutes. Calculate the charge that flows.

1. t = 2 × 60 = 120 s.
2. Q = 0.25 × 120 = 30 C.

How long does it take for 600 C to flow at 4.0 A?

1. t = Q ÷ I = 600 ÷ 4.0 = 150 s.

#### Common mistakes

- Forgetting to convert minutes to seconds.
- Thinking current is "used up" by components. The current going into a lamp equals the current coming out.

### 4.2.1.3 Current, resistance and potential difference

#### What you need to know

- The current through a component depends on both the resistance of the component and the potential difference across the component. The greater the resistance, the smaller the current for a given potential difference.

```latex
V = I R
```

- Potential difference V in volts (V), current I in amperes (A), resistance R in ohms (Ω).

#### Explained

Potential difference is the push that drives charge round; resistance is how hard it is for the charge to get through. With a bigger push you get more current; with more resistance you get less.

#### Worked example

A resistor has a potential difference of 6.0 V across it and a current of 0.30 A through it. Calculate its resistance.

1. R = V ÷ I = 6.0 ÷ 0.30 = 20 Ω.

A 12 V supply is connected to a 48 Ω heater. Calculate the current.

1. I = V ÷ R = 12 ÷ 48 = 0.25 A.

#### Common mistakes

- Using milliamps without converting. 250 mA = 0.25 A.

#### Practical

Required practical 3: investigating factors that affect resistance.

Part 1, length of a wire:

1. Tape a length of thin constantan (resistance) wire along a metre ruler. Connect a crocodile clip at the zero end.
2. Build a circuit with a cell or low-voltage supply, an ammeter in series and a switch. Connect a voltmeter across the length of wire being tested.
3. Attach the second crocodile clip at 10 cm. Close the switch, read the current and potential difference, then open the switch.
4. Repeat at 20 cm, 30 cm and so on up to 100 cm.
5. Calculate R = V ÷ I for each length. Plot resistance (y-axis) against length (x-axis).

Expected result: a straight line through the origin (or close to it), so resistance is directly proportional to length.

Keep the temperature constant by keeping the current small and opening the switch between readings, because a hot wire has a higher resistance. A line that does not go through the origin suggests a zero error, such as the clip not being exactly at 0 cm or resistance in the clips.

Part 2, resistors in series and parallel:

1. Measure the current and potential difference for one resistor and calculate its resistance.
2. Add a second identical resistor in series and repeat. The total resistance is the sum, so it doubles.
3. Put the two resistors in parallel and repeat. The total resistance is less than either one.

Safety: the wire can get hot at short lengths; use a low potential difference and do not touch it while the current flows.

### 4.2.1.4 Resistors

#### What you need to know

- For some resistors at constant temperature, the current is directly proportional to the potential difference across the resistor. The resistance stays the same as the current changes. These are called ohmic conductors.
- The resistance of components such as lamps, diodes, thermistors and LDRs is not constant; it changes with the current through the component.
- The resistance of a filament lamp increases as the temperature of the filament increases.
- The current through a diode flows in one direction only. The diode has a very high resistance in the reverse direction.
- The resistance of a thermistor decreases as the temperature increases. Thermistors are used in thermostats and temperature sensors.
- The resistance of an LDR decreases as light intensity increases. LDRs are used in circuits that switch lights on when it gets dark.
- You should be able to explain the design and use of a circuit to measure the resistance of a component by measuring the current through, and potential difference across, the component.
- You should be able to draw an appropriate circuit diagram using correct symbols and use graphs to explore whether circuit elements are linear or non-linear, and relate the curves to function and properties.

#### Explained

The I–V graph (current on the y-axis, potential difference on the x-axis) shows how a component behaves.

| Component | Shape of I–V graph | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resistor at constant temperature | Straight line through the origin, in both directions | Resistance is constant |
| Filament lamp | S-shaped curve that gets flatter at higher p.d., the same in both directions | As current rises the filament gets hotter, the ions vibrate more and get in the way of the electrons, so resistance rises |
| Diode | Zero current for negative p.d.; current rises steeply once a small positive p.d. (about 0.6 V) is reached | Very high resistance one way, low the other |

Thermistor: as it warms, resistance falls. Used in fire alarms, fridges and car engine sensors. LDR: in bright light resistance is low; in the dark it is high. Used in street lights and security lights.

#### Common mistakes

- Reading resistance from the gradient of an I–V graph. Resistance = V ÷ I at a point, not the gradient. For a straight line through the origin, the steeper the line, the lower the resistance.
- Saying a thermistor's resistance increases with temperature. For the thermistors at GCSE it decreases.

#### Practical

Required practical 4: investigating I–V characteristics.

1. Set up a circuit with a power supply, a variable resistor, an ammeter in series with the component, and a voltmeter in parallel with the component.
2. Use the variable resistor to change the potential difference across the component in steps. Record current and potential difference each time.
3. Reverse the connections to the component (or the supply) and repeat to get negative values.
4. Repeat for a fixed resistor, a filament lamp and a diode (a diode needs a protective resistor in series).
5. Plot current (y-axis) against potential difference (x-axis) for each, including negative values.

Keep the fixed resistor at a constant temperature by keeping currents low and switching off between readings.

Safety: the lamp gets hot; a diode can be damaged by a large current, so always include its protective resistor.

### 4.2.2 Series and parallel circuits

#### What you need to know

- There are two ways of joining electrical components: in series and in parallel. Some circuits include both.
- For components in series:
  - there is the same current through each component
  - the total potential difference of the power supply is shared between the components
  - the total resistance of two components is the sum of the resistance of each component.

```latex
R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2
```

- For components in parallel:
  - the potential difference across each branch is the same
  - the total current through the whole circuit is the sum of the currents through the separate branches
  - the total resistance of two resistors is less than the resistance of the smaller individual resistor.
- You should be able to use circuit diagrams to construct and check series and parallel circuits that include a variety of common components, and solve problems for circuits with resistors in series using the idea of equivalent resistance.
- You should be able to explain qualitatively why adding resistors in series increases the total resistance whilst adding resistors in parallel decreases the total resistance.
- You should be able to calculate the currents, potential differences and resistances in d.c. series circuits.

#### Explained

In series there is one path, so charge has to go through every resistor in turn: more resistors means more opposition. In parallel there are extra paths, so charge has more routes to take: the total current from the supply goes up for the same potential difference, which means the total resistance has gone down.

#### Worked example

A 9.0 V battery is connected to a 10 Ω resistor and a 20 Ω resistor in series. Calculate the current and the potential difference across each resistor.

1. R_total = 10 + 20 = 30 Ω.
2. I = V ÷ R = 9.0 ÷ 30 = 0.30 A (the same through both).
3. V across 10 Ω = 0.30 × 10 = 3.0 V.
4. V across 20 Ω = 0.30 × 20 = 6.0 V.
5. Check: 3.0 + 6.0 = 9.0 V, the supply potential difference.

Two lamps are in parallel across a 6.0 V supply. One takes 0.20 A and the other 0.50 A. What is the supply current? 0.20 + 0.50 = 0.70 A. The potential difference across each lamp is 6.0 V.

#### Common mistakes

- Adding resistances in parallel. At GCSE you only need to say the total is less than the smallest one.
- Saying the current is "shared" in series. It is the potential difference that is shared in series; current is shared in parallel.

### 4.2.3.1 Direct and alternating potential difference

#### What you need to know

- Mains electricity is an a.c. supply. In the UK the domestic supply has a frequency of 50 Hz and is about 230 V.
- You should be able to explain the difference between direct and alternating potential difference.

#### Explained

A direct potential difference (d.c.), such as from a cell, always pushes charge the same way, so the current flows in one direction. An alternating potential difference (a.c.) keeps reversing direction, so the current flows back and forth. UK mains changes direction 50 times each way every second (50 cycles per second).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying mains is 50 V. It is about 230 V at 50 Hz.

### 4.2.3.2 Mains electricity

#### What you need to know

- Most electrical appliances are connected to the mains using three-core cable.
- The insulation covering each wire is colour coded:
  - live wire: brown
  - neutral wire: blue
  - earth wire: green and yellow stripes.
- The live wire carries the alternating potential difference from the supply. The neutral wire completes the circuit. The earth wire is a safety wire to stop the appliance becoming live.
- The potential difference between the live wire and earth (0 V) is about 230 V. The neutral wire is at, or close to, earth potential (0 V). The earth wire is at 0 V; it only carries a current if there is a fault.
- You should be able to explain that a live wire may be dangerous even when a switch in the mains circuit is open, and explain the danger of providing any connection between the live wire and earth.

#### Explained

Your body is at about 0 V. If you touch the live wire, there is a potential difference of about 230 V across you, and a current flows through you to earth. That can cause a fatal shock.

A switch that is off still leaves the live wire at 230 V on the supply side of the switch, so it is still dangerous to touch. Any connection between the live wire and earth, such as damaged insulation touching a metal case or a person, gives a low-resistance path; a large current flows, which can cause a shock or a fire. In a metal-cased appliance, the earth wire gives that current a safe path; the large current melts the fuse and cuts off the supply.

#### Common mistakes

- Thinking the earth wire carries current all the time. It only does when there is a fault.
- Mixing up the colours. Live is brown, neutral is blue, earth is green and yellow.

### 4.2.4.1 Power

#### What you need to know

- The power transfer in any circuit device is related to the potential difference across it and the current through it, and to the energy changes over time.

```latex
P = V I \qquad P = I^2 R
```

- Power P in watts (W), potential difference V in volts (V), current I in amperes (A), resistance R in ohms (Ω).

#### Worked example

A hairdryer runs on 230 V and draws a current of 5.0 A. Calculate its power.

1. P = 230 × 5.0 = 1150 W.

A current of 3.0 A flows through a 40 Ω heating element. Calculate the power.

1. P = I²R = 3.0² × 40 = 9 × 40 = 360 W.

#### Common mistakes

- Forgetting to square the current in P = I²R.

### 4.2.4.2 Energy transfers in everyday appliances

#### What you need to know

- Everyday electrical appliances are designed to bring about energy transfers.
- The amount of energy an appliance transfers depends on how long it is switched on for and its power.
- You should be able to describe how different domestic appliances transfer energy from batteries or a.c. mains to the kinetic energy of electric motors or the energy of heating devices.
- Work is done when charge flows in a circuit.

```latex
E = P t \qquad E = Q V
```

- Energy E in joules (J), power P in watts (W), time t in seconds (s), charge flow Q in coulombs (C), potential difference V in volts (V).
- You should be able to explain how the power of a circuit device is related to the potential difference across it, the current through it, and the energy transferred over a given time.
- You should be able to describe, with examples, the relationship between the power ratings for domestic electrical appliances and the changes in stored energy when they are in use.

#### Explained

A fan transfers energy electrically from the mains to the kinetic store of the motor and blades. A kettle transfers energy electrically to the thermal store of the heating element and then by heating to the water. A higher power rating means more energy transferred each second: a 3 kW kettle boils water faster than a 2 kW one.

#### Worked example

A 9.0 V battery drives 450 C of charge through a toy motor. How much energy is transferred?

1. E = QV = 450 × 9.0 = 4050 J.

A 60 W lamp is left on for 8 hours. How much energy does it transfer in joules?

1. t = 8 × 3600 = 28 800 s.
2. E = 60 × 28 800 = 1 728 000 J (1.73 MJ).

#### Common mistakes

- Using hours with watts. In E = Pt, power in W and time in s give joules.

### 4.2.4.3 The National Grid

#### What you need to know

- The National Grid is a system of cables and transformers linking power stations to consumers.
- Electrical power is transferred from power stations to consumers using the National Grid.
- Step-up transformers increase the potential difference from the power station to the transmission cables; step-down transformers decrease it to a much lower value for domestic use.
- You should be able to explain why the National Grid system is an efficient way to transfer energy.

#### Explained

For a given power, P = VI. If the potential difference is made very high (hundreds of thousands of volts), the current is very low. The heating in the cables depends on I²R, so a low current means much less energy is wasted heating the cables. Step-down transformers near homes then bring the potential difference down to a safe 230 V.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying high voltage "makes the electricity travel faster". The point is a lower current, so less heating of the cables.

### 4.2.5.1 Static charge (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- When certain insulating materials are rubbed against each other they become electrically charged. Negatively charged electrons are rubbed off one material and onto the other.
- The material that gains electrons becomes negatively charged. The material that loses electrons is left with an equal positive charge.
- When two electrically charged objects are brought close together they exert a force on each other. Two objects that carry the same type of charge repel. Two objects that carry different types of charge attract.
- Attraction and repulsion between two charged objects are examples of non-contact force.
- You should be able to describe the production of static electricity, and sparking, by rubbing surfaces, and describe evidence that charged objects exert forces of attraction or repulsion on one another when not in contact.
- You should be able to explain how the transfer of electrons between objects can explain the phenomena of static electricity.

#### Explained

Only electrons move. Positive charges (protons) stay in the nuclei. Rub a polythene rod with a cloth: electrons move from the cloth to the rod, so the rod becomes negative and the cloth positive. Rub an acetate rod and electrons move from the rod to the cloth, so the rod becomes positive.

Evidence of the forces: hang one charged rod on a thread and bring another near; same charges swing apart, opposite charges swing together. A charged balloon sticks to a wall.

Sparking: if enough charge builds up, the potential difference between the object and something nearby (such as an earthed object) becomes large enough for electrons to jump across the air gap, making a spark.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying positive charges move. At GCSE only electrons are transferred.

### 4.2.5.2 Electric fields (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- A charged object creates an electric field around itself. The electric field is strongest close to the charged object; the further away, the weaker it is.
- A second charged object placed in the field experiences a force. The force gets stronger as the distance between the objects decreases.
- You should be able to draw the electric field pattern for an isolated charged sphere.
- You should be able to explain the concept of an electric field, and how it helps explain non-contact forces between charged objects and sparking.

#### Explained

Field lines for an isolated sphere are straight lines that go out radially, like spokes. For a positive sphere the arrows point outwards (the direction a positive charge would be pushed). For a negative sphere the arrows point inwards. Lines are closer together near the sphere, showing the field is stronger there.

The field explains a non-contact force: one charge does not need to touch the other because each sits in the other's field. Sparking happens when the field is strong enough to pull electrons from air molecules, so the air conducts and charge flows across the gap.

#### Common mistakes

- Drawing field lines that curve or cross for a single sphere. They are straight, radial and never cross.

## 4.7 Magnetism and electromagnetism

Magnets and electric currents both make magnetic fields, and the two are linked: a current in a field feels a force, and a moving magnet can make a current. This topic covers permanent and induced magnets, field patterns, electromagnets, (HT only) the motor effect, Fleming's left-hand rule and motors, and (Physics only, HT only) loudspeakers, the generator effect, microphones and transformers.

### Equations for this topic

| --- | --- | --- |
| force on a current-carrying conductor at right angles to a field = magnetic flux density × current × length | F = BIl (N, T, A, m) | Equation sheet (HT only) |
| potential difference across primary ÷ potential difference across secondary = turns on primary ÷ turns on secondary | Vp/Vs = np/ns | Equation sheet (Physics only, HT) |
| primary p.d. × primary current = secondary p.d. × secondary current | VpIp = VsIs | Equation sheet (Physics only, HT) |

### 4.7.1.1 Poles of a magnet

#### What you need to know

- The poles of a magnet are the places where the magnetic forces are strongest. When two magnets are brought close together they exert a force on each other. Two like poles repel each other. Two unlike poles attract each other. Attraction and repulsion between two magnetic poles are examples of non-contact force.
- A permanent magnet produces its own magnetic field. An induced magnet is a material that becomes a magnet when it is placed in a magnetic field. Induced magnetism always causes a force of attraction. When removed from the magnetic field an induced magnet loses most or all of its magnetism quickly.
- You should be able to describe the attraction and repulsion between unlike and like poles for permanent magnets, and describe the difference between permanent and induced magnets.

#### Explained

North–north repel, south–south repel, north–south attract.

A paperclip near a magnet becomes an induced magnet: the end nearest the magnet's north pole becomes a south pole, so it is always attracted. Take the magnet away and the paperclip stops being magnetic.

The magnetic materials are iron, steel, cobalt and nickel. Iron is magnetically soft (easy to magnetise and loses its magnetism quickly); steel is magnetically hard (keeps its magnetism, so it is used for permanent magnets).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a magnet attracts all metals. Only iron, steel, cobalt and nickel are magnetic.
- Using attraction to test whether something is a magnet. Only repulsion proves it, because an induced magnet is also attracted.

### 4.7.1.2 Magnetic fields

#### What you need to know

- The region around a magnet where a force acts on another magnet or on a magnetic material (iron, steel, cobalt and nickel) is called the magnetic field.
- The force between a magnet and a magnetic material is always one of attraction.
- The strength of the magnetic field depends on the distance from the magnet. The field is strongest at the poles of the magnet.
- The direction of the magnetic field at any point is given by the direction of the force that would act on another north pole placed at that point. The direction of a magnetic field line is from the north (seeking) pole of a magnet to the south (seeking) pole of the magnet.
- A magnetic compass contains a small bar magnet. The Earth has a magnetic field. The compass needle points in the direction of the Earth's magnetic field.
- You should be able to describe how to plot the magnetic field pattern of a magnet using a compass, draw the magnetic field pattern of a bar magnet showing how strength and direction change from one point to another, and explain how the behaviour of a magnetic compass is related to evidence that the core of the Earth must be magnetic.

#### Explained

Plotting a field with a compass:

1. Put a bar magnet on paper and draw round it.
2. Put a small plotting compass near the north pole. Mark a dot at the end the needle points to.
3. Move the compass so its tail is on the dot, and mark the new tip position.
4. Keep going until you reach the south pole. Join the dots to make a field line, with an arrow from north to south.
5. Repeat from different starting points.

Field lines of a bar magnet curve from N round to S. They are closest together at the poles, where the field is strongest, and never cross.

A compass far away from any magnet still points north. This shows the Earth has its own magnetic field, which must come from its magnetic core.

#### Common mistakes

- Drawing arrows from south to north. Field lines go from N to S outside the magnet.

### 4.7.2.1 Electromagnetism

#### What you need to know

- When a current flows through a conducting wire a magnetic field is produced around the wire. The strength of the magnetic field depends on the current through the wire and the distance from the wire.
- Shaping a wire to form a solenoid increases the strength of the magnetic field created by a current through the wire. The magnetic field inside a solenoid is strong and uniform.
- The magnetic field around a solenoid has a similar shape to that of a bar magnet. Adding an iron core increases the strength of the magnetic field of a solenoid. An electromagnet is a solenoid with an iron core.
- You should be able to describe how the magnetic effect of a current can be demonstrated, draw the magnetic field pattern for a straight wire carrying a current and for a solenoid (showing the direction of the field), and explain how a solenoid arrangement can increase the magnetic effect of the current.
- (Physics only) You should be able to interpret diagrams of electromagnetic devices in order to explain how they work.

#### Explained

Straight wire: the field is a set of circles around the wire, getting further apart further from the wire. Use the right-hand grip rule: point your right thumb in the direction of the current; your fingers curl the way the field goes.

Demonstration: pass a wire vertically through a card, put plotting compasses on the card, and switch on a large current. The compasses line up in a circle. Reverse the current and they all reverse.

Solenoid: coiling the wire makes the fields of each turn add together inside the coil, giving a strong, uniform field (straight, parallel, evenly spaced lines inside), with a bar-magnet-shaped field outside.

Ways to make an electromagnet stronger: more current, more turns on the coil, add an iron core.

(Physics only) Examples of devices:

- Electric bell: closing the switch magnetises an electromagnet, which pulls an iron arm so the hammer hits the bell. This breaks the contact, the electromagnet switches off, the arm springs back, the contact closes again, and the cycle repeats.
- Relay: a small current in one circuit magnetises an electromagnet, which pulls an iron armature and closes the switch in a second circuit that carries a large current (such as a car starter motor).
- Scrapyard crane: a large electromagnet lifts iron and steel when switched on and drops them when switched off.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a steel core is best. Iron is used because it loses its magnetism when the current stops; steel would stay magnetised.

### 4.7.2.2 Fleming's left-hand rule (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- When a conductor carrying a current is placed in a magnetic field the magnet producing the field and the conductor exert a force on each other. This is called the motor effect.
- You should be able to show that Fleming's left-hand rule represents the relative orientation of the force, the current in the conductor and the magnetic field.
- You should be able to recall the factors that affect the size of the force on the conductor.
- For a conductor at right angles to a magnetic field and carrying a current:

```latex
F = B I l
```

- Force F in newtons (N), magnetic flux density B in tesla (T), current I in amperes (A), length l in metres (m). This equation is on the equation sheet.

#### Explained

Fleming's left-hand rule: hold your thumb, first finger and second finger of your left hand at right angles.

- First finger: Field (north to south).
- Second finger: Current (positive to negative, conventional current).
- Thumb: Thrust (the force, or motion).

The force is biggest when the wire is at right angles to the field and zero when the wire is parallel to it. The force gets bigger if the current, the magnetic flux density, or the length of wire in the field increases.

#### Worked example

A wire 0.050 m long carries 3.0 A at right angles to a magnetic field of flux density 0.20 T. Calculate the force.

1. F = 0.20 × 3.0 × 0.050 = 0.030 N.

#### Common mistakes

- Using the right hand. The motor effect uses the left hand.
- Leaving length in cm.

### 4.7.2.3 Electric motors (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- A coil of wire carrying a current in a magnetic field tends to rotate. This is the basis of an electric motor.
- You should be able to explain how the force on a conductor in a magnetic field causes the rotation of the coil in an electric motor.

#### Explained

A rectangular coil sits between the poles of a magnet. Current flows one way along one side of the coil and the opposite way along the other side. By Fleming's left-hand rule, one side is pushed up and the other down, so the coil turns.

A split-ring commutator connects the coil to the supply through brushes. Every half turn it swaps the connections, reversing the current in the coil. This keeps the forces turning the coil in the same direction, so it spins continuously.

To make the motor turn faster: increase the current, use a stronger magnet, or add more turns. To reverse the direction: reverse the current or swap the magnetic poles.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying the commutator reverses the magnetic field. It reverses the current in the coil.

### 4.7.2.4 Loudspeakers (Physics only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Loudspeakers and headphones use the motor effect to convert variations in current in electrical circuits to the pressure variations in sound waves.
- You should be able to explain how a moving-coil loudspeaker and headphones work.

#### Explained

A coil of wire is fixed to a paper or plastic cone and sits around a permanent magnet. An alternating current from the amplifier flows in the coil. The coil feels a force (motor effect) that keeps reversing as the current reverses, so the coil and cone vibrate back and forth. The cone pushes and pulls on the air, making compressions and rarefactions: a sound wave with the same frequency as the current. A bigger current gives a bigger amplitude, so a louder sound.

### 4.7.3.1 Induced potential (Physics only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- If an electrical conductor moves relative to a magnetic field or if there is a change in the magnetic field around a conductor, a potential difference is induced across the ends of the conductor. If the conductor is part of a complete circuit, a current is induced in the conductor. This is called the generator effect.
- An induced current generates a magnetic field that opposes the original change, either the movement of the conductor or the change in magnetic field.
- You should be able to recall the factors that affect the size of the induced potential difference/induced current, and the factors that affect the direction of the induced potential difference/induced current.
- You should be able to apply the principles of the generator effect in a given context.

#### Explained

Push a magnet into a coil connected to a sensitive ammeter and the needle flicks. Hold the magnet still and the needle returns to zero: there must be movement or a changing field. Pull the magnet out and the needle flicks the other way.

Size of the induced p.d. increases with: faster movement, a stronger magnet, more turns on the coil.

Direction reverses if: the direction of movement is reversed, or the magnet is turned round (the other pole is used).

The induced current makes its own magnetic field that pushes against the change. If you push a north pole into a coil, the near end of the coil becomes a north pole and repels it, so you have to do work. That work is where the electrical energy comes from.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a stationary magnet in a coil induces a current. Only a change causes induction.

### 4.7.3.2 Uses of the generator effect (Physics only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- The generator effect is used in an alternator to generate a.c. and in a dynamo to generate d.c.
- You should be able to explain how the generator effect is used in an alternator to generate a.c. and in a dynamo to generate d.c.
- You should be able to draw and interpret graphs of potential difference generated in the coil against time.

#### Explained

Alternator: a coil spins in a magnetic field (or a magnet spins inside a coil). Each side of the coil moves up through the field and then down, so the induced p.d. keeps reversing. Slip rings and brushes keep each end of the coil connected to the same wire, so the output is a.c.

Dynamo: like an alternator but with a split-ring commutator. Every half turn it swaps the connections, so the output current always flows the same way: d.c.

Graphs of p.d. against time:

- Alternator: a wave going above and below zero (like a sine wave).
- Dynamo: a series of humps all above zero.
- Spinning faster: the peaks get taller (bigger p.d.) and closer together (higher frequency).
- The p.d. is largest when the coil sides cut across the field lines fastest (coil horizontal in a horizontal field) and zero when they move along the field lines (coil vertical).

#### Common mistakes

- Mixing up the parts: slip rings give a.c., a split-ring commutator gives d.c.

### 4.7.3.3 Microphones (Physics only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Microphones use the generator effect to convert the pressure variations in sound waves into variations in current in electrical circuits.
- You should be able to explain how a moving-coil microphone works.

#### Explained

A moving-coil microphone is built like a loudspeaker, but works the other way round. Sound waves hit a diaphragm and make it vibrate. A coil attached to the diaphragm moves back and forth in the field of a permanent magnet. This induces an alternating p.d. in the coil with the same pattern as the sound wave, which can then be amplified.

| | Loudspeaker | Microphone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Effect used | Motor effect | Generator effect |
| Input | Changing current | Sound wave |
| Output | Sound wave | Changing current |

### 4.7.3.4 Transformers (Physics only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- A basic transformer consists of a primary coil and a secondary coil wound on an iron core.
- Iron is used as it is easily magnetised.
- Knowledge of laminations and eddy currents in the core is not required.
- The ratio of the potential differences across the primary and secondary coils of a transformer, Vp and Vs, depends on the ratio of the number of turns on each coil, np and ns.

```latex
\frac{V_p}{V_s} = \frac{n_p}{n_s}
```

- In a step-up transformer Vs > Vp. In a step-down transformer Vs < Vp.
- If transformers were 100% efficient, the electrical power output would equal the electrical power input.

```latex
V_s I_s = V_p I_p
```

- Vp × Ip is the power input (primary coil) and Vs × Is is the power output (secondary coil). Both equations are on the equation sheet.
- You should be able to explain how the effect of an alternating current in one coil in inducing a current in another is used in transformers, explain how the ratio of the potential differences across the two coils depends on the ratio of the number of turns on each, calculate the current drawn from the input supply to provide a particular power output, and apply the equation linking the p.d.s and number of turns in the two coils of a transformer to the currents and the power transfer involved, and relate these to the advantages of power transmission at high potential differences.

#### Explained

An alternating current in the primary coil makes a magnetic field that keeps changing. The iron core carries this changing field to the secondary coil. The changing field through the secondary induces an alternating p.d. across it. A transformer only works with a.c., because d.c. gives a steady field that induces nothing.

More turns on the secondary than the primary gives a bigger output p.d. (step-up). Fewer turns gives a smaller output p.d. (step-down).

Link to the National Grid: a step-up transformer raises the p.d. so that, for the same power, the current is much smaller (VpIp = VsIs). A smaller current wastes less energy heating the cables.

#### Worked example

A transformer has 200 turns on the primary and 4000 turns on the secondary. The primary p.d. is 12 V. Calculate the secondary p.d.

1. Vs = Vp × ns ÷ np = 12 × 4000 ÷ 200 = 240 V.

A 100% efficient step-down transformer changes 230 V to 11.5 V. The output current is 2.0 A. Calculate the input current.

1. Power out = 11.5 × 2.0 = 23 W.
2. Ip = 23 ÷ 230 = 0.10 A.

#### Common mistakes

- Thinking a transformer increases power. A step-up transformer raises p.d. but lowers current; at best the power stays the same.
- Saying transformers work with d.c.

## 4.6 Waves

Waves carry energy from one place to another without carrying matter with them. This topic covers transverse and longitudinal waves, wave measurements, reflection, sound and ultrasound (Physics only), the electromagnetic spectrum and its uses, lenses and visible light (Physics only), and black body radiation (Physics only).

### Equations for this topic

| Equation | Symbols and units | Recall or given |
| --- | --- | --- |
| wave speed = frequency × wavelength | v = fλ (m/s, Hz, m) | Recall |
| period = 1 ÷ frequency | T = 1/f (s, Hz) | Equation sheet |
| magnification = image height ÷ object height | no units | Equation sheet (Physics only) |

### 4.6.1.1 Transverse and longitudinal waves

#### What you need to know

- Waves may be either transverse or longitudinal.
- The ripples on a water surface are an example of a transverse wave.
- Longitudinal waves show areas of compression and rarefaction. Sound waves travelling through air are longitudinal.
- You should be able to describe the difference between longitudinal and transverse waves.
- You should be able to describe evidence that, for both ripples on a water surface and sound waves in air, it is the wave and not the water or air itself that travels.

#### Explained

| | Transverse | Longitudinal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direction of vibrations | At right angles to the direction the energy travels | Parallel to the direction the energy travels |
| Examples | Water ripples, all electromagnetic waves, waves on a string | Sound waves, some seismic waves (P-waves) |
| Features | Peaks (crests) and troughs | Compressions (particles squashed together) and rarefactions (particles spread out) |

Evidence that the medium does not travel: a cork floating on a pond bobs up and down as ripples pass but does not move along with them. A loudspeaker makes sound reach you, but you do not feel a wind from it; the air particles only vibrate back and forth.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying sound is transverse. Sound in air is longitudinal.

### 4.6.1.2 Properties of waves

#### What you need to know

- You should be able to describe wave motion in terms of amplitude, wavelength, frequency and period.
- The amplitude of a wave is the maximum displacement of a point on a wave away from its undisturbed position.
- The wavelength of a wave is the distance from a point on one wave to the equivalent point on the adjacent wave.
- The frequency of a wave is the number of waves passing a point each second.

```latex
T = \frac{1}{f}
```

- Period T in seconds (s), frequency f in hertz (Hz).
- The wave speed is the speed at which the energy is transferred (or the wave moves) through the medium.

```latex
v = f \lambda
```

- Wave speed v in metres per second (m/s), frequency f in hertz (Hz), wavelength λ in metres (m).
- You should be able to identify amplitude and wavelength from given diagrams.
- You should be able to describe a method to measure the speed of sound waves in air.
- You should be able to describe a method to measure the speed of ripples on a water surface.
- (Physics only) You should be able to show how changes in velocity, frequency and wavelength, in transmission of sound waves from one medium to another, are inter-related.

#### Explained

Amplitude is measured from the middle line to a peak, not from peak to trough. Wavelength can be measured peak to peak, or trough to trough.

Speed of sound in air: two people stand a measured distance apart (for example 400 m). One bangs two blocks together; the other starts a stopwatch on seeing the blocks hit and stops it on hearing the bang. Speed = distance ÷ time. Repeat and take a mean. Light arrives almost instantly, so the time measured is the time for the sound.

(Physics only) When sound passes from one medium to another, its frequency stays the same (set by the source). Its speed changes (sound is faster in water than in air, and faster still in solids), so its wavelength changes in proportion: v = fλ with f fixed means λ rises when v rises.

#### Worked example

A wave has a frequency of 250 Hz and a wavelength of 1.4 m. Calculate its speed. v = 250 × 1.4 = 350 m/s.

A wave has a frequency of 50 Hz. Calculate its period. T = 1 ÷ 50 = 0.020 s.

(Physics only) A 500 Hz sound wave goes from air (330 m/s) into water (1500 m/s). Wavelength in air = 330 ÷ 500 = 0.66 m. Wavelength in water = 1500 ÷ 500 = 3.0 m.

#### Common mistakes

- Measuring amplitude from peak to trough. That gives twice the amplitude.
- Leaving frequency in kHz. 2 kHz = 2000 Hz.

#### Practical

Required practical 8: measuring wave speed, frequency and wavelength.

Ripple tank:

1. Set up a ripple tank with a vibrating bar (dipper) driven by a signal generator, and a lamp above so the waves cast shadows on paper or a screen below.
2. Use a low frequency so the waves can be seen and counted.
3. Wavelength: place a ruler next to the shadows and measure across several waves (for example 10), then divide by the number of waves. A photo with the ruler in it helps.
4. Frequency: count the waves passing a point in 10 seconds and divide by 10, or read it from the signal generator.
5. Wave speed = frequency × wavelength.

Wave in a solid (string):

1. Attach a string to a vibration generator, pass it over a pulley and hang masses on the end to keep it tight.
2. Switch on the signal generator and adjust the frequency until a clear standing wave is seen.
3. Measure the length of several half-wavelengths (loops) with a metre ruler and work out one wavelength (one wavelength = two loops).
4. Read the frequency from the signal generator. Wave speed = frequency × wavelength.

Safety: mop up spilt water near the mains-powered lamp; keep the hanging masses low and put something soft below them.

### 4.6.1.3 Reflection of waves (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- Waves can be reflected at the boundary between two different materials.
- Waves can be absorbed or transmitted at the boundary between two different materials.
- You should be able to construct ray diagrams to illustrate the reflection of a wave at a surface.
- You should be able to describe the effects of reflection, transmission and absorption of waves at material interfaces.

#### Explained

When a wave meets a boundary, one or more of these happens:

- Reflection: the wave bounces back. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, both measured from the normal (a line at right angles to the surface).
- Transmission: the wave passes through into the new material, often refracting (changing direction) as it changes speed.
- Absorption: the energy of the wave is taken in by the material, often warming it.

Ray diagram for a mirror: draw the mirror, draw the normal as a dashed line at the point the ray hits, draw the incident ray with an arrow, then draw the reflected ray at the same angle on the other side of the normal.

A smooth surface (like a mirror) gives specular reflection: parallel rays stay parallel and a clear image forms. A rough surface gives diffuse reflection: rays scatter in different directions and no clear image forms.

#### Common mistakes

- Measuring angles from the surface instead of the normal.

#### Practical

Required practical 9 (Physics only): reflection and refraction of light.

Reflection:

1. Put a plane mirror upright on a sheet of paper. Draw along the back of it and draw a normal.
2. Shine a narrow ray from a ray box at the mirror where the normal meets it.
3. Mark the incident and reflected rays with crosses, then join them with a ruler.
4. Measure the angles of incidence and reflection with a protractor. Repeat for several angles and for different surfaces (for example foil, white paper, black card).

Refraction:

1. Place a rectangular glass block on paper and draw round it. Draw a normal where the ray will enter.
2. Shine a ray into the block at an angle. Mark the ray going in and coming out.
3. Remove the block and join the points to show the path inside it.
4. Measure the angle of incidence and the angle of refraction. Repeat for a Perspex block or a water-filled container.

Light slows down in glass, so it bends towards the normal on the way in and away from the normal on the way out.

Safety: ray box lamps get hot; work in a dimmed room but keep the floor clear.

### 4.6.1.4 Sound waves (Physics only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- Sound waves can travel through solids, causing vibrations in the solid.
- Within the ear, sound waves cause the ear drum and other parts to vibrate, which causes the sensation of sound. The conversion of sound waves to vibrations of solids works over a limited frequency range. This restricts the limits of human hearing.
- The range of normal human hearing is from 20 Hz to 20 kHz.
- You should be able to describe, with examples, processes which convert wave disturbances between sound waves and vibrations in solids. Examples may include the effect of sound waves on the ear drum.
- You should be able to explain why such processes only work over a limited frequency range and the relevance of this to human hearing.

#### Explained

A sound wave reaches the ear and makes the eardrum vibrate at the same frequency. Tiny bones pass the vibrations on to the inner ear, where they are turned into nerve signals.

The eardrum and bones have mass and stiffness, so they can only follow vibrations in a certain range. Too low a frequency and the vibrations are not detected; too high and the parts cannot move back and forth quickly enough. So we hear only 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz, and the top limit falls with age.

#### Common mistakes

- Writing the upper limit as 20 Hz or 2000 Hz. It is 20 kHz (20 000 Hz).

### 4.6.1.5 Waves for detection and exploration (Physics only) (HT only)

#### What you need to know

- You should be able to explain in qualitative terms how the differences in velocity, absorption and reflection between different types of wave in solids and liquids can be used both for detection and exploration of structures which are hidden from direct observation.
- The three main areas are: ultrasound, seismic waves and echo sounding.
- Ultrasound waves have a frequency higher than the upper limit of hearing for humans. Ultrasound waves are partially reflected when they meet a boundary between two different media. The time taken for the reflections to reach a detector can be used to determine how far away such a boundary is. This allows ultrasound waves to be used for both medical and industrial imaging.
- Seismic waves are produced by earthquakes. P-waves are longitudinal seismic waves. P-waves travel at different speeds through solids and liquids. S-waves are transverse seismic waves. S-waves cannot travel through a liquid. P-waves and S-waves provide evidence for the structure and size of the Earth's core.
- Echo sounding, using high frequency sound waves, is used to detect objects in deep water and measure water depth.
- You should be aware that the study of seismic waves provided new evidence that led to discoveries about parts of the Earth which are not directly observable.

#### Explained

Ultrasound: a probe sends pulses into the body. At each boundary between tissues some of the wave reflects back. A computer uses the time of each echo to build an image. Used for scanning unborn babies (it is not ionising, so it is safe) and for finding cracks inside metal parts.

Seismic waves: after an earthquake, detectors around the world record the waves. S-waves are not detected on the opposite side of the Earth (an S-wave shadow zone), showing that part of the core is liquid. P-waves refract at the boundaries inside the Earth, which leaves a smaller P-wave shadow zone and lets scientists work out the size of the core.

Echo sounding (sonar): a ship sends a pulse of high frequency sound down. The echo from the seabed returns, and depth = (speed × time) ÷ 2, because the sound goes there and back.

#### Worked example

A sonar pulse returns to a ship 0.80 s after it was sent. Sound travels at 1500 m/s in seawater. Calculate the depth.

1. Total distance = 1500 × 0.80 = 1200 m.
2. Depth = 1200 ÷ 2 = 600 m.

#### Common mistakes

- Forgetting to halve the distance for an echo.

### 4.6.2.1 Types of electromagnetic waves

#### What you need to know

- Electromagnetic waves are transverse waves that transfer energy from the source of the waves to an absorber.
- Electromagnetic waves form a continuous spectrum and all types of electromagnetic wave travel at the same velocity through a vacuum (space) or air.
- The waves that form the electromagnetic spectrum are grouped in terms of their wavelength and their frequency. Going from long to short wavelength (or from low to high frequency) the groups are: radio, microwave, infrared, visible light (red to violet), ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays.
- Our eyes only detect visible light and so detect a limited range of electromagnetic waves.
- You should be able to give examples that illustrate the transfer of energy by electromagnetic waves.

#### Explained

A way to remember the order from long wavelength to short: Radio, Microwave, Infrared, Visible, Ultraviolet, X-ray, Gamma. Make up your own phrase with those initials (R M I V U X G).

Speed in a vacuum is about 3 × 10⁸ m/s for all of them. Since v = fλ and v is fixed, a shorter wavelength means a higher frequency.

Examples of energy transfer: the Sun warms your skin (infrared), a microwave oven heats food, a radio transmitter sends energy to an aerial.

#### Common mistakes

- Thinking different EM waves travel at different speeds in a vacuum. They all travel at the same speed.

### 4.6.2.2 Properties of electromagnetic waves 1

#### What you need to know

- Different substances may absorb, transmit, refract or reflect electromagnetic waves in ways that vary with wavelength.
- (HT only) Some effects, for example refraction, are due to the difference in velocity of the waves in different substances.
- You should be able to construct ray diagrams to illustrate the refraction of a wave at the boundary between two different media.
- (HT only) You should be able to use wave front diagrams to explain refraction in terms of the change of speed that happens when a wave travels from one medium to a different medium.

#### Explained

Glass transmits visible light but absorbs much ultraviolet. Skin absorbs ultraviolet. Soft tissue transmits X-rays, but bone absorbs them.

Refraction ray diagram: a ray going from air into glass at an angle bends towards the normal. Going from glass into air, it bends away from the normal. A ray along the normal goes straight through without bending.

(HT only) Wavefront explanation: imagine wavefronts (lines joining the peaks) hitting a glass block at an angle. One end of each wavefront enters the glass first and slows down, while the other end is still in air moving faster. This makes the wavefront swing round and change direction. The wavefronts get closer together in the glass because the wavelength is shorter at the lower speed (frequency stays the same).

#### Common mistakes

- Saying light bends away from the normal when it enters glass. It bends towards the normal when it slows down.

#### Practical

Required practical 10: investigating infrared absorption and emission by different surfaces.

1. Use a Leslie cube (a hollow metal cube with four different faces: matt black, shiny black, matt white and shiny silver).
2. Fill it with very hot water from a kettle and put the lid on.
3. Use an infrared detector (or a thermometer with a blackened bulb) held the same distance from each face, and record the reading for each face.
4. Repeat the readings to check they are repeatable.

Result: the matt black face emits the most infrared and the shiny silver face the least.

Absorption version: stand two identical metal plates, one matt black and one shiny silver, at equal distances from a radiant heater, with a drawing pin stuck on the back of each with wax. The pin on the matt black plate falls first because it absorbs infrared faster.

Control: same distance from the cube, same starting water temperature, same time after filling. Safety: take care with boiling water; do not touch the cube.

### 4.6.2.3 Properties of electromagnetic waves 2

#### What you need to know

- (HT only) Radio waves can be produced by oscillations in electrical circuits.
- (HT only) When radio waves are absorbed they may create an alternating current with the same frequency as the radio wave itself, so radio waves can themselves induce oscillations in an electrical circuit.
- Changes in atoms and the nuclei of atoms can result in electromagnetic waves being generated or absorbed over a wide frequency range. Gamma rays originate from changes in the nucleus of an atom.
- Ultraviolet waves, X-rays and gamma rays can have hazardous effects on human body tissue. The effects depend on the type of radiation and the size of the dose. Radiation dose (in sieverts) is a measure of the risk of harm resulting from an exposure of the body to the radiation. 1000 millisieverts (mSv) = 1 sievert (Sv).
- You should be able to draw conclusions from given data about the risks and consequences of exposure to radiation.
- Ultraviolet waves can cause skin to age prematurely and increase the risk of skin cancer. X-rays and gamma rays are ionising radiation that can cause the mutation of genes and cancer.

#### Explained

(HT only) In a radio transmitter, an alternating current in the aerial makes electrons oscillate, which gives out radio waves of the same frequency. In a receiving aerial the radio waves make the electrons oscillate, giving a tiny alternating current of the same frequency that the radio picks up.

Data on dose: a chest X-ray gives about 0.02 mSv, while the average UK background dose is a few mSv a year. A question may ask you to compare doses like these and judge whether a scan is worth the risk.

#### Common mistakes

- Calling ultraviolet harmless because it is not ionising like X-rays. It still damages skin and eyes.

### 4.6.2.4 Uses and applications of electromagnetic waves

#### What you need to know

- Electromagnetic waves have many practical applications. For example:
  - radio waves: television and radio
  - microwaves: satellite communications, cooking food
  - infrared: electrical heaters, cooking food, infrared cameras
  - visible light: fibre optic communications
  - ultraviolet: energy efficient lamps, sun tanning
  - X-rays and gamma rays: medical imaging and treatments.
- (HT only) You should be able to give brief explanations why each type of electromagnetic wave is suitable for the practical application.

#### Explained

(HT only)

| Wave | Use | Why it suits the use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Radio | TV and radio broadcasting | Long wavelengths travel long distances, bend around hills and reflect off the upper atmosphere |
| Microwave | Satellite communication | Passes through the atmosphere to satellites in space |
| Microwave | Cooking | Absorbed by water molecules in food, heating them |
| Infrared | Heaters, grills, thermal cameras | Absorbed by surfaces and turned into thermal energy; hot objects give out more infrared, so cameras can show temperature |
| Visible light | Optical fibres | Reflects along the inside of glass fibres without much loss |
| Ultraviolet | Energy-efficient lamps, sunbeds | Some substances absorb UV and give out visible light (fluorescence); UV makes skin produce darker pigment |
| X-rays | Medical images | Pass through soft tissue but are absorbed by bone |
| Gamma | Treating cancer, sterilising equipment | High energy, kills cells and microbes |

#### Common mistakes

- Saying microwaves cook food from the inside out. They are absorbed by water molecules in the outer layers, and energy is conducted inwards.

### 4.6.2.5 Lenses (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- A lens forms an image by refracting light. In a convex lens, parallel rays of light are brought to a focus at the principal focus. The distance from the lens to the principal focus is called the focal length.
- Ray diagrams are used to show the formation of images by convex and concave lenses.
- The image produced by a convex lens can be either real or virtual. The image produced by a concave lens is always virtual.
- You should be able to construct ray diagrams to illustrate the similarities and differences between convex and concave lenses.

```latex
\text{magnification} = \frac{\text{image height}}{\text{object height}}
```

- Magnification has no units. Image height and object height should both be in the same units, for example mm or cm. This equation is on the equation sheet.
- In ray diagrams a convex lens is represented by a vertical line with outward-facing arrows at each end, and a concave lens by a vertical line with inward-facing arrows.

#### Explained

Convex (converging) lens: thicker in the middle. It bends parallel rays inwards to meet at the principal focus.

Concave (diverging) lens: thinner in the middle. It spreads parallel rays out, so they seem to come from a principal focus on the same side as the light came from.

A real image is formed where rays actually meet; it can be shown on a screen and is upside down. A virtual image is where rays only seem to come from; it cannot be put on a screen and is the right way up.

Drawing a convex lens image (object beyond the principal focus):

1. Draw a ray from the top of the object parallel to the axis; after the lens it passes through the principal focus on the far side.
2. Draw a ray from the top of the object through the centre of the lens; it goes straight on.
3. Where they cross is the top of the image. It is real and inverted.

If the object is closer to a convex lens than the principal focus, the rays spread out after the lens. Tracing them back gives a virtual, upright, magnified image on the same side as the object (a magnifying glass).

For a concave lens the image is always virtual, upright and smaller than the object.

#### Worked example

An object 2.0 cm tall forms an image 7.0 cm tall. Magnification = 7.0 ÷ 2.0 = 3.5.

#### Common mistakes

- Giving magnification a unit.
- Mixing up the lens symbols. Convex has arrows pointing outwards.

### 4.6.2.6 Visible light (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- Each colour within the visible light spectrum has its own narrow band of wavelength and frequency.
- Reflection from a smooth surface in a single direction is called specular reflection. Reflection from a rough surface causes scattering: this is called diffuse reflection.
- Colour filters work by absorbing certain wavelengths (and colour) and transmitting other wavelengths (and colour).
- The colour of an opaque object is determined by which wavelengths of light are more strongly reflected. Wavelengths that are not reflected are absorbed. If all wavelengths are reflected equally the object appears white. If all wavelengths are absorbed the objects appears black.
- Objects that transmit light are either transparent or translucent.
- You should be able to explain how the colour of an object is related to the differential absorption, transmission and reflection of different wavelengths of light by the object.

#### Explained

Red light has the longest wavelength of visible light; violet has the shortest.

A red jumper in white light reflects red and absorbs the other colours, so it looks red. In blue light, there is no red to reflect, so it looks black.

A green filter transmits green and absorbs other colours. Shine white light through it and only green comes out. Shine red light at it and nothing comes through.

Transparent materials (clear glass) let light through without scattering, so you see clearly through them. Translucent materials (frosted glass) let light through but scatter it, so you cannot see a clear image.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a filter "adds" colour. It takes colours away by absorbing them.

### 4.6.3.1 Emission and absorption of infrared radiation (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- All bodies (objects), no matter what temperature, emit and absorb infrared radiation. The hotter the body, the more infrared radiation it radiates in a given time.
- A perfect black body is an object that absorbs all of the radiation incident on it. A black body does not reflect or transmit any radiation. Since a good absorber is also a good emitter, a perfect black body would be the best possible emitter.

#### Explained

Even an ice cube emits infrared, just much less than a hot iron. A thermal camera uses this to show warm areas.

Matt black surfaces are close to black bodies: good absorbers and good emitters. Shiny silver surfaces are poor absorbers and poor emitters, which is why emergency blankets are shiny and silver.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying cold objects give out no infrared. All objects emit some.

### 4.6.3.2 Perfect black bodies and radiation (Physics only)

#### What you need to know

- All bodies emit radiation. The intensity and wavelength distribution of any emission depends on the temperature of the body.
- (HT only) A body at constant temperature is absorbing radiation at the same rate as it is emitting radiation. The temperature of a body increases when the body absorbs radiation faster than it emits radiation.
- (HT only) The temperature of the Earth depends on many factors including: the rates of absorption and emission of radiation, and reflection of radiation into space.
- You should be able to explain how the temperature of a body is related to the balance between incoming radiation absorbed and radiation emitted, using everyday examples to illustrate this balance, and the example of the factors which determine the temperature of the Earth.
- (HT only) You should be able to use information, or draw or interpret diagrams to show how radiation affects the temperature of the Earth's surface and atmosphere.

#### Explained

A hotter object gives out more radiation overall, and the peak of its emission moves to shorter wavelengths. A metal bar glows dull red, then orange, then white-hot as it heats up.

(HT only) A cup of tea cools because it emits more than it absorbs from the cooler room. It stops cooling at room temperature, when absorption and emission are equal.

(HT only) The Earth: radiation from the Sun (mainly visible and short-wavelength infrared) passes through the atmosphere. Some is reflected by clouds, ice and snow back to space. The rest is absorbed, warming the surface. The warm surface emits longer-wavelength infrared. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere absorb some of this and re-emit it in all directions, including back down. The Earth's temperature stays steady when the total rate of absorption equals the total rate of emission; more greenhouse gases or less reflective ice upset this balance and the temperature rises.

#### Common mistakes

- Saying a body at constant temperature emits no radiation. It emits and absorbs at equal rates.

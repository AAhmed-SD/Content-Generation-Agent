# Thermodynamics: Born–Haber cycles, solutions, entropy and free energy

This file extends energetics (file 4). It covers lattice enthalpy and Born–Haber cycles, the perfect ionic model and polarisation, enthalpies of solution and hydration, entropy, and Gibbs free energy, which decides whether a reaction is feasible.

## Overview

A-level only. You need Hess's law and the enthalpy definitions from file 4.

## 3.1.8.1 Born–Haber cycles

*Refs: AQA 3.1.8.1 · Edexcel 13.1 to 13.11 · OCR A 5.2.1(a) to (e) · OCR B O(a) to (c) · Eduqas PI4.1(a) to (d). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Definitions: enthalpy of atomisation, ionisation energy, electron affinity, lattice enthalpy (formation and dissociation), enthalpy of hydration, enthalpy of solution.
- Construct Born–Haber cycles and use them to calculate any one missing quantity.
- Compare lattice enthalpies from Born–Haber cycles with values from the perfect ionic model to judge covalent character.
- Use cycles of solution, lattice and hydration enthalpies.
- Effect of ionic charge and radius on lattice and hydration enthalpies.

### Explained

**Definitions.**

| Term | Definition | Sign |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enthalpy of atomisation ΔatH | Forming 1 mol of gaseous atoms from the element in its standard state | + |
| First ionisation energy | Removing 1 electron from each atom in 1 mol of gaseous atoms | + |
| First electron affinity | Adding 1 electron to each atom in 1 mol of gaseous atoms to form 1 mol of gaseous 1− ions | − (usually) |
| Second electron affinity | Adding 1 electron to each ion in 1 mol of gaseous 1− ions | + (repulsion between ion and electron) |
| Lattice enthalpy of formation ΔLEH | 1 mol of ionic solid formed from its gaseous ions | − |
| Lattice enthalpy of dissociation | 1 mol of ionic solid separated into gaseous ions | + |
| Enthalpy of hydration ΔhydH | 1 mol of gaseous ions dissolved in water to form 1 mol of aqueous ions | − |
| Enthalpy of solution ΔsolH | 1 mol of solute dissolved in enough solvent that the ions are separated and do not interact | + or − |

Example equations for NaCl: Na(s) → Na(g); ½Cl₂(g) → Cl(g); Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻; Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g); Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g) → NaCl(s).

**Born–Haber cycle for NaCl** (lattice formation). Going round from the elements:

ΔfH(NaCl) = ΔatH(Na) + ΔatH(Cl) + IE₁(Na) + EA₁(Cl) + ΔLEH(NaCl)

Draw it with upward arrows for endothermic steps and downward for exothermic steps, keeping electrons and all species written on each level.

**Factors affecting lattice enthalpy.** Larger charges and smaller ions give a more exothermic lattice enthalpy of formation, because the ions attract more strongly and are closer. MgO (−3791 kJ mol⁻¹) is far more exothermic than NaCl (−787 kJ mol⁻¹).

**Perfect ionic model and covalent character.** A theoretical lattice enthalpy can be calculated assuming perfectly spherical ions and purely ionic bonding. If the Born–Haber (experimental) value is more exothermic than the theoretical one, the bonding has some covalent character. This happens when a small, highly charged cation **polarises** (distorts) a large anion, pulling its electron cloud towards itself.

- **Polarising power** of a cation increases with higher charge and smaller radius (high charge density). Al³⁺ and Mg²⁺ are strongly polarising.
- **Polarisability** of an anion increases with larger radius and higher charge. I⁻ is more polarisable than F⁻.

For NaCl the two values agree closely (nearly fully ionic). For AgI or MgI₂ the experimental value is noticeably more exothermic.

**Solution cycles.** Dissolving an ionic solid can be thought of as breaking up the lattice into gaseous ions then hydrating them:

ΔsolH = ΔLEH(dissociation) + ΣΔhydH

or, with lattice enthalpy of formation, ΔsolH = −ΔLEH(formation) + ΣΔhydH.

**Hydration enthalpy** is more exothermic for smaller and more highly charged ions, because they attract the polar water molecules more strongly (water's δ− oxygen faces cations; δ+ hydrogens face anions).

**Solubility (OCR B, Eduqas).** Whether a salt dissolves depends on the balance of lattice and hydration enthalpies, and on entropy (dissolving usually increases entropy). A small or negative ΔsolH favours dissolving. The solubility trend of Group 2 hydroxides and sulfates (file 13) comes from how lattice and hydration enthalpies change with ion size.

### Worked example

1. Find the lattice enthalpy of formation of potassium chloride. Data (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔfH(KCl) −437, ΔatH(K) +89, ΔatH(Cl) +122, IE₁(K) +419, EA₁(Cl) −349.

   ΔLEH = −437 − (89 + 122 + 419 − 349) = −437 − 281 = −718 kJ mol⁻¹.

2. Find ΔsolH of MgCl₂. Lattice enthalpy of formation −2526; ΔhydH: Mg²⁺ −1921, Cl⁻ −364 (kJ mol⁻¹).

   ΔsolH = +2526 + (−1921 + 2(−364)) = +2526 − 2649 = −123 kJ mol⁻¹.

3. For MgO, the cycle needs two electron affinities of oxygen: O(g) + e⁻ → O⁻(g) (−141) and O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g) (+798), and two ionisation energies of magnesium. The second electron affinity is positive because an electron is being added to an ion that is already negative.

### Common mistakes

- Forgetting that atomisation of Cl₂ gives one Cl per ½Cl₂. Use the enthalpy for one mole of Cl atoms; for MgCl₂, double it.
- Using the bond enthalpy of Cl₂ (242) instead of ΔatH (121) without halving.
- Mixing up lattice formation and dissociation signs.
- Saying a more exothermic experimental lattice enthalpy means "more ionic". It means some covalent character.

## 3.1.8.2 Gibbs free-energy change and entropy change

*Refs: AQA 3.1.8.2 · Edexcel 13.12 to 13.22 · OCR A 5.2.2(a) to (f) · OCR B O(d) to (h) · Eduqas PI4.2(a) to (e). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Entropy, S, is a measure of disorder: the number of ways particles and their energy can be arranged. Units J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
- Entropy increases from solid to liquid to gas, with more moles of gas, with dissolving, and with temperature.
- ΔS = ΣS(products) − ΣS(reactants).
- ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. A reaction is feasible when ΔG ≤ 0.
- Find the temperature at which a reaction becomes feasible.
- Edexcel: ΔS(total) = ΔS(system) + ΔS(surroundings); ΔS(surroundings) = −ΔH ÷ T; ΔG = −RT ln K.
- Feasible reactions may not happen because of a high activation energy (kinetic stability).
- OCR B: solubility product, Ksp.

### Explained

**Why entropy.** Some endothermic reactions happen on their own (ice melting above 0 °C, ammonium nitrate dissolving), so ΔH alone cannot decide feasibility. Systems tend towards more disordered states because there are more ways to arrange them.

All substances above 0 K have positive entropy. A perfect crystal at 0 K has zero entropy.

**Predicting the sign of ΔS.** Look for a change of state or a change in gas moles. CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g): a gas forms, so ΔS is positive.

**Gibbs free energy.** ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, with ΔH in kJ mol⁻¹, so convert ΔS to kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (divide by 1000) and use T in K.

| ΔH | ΔS | Feasible? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| − | + | Always |
| + | − | Never |
| − | − | At low temperature (below T = ΔH/ΔS) |
| + | + | At high temperature (above T = ΔH/ΔS) |

The temperature where feasibility changes is when ΔG = 0, so T = ΔH ÷ ΔS.

A plot of ΔG against T is a straight line with gradient −ΔS and intercept ΔH.

**Total entropy (Edexcel).** ΔS(total) = ΔS(system) + ΔS(surroundings). An exothermic reaction releases heat, which increases the entropy of the surroundings: ΔS(surroundings) = −ΔH ÷ T. A reaction is feasible when ΔS(total) is positive. Since ΔG = −TΔS(total), the two approaches agree.

**ΔG and K (Edexcel).** ΔG = −RT ln K. A negative ΔG means K > 1 (products favoured); a very negative ΔG means the reaction goes almost to completion. A positive ΔG means K < 1.

**Limits.** ΔG says whether a reaction can happen, not how fast. Diamond turning into graphite has negative ΔG but is extremely slow because of a large activation energy. ΔH and ΔS also change slightly with temperature, so calculated feasibility temperatures are approximate. The calculation also assumes standard conditions.

**Solubility product (OCR B O(h)).** For a sparingly soluble salt in a saturated solution, AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq), Ksp = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻]. For CaF₂, Ksp = [Ca²⁺][F⁻]². If the product of the ion concentrations in a mixture exceeds Ksp, a precipitate forms. Adding a common ion (for example Cl⁻ to a saturated AgCl solution) lowers the solubility.

### Worked example

1. For CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g), ΔH = +178 kJ mol⁻¹. Entropies (J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹): CaCO₃ 93, CaO 40, CO₂ 214. Find ΔS, ΔG at 298 K, and the minimum temperature for decomposition.

   ΔS = (40 + 214) − 93 = +161 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 0.161 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

   ΔG(298) = 178 − 298 × 0.161 = 178 − 48.0 = +130 kJ mol⁻¹, not feasible.

   T = 178 ÷ 0.161 = 1106 K.

2. (Edexcel) Find ΔS(surroundings) at 298 K for a reaction with ΔH = −92 kJ mol⁻¹.

   ΔS(surr) = −(−92 000) ÷ 298 = +309 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

3. (Edexcel) ΔG for a reaction at 298 K is −20.0 kJ mol⁻¹. Find K.

   ln K = −ΔG ÷ RT = 20 000 ÷ (8.31 × 298) = 8.08. K = e⁸·⁰⁸ = 3.2 × 10³.

4. (OCR B) Ksp of BaSO₄ is 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁰ mol² dm⁻⁶. Find its solubility in mol dm⁻³.

   [Ba²⁺] = [SO₄²⁻] = s. s² = 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁰, s = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.

### Common mistakes

- Not converting ΔS from J to kJ.
- Using °C for T.
- Claiming a reaction with negative ΔG must happen quickly.
- Forgetting that elements have non-zero standard entropies (unlike ΔfH).

## Check questions

1. Define first electron affinity and explain why the second electron affinity of oxygen is endothermic. (3 marks)
2. Calculate the lattice enthalpy of formation of sodium oxide, Na₂O. Data (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔfH(Na₂O) −414, ΔatH(Na) +107, ΔatH(O) +249, IE₁(Na) +496, EA₁(O) −141, EA₂(O) +798. (3 marks)
3. Explain why the lattice enthalpy of formation of MgO is much more exothermic than that of NaF. (2 marks)
4. The experimental lattice enthalpy of silver iodide is more exothermic than the value from the perfect ionic model. Explain. (3 marks)
5. Explain why the hydration enthalpy of Mg²⁺ is more exothermic than that of Na⁺. (2 marks)
6. Predict, with a reason, the sign of ΔS for N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g). (1 mark)
7. For N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g), ΔH = −92 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS = −199 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Find the temperature above which the reaction is not feasible. (2 marks)
8. Explain why a reaction with a negative ΔG may not take place at room temperature. (2 marks)
9. Ammonium nitrate dissolves in water endothermically but spontaneously. Explain in terms of entropy. (2 marks)

## Mark scheme

1. Enthalpy change when one electron is added to each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms (1) to form one mole of gaseous 1− ions (1). Second: electron added to a negative ion, so repulsion must be overcome (1).
2. ΔLEH = −414 − [2(107) + 249 + 2(496) − 141 + 798] (1) = −414 − 2112 (1) = −2526 kJ mol⁻¹ (1).
3. Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ have larger charges than Na⁺ and F⁻ (1); stronger attraction between ions (and similar or smaller ions) (1).
4. Ag⁺ polarises the large I⁻ ion / I⁻ is easily polarised (1); electron density distorted towards Ag⁺ (1); so some covalent character / extra attraction not in the ionic model (1).
5. Mg²⁺ is smaller and has a higher charge (higher charge density) (1); attracts δ− oxygen of water more strongly (1).
6. Negative: 4 mol of gas form 2 mol of gas (fewer gas moles / less disorder) (1).
7. T = ΔH/ΔS = −92 ÷ −0.199 (1) = 462 K (1).
8. High activation energy (1); so reaction very slow / kinetically stable (1).
9. ΔS(system) is large and positive: ordered lattice becomes free aqueous ions (1); TΔS is greater than ΔH, so ΔG is negative (1).

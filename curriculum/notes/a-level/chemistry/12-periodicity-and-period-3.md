# Periodicity and the Period 3 elements and their oxides

The periodic table repeats its patterns every period. This file covers how the table is arranged, the s, p and d blocks, trends in atomic radius, ionisation energy and melting point across Period 3, and (AQA A-level) the reactions of the Period 3 elements with water and oxygen and the acid–base nature of their oxides.

## Overview

Periodicity is AS. The reactions of Period 3 elements and oxides are AQA A-level only. Ionisation energy explanations are in file 1; structure and bonding types are in file 3.

## 3.2.1.1 Classification

*Refs: AQA 3.2.1.1 · Edexcel 1.24 · OCR A 3.1.1(a), (b) · OCR B EL(m), EL(o) · Eduqas C1.6(a), (b). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The periodic table is arranged in order of atomic (proton) number, in periods (rows) and groups (columns).
- Elements are classified into s, p, d (and f) blocks by the sub-shell of their highest-energy electron.
- Elements in a group have the same outer electron configuration and similar chemistry.

### Explained

Mendeleev arranged elements by atomic mass but swapped some (such as tellurium and iodine) to keep chemical families together, and left gaps for undiscovered elements, predicting their properties. When the gaps were filled (gallium, germanium), the predictions were confirmed. The modern table uses atomic number, which removes the anomalies.

- s block: Groups 1 and 2 (and He).
- p block: Groups 3 to 0 (13 to 18).
- d block: the transition metals, Sc to Zn in Period 4.
- f block: lanthanides and actinides.

The period number is the number of the outer shell; the group number (for s and p blocks) is the number of outer electrons.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the modern table is ordered by atomic mass.
- Putting helium in the p block. Its electrons are 1s².

## 3.2.1.2 Physical properties of Period 3 elements

*Refs: AQA 3.2.1.2 · Edexcel 1.25, 1.26 · OCR A 3.1.1(g) · OCR B EL(n) · Eduqas C1.6(d). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Trends in atomic radius, first ionisation energy and melting point across Period 3 (Na to Ar).
- Explain the melting point trend in terms of structure and bonding.

### Explained

**Atomic radius decreases** across the period. Nuclear charge increases; the electrons are added to the same shell with similar shielding; so the outer electrons are pulled closer.

**First ionisation energy** generally increases, with dips at Al and S (file 1).

**Electronegativity** increases across the period for the same reasons as radius decreases.

**Melting points:**

| Element | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P (P₄) | S (S₈) | Cl (Cl₂) | Ar |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Structure | Giant metallic | Giant metallic | Giant metallic | Giant covalent | Simple molecular | Simple molecular | Simple molecular | Atoms |
| m.p. / K | 371 | 923 | 933 | 1683 | 317 | 392 | 172 | 84 |

- Na to Al: metallic bonding gets stronger. Ions have charges 1+, 2+, 3+, are smaller, and there are 1, 2, 3 delocalised electrons per atom. Stronger attraction.
- Si: giant covalent (like diamond). Many strong covalent bonds must break, so the highest melting point.
- P₄, S₈, Cl₂, Ar: simple molecules held by London forces. The bigger the molecule (more electrons), the stronger the London forces. S₈ > P₄ > Cl₂ > Ar.

Period 2 shows the same pattern: Li, Be metallic; B, C (diamond/graphite) giant covalent with very high melting points; N₂, O₂, F₂, Ne simple molecular.

### Worked example

Explain why sulfur has a higher melting point than phosphorus.

Sulfur exists as S₈ molecules and phosphorus as P₄ molecules. S₈ has more electrons, so stronger London forces between molecules, which need more energy to overcome.

### Common mistakes

- Saying covalent bonds break when sulfur melts.
- Explaining silicon's high melting point with London forces.
- Saying atomic radius decreases because of "more shells". It is the same shell.

## 3.2.4 Properties of Period 3 elements and their oxides

*Refs: AQA 3.2.4. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Reactions of Na and Mg with water.
- Reactions of Na, Mg, Al, Si, P and S with oxygen, the formulae of the oxides and their structure and bonding.
- Trend in melting points of the oxides.
- Reactions of the oxides with water and the pH of the solutions.
- The acid–base character of the oxides; amphoteric aluminium oxide; equations for reactions with acids and bases.

### Explained

**Reactions with water.**

- Sodium reacts vigorously with cold water, fizzing and moving on the surface: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂. Solution pH about 14.
- Magnesium reacts very slowly with cold water: Mg + 2H₂O → Mg(OH)₂ + H₂. Mg(OH)₂ is only slightly soluble, so pH about 10. With steam it reacts quickly with a bright white flame: Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂.

**Reactions with oxygen.**

| Element | Observation | Oxide | Bonding and structure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Na | Yellow flame, white solid | Na₂O | Giant ionic |
| Mg | Brilliant white flame, white solid | MgO | Giant ionic |
| Al | White flame (powder), white solid | Al₂O₃ | Giant ionic with some covalent character |
| Si | Burns when heated strongly | SiO₂ | Giant covalent |
| P | Burns with a white flame, white smoke | P₄O₁₀ | Simple molecular |
| S | Blue flame, choking gas | SO₂ (some SO₃ with a catalyst) | Simple molecular |

**Melting points of oxides.** MgO is highest of the ionic oxides (2+ and 2− ions; strong attraction), higher than Na₂O. Al₂O₃ is lower than expected for 3+ ions because of covalent character. SiO₂ is high (giant covalent). P₄O₁₀ and SO₂ are low (London and permanent dipole forces between molecules), with P₄O₁₀ higher because it is a larger molecule.

**Oxides with water.**

| Oxide | Reaction with water | pH |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Na₂O | Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH | 13 to 14 |
| MgO | MgO + H₂O → Mg(OH)₂ (slightly soluble) | 9 to 10 |
| Al₂O₃ | Insoluble | 7 |
| SiO₂ | Insoluble | 7 |
| P₄O₁₀ | P₄O₁₀ + 6H₂O → 4H₃PO₄ | 0 to 1 |
| SO₂ | SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃ | 2 to 3 |
| SO₃ | SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄ | 0 to 1 |

Ionic oxides contain O²⁻, which reacts with water as a base: O²⁻ + H₂O → 2OH⁻. Non-metal oxides are covalent and form acids.

**Acid–base character.**

- Basic: Na₂O, MgO. Na₂O + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O; MgO + H₂SO₄ → MgSO₄ + H₂O.
- Amphoteric: Al₂O₃ reacts with both.
  - With acid: Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O.
  - With base: Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH + 3H₂O → 2NaAl(OH)₄.
- Acidic: SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₂, SO₃.
  - SiO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SiO₃ + H₂O (only with hot concentrated alkali).
  - P₄O₁₀ + 12NaOH → 4Na₃PO₄ + 6H₂O.
  - SO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₃ + H₂O.
  - SO₃ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + H₂O.

**The structures of the acids.** H₃PO₄ has P bonded to one =O and three –OH groups; H₂SO₄ has S bonded to two =O and two –OH groups. The H⁺ ions come from the O–H groups. The anions (PO₄³⁻, SO₄²⁻, SO₃²⁻) are stabilised by charge spread over the oxygens.

### Common mistakes

- Saying Al₂O₃ is basic only. It is amphoteric.
- Writing SiO₂ + H₂O as a reaction. It does not react with water.
- Saying MgO solution has pH 14. Mg(OH)₂ is only slightly soluble.

## Check questions

1. Explain why atomic radius decreases from sodium to chlorine. (2 marks)
2. Explain why the melting point of aluminium is higher than that of sodium. (3 marks)
3. Explain why silicon has the highest melting point in Period 3. (2 marks)
4. Explain why argon has the lowest melting point in Period 3. (2 marks)
5. Write equations for the reactions of sodium with water and of magnesium with steam. (2 marks)
6. Give the formulae of the oxides of phosphorus and sulfur formed on burning in excess oxygen and state their type of structure. (2 marks)
7. Write equations to show that aluminium oxide is amphoteric. (2 marks)
8. Explain why MgO has a higher melting point than P₄O₁₀. (3 marks)

## Mark scheme

1. Nuclear charge / proton number increases (1); electrons in same shell, similar shielding, so greater attraction (1).
2. Al³⁺ has higher charge and is smaller than Na⁺ (1); three delocalised electrons per atom compared with one (1); stronger metallic bonding / attraction between ions and delocalised electrons (1).
3. Giant covalent structure (1); many strong covalent bonds to break (1).
4. Exists as single atoms with fewest electrons of the molecular elements (1); weakest London forces (1).
5. 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ (1); Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂ (1).
6. P₄O₁₀, SO₂ (and SO₃) (1); simple molecular (1).
7. Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O (1); Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH + 3H₂O → 2NaAl(OH)₄ (1).
8. MgO giant ionic (1); strong attraction between Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ in all directions (1); P₄O₁₀ simple molecular with weak intermolecular forces (1).
9. Na₂O: pH 13 to 14 (1); Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH (1). SO₂: pH 2 to 3 (1); SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃ (1).

# Atomic structure, isotopes, mass spectrometry and electron configuration

Everything in chemistry comes back to electrons: how many an atom has, where they sit, and how hard they are to remove. This file covers the particles in an atom, isotopes, the time of flight mass spectrometer, relative masses, electron configurations and ionisation energies.

## Overview

AS content for all boards. Ionisation energy trends across Period 3 and down groups appear again in file 12 (periodicity) and file 13 (Group 2). Mass spectrometry of organic molecules is in file 22. Atomic emission spectra and radioactivity are in file 33.

### Board differences

- AQA 3.1.1.1 to 3.1.1.3 name the time of flight (TOF) mass spectrometer and expect the four stages: ionisation, acceleration, ion drift, detection. They also expect the two ionisation methods: electron impact and electrospray.
- Edexcel 1.1 to 1.23 do not require the workings of the spectrometer but do ask for predicting the mass spectrum of Cl₂ including relative peak heights (1.9), and how emission spectra and ionisation energy data give evidence for shells and sub-shells (1.15).

## 3.1.1.1 Fundamental particles

*Refs: AQA 3.1.1.1 · Edexcel 1.1 to 1.4 · OCR A 2.1.1(b) · OCR B EL(a), EL(g) · Eduqas C1.3(a). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- An atom has a small, dense nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons in shells (energy levels).
- Relative charge and relative mass of the three particles.
- Atomic number Z = number of protons. Mass number A = protons + neutrons.
- How to work out the numbers of each particle in atoms and ions.
- The model of the atom has changed as new evidence appeared.

### Explained

| Particle | Relative charge | Relative mass | Where |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Proton | +1 | 1 | Nucleus |
| Neutron | 0 | 1 | Nucleus |
| Electron | −1 | 1/1840 (about 0.0005) | Shells around the nucleus |

Almost all the mass is in the nucleus, but almost all the volume is the space where the electrons are. A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons. An ion has gained or lost electrons; the number of protons never changes in a chemical reaction.

**Notation.** In ²³Na written with 23 at the top left and 11 at the bottom left, 23 is A and 11 is Z. Neutrons = A − Z = 12.

**For ions**, adjust only the electrons. ³²S²⁻ has 16 protons, 16 neutrons and 18 electrons. ²⁷Al³⁺ has 13 protons, 14 neutrons and 10 electrons.

**How the model developed.** Dalton pictured atoms as solid indivisible spheres. Thomson found the electron and proposed a "plum pudding" of negative electrons in a spread-out positive charge. Rutherford's gold foil experiment showed most alpha particles passed straight through but a few bounced back, so the positive charge and the mass must be in a tiny nucleus. Bohr put the electrons in fixed energy levels to explain line spectra. Chadwick later found the neutron. Each model was replaced because it could not explain new evidence. That is how science works: models are kept while they explain the data.

### Worked example

How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in ⁵⁶Fe³⁺ (Z = 26)?

- Protons = Z = 26.
- Neutrons = A − Z = 56 − 26 = 30.
- Electrons = 26 − 3 = 23 (a 3+ ion has lost three).

### Common mistakes

- Changing the number of protons when forming an ion. Only electrons change.
- Giving the electron a relative mass of 0. It is small (about 1/1840), not zero.
- Adding electrons for a positive ion. Positive means electrons were lost.

## 3.1.1.2 Mass number and isotopes

*Refs: AQA 3.1.1.2 · Edexcel 1.5 to 1.10 · OCR A 2.1.1(a), (c), (d), (e) · OCR B EL(a), EL(x) · Eduqas C1.3(a) to (c). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
- Isotopes have the same chemical properties (same electron configuration) but slightly different physical properties (mass, density, rate of diffusion).
- Relative isotopic mass and relative atomic mass are measured on the carbon-12 scale.
- AQA: the principles of a simple time of flight (TOF) mass spectrometer, limited to ionisation, acceleration to give all ions constant kinetic energy, ion drift, ion detection, and data analysis.
- Interpret simple mass spectra of elements; calculate relative atomic mass from isotopic abundance (and the reverse).
- Use mass spectrometry to identify elements, and to find the relative molecular mass from the molecular ion peak.

### Explained

**Definitions.**

- Relative isotopic mass: the mass of an atom of an isotope compared with one twelfth of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
- Relative atomic mass, Aᵣ: the weighted mean mass of an atom of an element compared with one twelfth of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.

The weighting is by abundance. That is why Aᵣ values are rarely whole numbers.

**The time of flight mass spectrometer (AQA).**

1. **Ionisation.** The sample is turned into positive ions. Two methods:
   - *Electron impact*: the sample is vaporised and high-energy electrons from an electron gun knock an electron off each particle: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁺(g) + 2e⁻. Used for elements and small molecules with low Mᵣ. It often breaks molecules into fragments.
   - *Electrospray ionisation*: the sample is dissolved in a volatile solvent and pushed through a fine, high-voltage needle. Each molecule gains a proton: X(g) + H⁺ → XH⁺(g). Used for large molecules such as proteins, because it causes little fragmentation. The peak you see is at Mᵣ + 1.
2. **Acceleration.** An electric field gives all the ions the same kinetic energy.
3. **Ion drift.** The ions travel through a flight tube with no field. Since KE = ½mv², lighter ions move faster and arrive first.
4. **Detection.** Ions hit a negatively charged plate and gain electrons. This makes a current proportional to the number of ions hitting it. The time of flight gives m/z; the current gives abundance.
5. **Data analysis.** A computer plots relative abundance against m/z.

Time of flight comes from KE = ½mv² and v = d/t, so t = d√(m/2KE). Heavier ions take longer.

**Why ions?** Only charged particles are accelerated by the field and only charged particles make a current at the detector.

**Diatomic molecules (Edexcel, Eduqas).** Chlorine has two isotopes, ³⁵Cl (about 75%) and ³⁷Cl (about 25%). Cl₂ gives three molecular ion peaks:

| Molecule | m/z | Probability | Ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ³⁵Cl–³⁵Cl | 70 | 0.75 × 0.75 = 0.5625 | 9 |
| ³⁵Cl–³⁷Cl (either order) | 72 | 2 × 0.75 × 0.25 = 0.375 | 6 |
| ³⁷Cl–³⁷Cl | 74 | 0.25 × 0.25 = 0.0625 | 1 |

There are also peaks at 35 and 37 (ratio 3 : 1) from Cl⁺ ions formed when the molecule splits.

**Relative molecular mass from a mass spectrum.** For a molecular substance, the peak with the highest m/z (ignoring small isotope peaks) is the molecular ion, M⁺. Its m/z equals Mᵣ, because the charge is +1.

**Relative molecular mass and relative formula mass.** Mᵣ is the sum of Aᵣ values for a simple molecule. For giant ionic compounds such as NaCl, use "relative formula mass" because there are no molecules.

### Worked example

1. A sample of boron gives peaks at m/z 10 (relative abundance 19.9) and m/z 11 (80.1). Find Aᵣ.

   Aᵣ = (10 × 19.9 + 11 × 80.1) ÷ (19.9 + 80.1) = (199 + 881.1) ÷ 100 = 10.8.

2. Copper has Aᵣ = 63.55 and two isotopes, ⁶³Cu and ⁶⁵Cu. Find the percentage of ⁶³Cu.

   Let x = % of ⁶³Cu. 63x + 65(100 − x) = 6355. 6500 − 2x = 6355, so x = 72.5%.

3. In a TOF spectrometer, a ²⁴Mg⁺ ion takes 1.20 × 10⁻⁵ s to cross the flight tube. How long does a ²⁶Mg⁺ ion take, given both have the same kinetic energy?

   t ∝ √m, so t = 1.20 × 10⁻⁵ × √(26 / 24) = 1.25 × 10⁻⁵ s.

### Common mistakes

- Defining relative atomic mass without "weighted mean" or without "one twelfth of carbon-12".
- Saying isotopes have different chemical properties. Same electron configuration, same reactions.
- Saying ions are deflected by a magnetic field in the TOF spectrometer. In the TOF design they are separated by time, not deflection.
- Forgetting that electrospray gives MH⁺, so the peak is at Mᵣ + 1.
- Missing the factor of 2 for the mixed ³⁵Cl–³⁷Cl molecule.

## 3.1.1.3 Electron configuration

*Refs: AQA 3.1.1.3 · Edexcel 1.11 to 1.23 · OCR A 2.2.1(a) to (d), 3.1.1(c) · OCR B EL(e), EL(f), EL(q) · Eduqas C1.2(e) to (g). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Electron configurations of atoms and ions up to Z = 36 in terms of shells and sub-shells (orbitals) s, p and d.
- Define first ionisation energy; write equations for first and successive ionisation energies.
- How first and successive ionisation energies give evidence for electron configuration and the group of an element.
- Trends in first ionisation energy across Period 3 and down Group 2 (AQA), across Periods 2 and 3 and down a group (other boards).
- Shapes of s and p orbitals; orbitals hold up to two electrons with opposite spins; electrons fill singly before pairing.

### Explained

**Shells, sub-shells and orbitals.**

| Shell n | Sub-shells | Orbitals | Max electrons |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1s | 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 2s, 2p | 1 + 3 | 8 |
| 3 | 3s, 3p, 3d | 1 + 3 + 5 | 18 |
| 4 | 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f | 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 | 32 |

An orbital is a region around the nucleus that can hold up to two electrons with opposite spins. An s orbital is spherical. A p orbital is dumbbell shaped; there are three at right angles (pₓ, p_y, p_z). A d sub-shell has five orbitals.

**Filling order.** 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p. The 4s fills before 3d because it is lower in energy when empty. In a sub-shell, electrons occupy orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing (pairing costs energy because paired electrons repel).

Examples:

- Na (11): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹, or [Ne] 3s¹.
- Fe (26): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s².
- **Chromium and copper are exceptions.** Cr: [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹. Cu: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹. A half-full or full 3d sub-shell is more stable.
- **Transition metal ions lose 4s first.** Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶, not [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s².

**Electrons in boxes.** Draw a box per orbital and an arrow per electron, arrows in a pair pointing opposite ways. Nitrogen 2p is three boxes each with one up arrow.

**Blocks.** The sub-shell holding the highest-energy electron gives the block: s block (Groups 1, 2), p block (Groups 3 to 0), d block (transition metals). The configuration decides the chemistry, which is why elements in a group behave alike.

**Ionisation energy.**

First ionisation energy: the energy needed to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.

Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻

Second ionisation energy: Na⁺(g) → Na²⁺(g) + e⁻. Always include (g).

Three things decide ionisation energy:

1. **Nuclear charge.** More protons, stronger attraction.
2. **Distance from nucleus (atomic radius).** Further out, weaker attraction.
3. **Shielding.** Inner electrons repel the outer electron and cut the attraction it feels.

**Successive ionisation energies** always increase, because each electron is removed from an increasingly positive ion, and the remaining electrons are pulled closer. A big jump shows the next electron comes from a shell nearer the nucleus. Count the electrons before the first big jump to find the group.

For aluminium the first three are fairly close; there is a huge jump between the 3rd and 4th. So Al is in Group 3.

**Trend down a group.** First ionisation energy decreases. The outer electron is in a shell further from the nucleus and more shielded. The extra protons are outweighed.

**Trend across a period.** First ionisation energy generally increases. Nuclear charge rises, the electron is in the same shell with similar shielding, so attraction increases and atomic radius falls.

**The two dips across Period 3 (and Period 2):**

- **Mg to Al (Be to B):** the drop happens because Al's outer electron is in a 3p orbital, higher in energy and slightly further out than 3s, and partly shielded by the 3s electrons. This is evidence for sub-shells.
- **P to S (N to O):** sulfur's fourth 3p electron goes into an orbital that already holds one. The two electrons in the same orbital repel, so one is easier to remove. This is evidence for electrons pairing in orbitals.

Helium has the highest first ionisation energy of all; noble gases are at the peaks.

**Evidence for shells and sub-shells (Edexcel 1.15, OCR B).** Successive ionisation energies on a log scale fall into groups (2, 8, 8, 2 for calcium), which shows shells. The pattern of first ionisation energies across a period, with the dips above, shows sub-shells. Line emission spectra (file 33) show electrons occupy fixed energy levels.

### Worked example

1. Write the configuration of Mn²⁺ (Mn is Z = 25).

   Mn: [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s². Remove the two 4s electrons: Mn²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁵.

2. Successive ionisation energies (kJ mol⁻¹) of element X: 738, 1451, 7733, 10 541, 13 630. Which group?

   The jump is between the 2nd (1451) and 3rd (7733). Two electrons in the outer shell, so Group 2 (X is magnesium).

3. Write the equation for the third ionisation energy of aluminium.

   Al²⁺(g) → Al³⁺(g) + e⁻

### Common mistakes

- Writing 4s electrons after 3d is full for every element. Remember Cr and Cu.
- Removing 3d electrons before 4s when forming transition metal ions.
- Leaving out (g) or writing X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g) (that is electron affinity, not ionisation).
- Explaining the Mg/Al drop by "full sub-shell is stable". Say the 3p electron is higher in energy and shielded by 3s.
- Explaining the P/S drop by "half-full sub-shell is stable". Say the paired electrons repel.
- Saying shielding increases across a period. It stays about the same: the new electrons go into the same shell.

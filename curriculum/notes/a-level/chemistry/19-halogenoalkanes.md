# Halogenoalkanes: substitution, elimination and ozone depletion

Halogenoalkanes have a polar carbon–halogen bond that attracts nucleophiles. This file covers their classification, nucleophilic substitution with hydroxide, cyanide and ammonia, the effect of bond enthalpy on rate, SN1 and SN2 mechanisms, elimination to form alkenes, the competition between the two, uses, and how CFCs destroy ozone.

## 3.3.3.1 Nucleophilic substitution

*Refs: AQA 3.3.3.1 · Edexcel 6.30 to 6.36, CP4 · OCR A 4.2.2(a) to (d) · OCR B OZ(j) to (n) · Eduqas C3.3(b) to (e), (g). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Halogenoalkanes contain a polar C–X bond; the carbon is δ+.
- Primary, secondary and tertiary classification.
- Nucleophilic substitution with OH⁻ (aqueous), CN⁻ (ethanolic) and NH₃ (excess, ethanolic, under pressure); mechanisms.
- Reactivity depends on C–X bond enthalpy: C–I is the weakest and reacts fastest, even though C–F is the most polar.
- SN1 and SN2 (Edexcel, OCR B): tertiary halogenoalkanes react by SN1, primary by SN2.

### Explained

**Classification.** Primary: the carbon bonded to X is attached to one other carbon. Secondary: two. Tertiary: three. 1-bromobutane is primary, 2-bromobutane secondary, 2-bromo-2-methylpropane tertiary.

**Physical properties (OCR B).** Boiling points are higher than alkanes of similar size because of permanent dipole–dipole forces, and rise from F to I (more electrons, stronger London forces). They do not mix with water because they cannot form hydrogen bonds with it.

**Mechanism (SN2) with hydroxide.** CH₃CH₂Br + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂OH + Br⁻.

1. The lone pair on OH⁻ is attracted to the δ+ carbon. Curly arrow from the lone pair on O to the carbon.
2. At the same time the C–Br bond breaks heterolytically. Curly arrow from the C–Br bond to Br.
3. Products: ethanol and Br⁻.

Conditions: warm aqueous NaOH or KOH, under reflux. This is also called hydrolysis.

**With cyanide.** CH₃CH₂Br + CN⁻ → CH₃CH₂CN + Br⁻. Conditions: KCN in ethanol, warm under reflux. Product: propanenitrile. The carbon chain grows by one carbon, which is useful in synthesis (file 29). The lone pair is on the carbon of C≡N⁻.

**With ammonia.** CH₃CH₂Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Br. Conditions: excess ammonia in ethanol, heated in a sealed tube (under pressure). Mechanism: the lone pair on N attacks the δ+ carbon; Br⁻ leaves; the intermediate CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺ loses a proton to a second NH₃ molecule. Excess ammonia reduces further substitution (the amine product is itself a nucleophile, file 26).

**Rate and bond enthalpy.**

| Bond | Bond enthalpy / kJ mol⁻¹ | Polarity | Rate of hydrolysis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C–F | 467 | Most polar | Slowest (almost none) |
| C–Cl | 346 | | Slow |
| C–Br | 290 | | Faster |
| C–I | 228 | Least polar | Fastest |

Polarity alone would predict C–F reacts fastest. In fact the rate is decided by how easily the C–X bond breaks, so bond enthalpy is the dominant factor.

**Comparing rates experimentally (OCR A 4.2.2(d), Edexcel CP4).** Add a few drops of each halogenoalkane to ethanol (a common solvent for water and the halogenoalkane) in test tubes in a water bath at 50 °C. Add warm aqueous silver nitrate and start timing. Water acts as the nucleophile, releasing X⁻, which forms a silver halide precipitate. Time how long until a precipitate appears: iodo (yellow, fastest), bromo (cream), chloro (white, slowest).

R–X + H₂O → R–OH + H⁺ + X⁻; Ag⁺ + X⁻ → AgX(s).

**SN1 and SN2 (Edexcel 6.36, OCR B OZ(m)).**

- **SN2** (substitution, nucleophilic, bimolecular): one step, the nucleophile attacks as the halide leaves. Rate = k[RX][OH⁻]. Typical of primary halogenoalkanes. The transition state has five groups around carbon.
- **SN1** (unimolecular): two steps. The C–X bond breaks first to give a carbocation (slow, rate-determining), then the nucleophile attacks (fast). Rate = k[RX]. Typical of tertiary halogenoalkanes because the three alkyl groups push electrons towards the positive carbon (positive inductive effect), stabilising the tertiary carbocation, and because they crowd the carbon so a nucleophile cannot reach it from the back.
- Secondary halogenoalkanes react by both.

So the order of reactivity in hydrolysis is tertiary > secondary > primary (Edexcel 6.34).

**Uses (Eduqas C3.3(e)).** Solvents (dichloromethane in paint stripping and decaffeinating); anaesthetics (halothane, CF₃CHBrCl); refrigerants and aerosol propellants (CFCs, now replaced by HFCs and hydrocarbons); fire retardants; making PVC and PTFE.

### Practical

**Eduqas: nucleophilic substitution**, for example warming 1-bromobutane with aqueous sodium hydroxide under reflux to make butan-1-ol. Reflux (Eduqas C3.3(g)): heat the mixture in a round-bottomed flask with anti-bumping granules and a vertical condenser, so vapours condense and drip back; the reaction can be heated for a long time without losing volatile reactants or products. Then distil the product (file 21).

### Common mistakes

- Curly arrow from the negative charge instead of the lone pair of OH⁻.
- Showing the C–Br bond breaking towards carbon.
- Saying C–F reacts fastest because it is most polar.
- Using aqueous KCN (it gives some hydrolysis to alcohol); use ethanolic KCN.

## 3.3.3.2 Elimination

*Refs: AQA 3.3.3.2 · Edexcel 6.32 · Eduqas C3.3(a). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- OH⁻ can act as a base and remove HX to form an alkene.
- Conditions decide whether substitution or elimination dominates.
- The elimination mechanism.

### Explained

CH₃CHBrCH₃ + KOH → CH₂=CHCH₃ + KBr + H₂O. Conditions: KOH dissolved in ethanol, heated under reflux.

**Mechanism.**

1. The lone pair on OH⁻ attacks an H atom on the carbon next to the C–Br carbon (acting as a base). Curly arrow from the lone pair to that H.
2. The C–H bond electrons move to form the C=C. Curly arrow from the C–H bond to between the two carbons.
3. The C–Br bond breaks and Br⁻ leaves. Curly arrow from the C–Br bond to Br.

**Substitution or elimination?**

| Favours substitution | Favours elimination |
| --- | --- |
| Aqueous solvent | Ethanolic solvent |
| Lower temperature | Higher temperature |
| Primary halogenoalkane | Tertiary halogenoalkane |

OH⁻ in water acts mainly as a nucleophile; OH⁻ in ethanol acts mainly as a base.

**Isomers.** Eliminating HBr from 2-bromobutane gives but-1-ene and but-2-ene (as E and Z isomers): three alkenes.

### Common mistakes

- Attacking the carbon bonded to the halogen in elimination. The base removes an H from the neighbouring carbon.
- Forgetting the extra alkene isomers.

## 3.3.3.3 Ozone depletion

*Refs: AQA 3.3.3.3 · OCR A 4.2.2(e) · Eduqas C3.3(f). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Ozone in the upper atmosphere absorbs harmful UV radiation.
- CFCs break down in UV to give Cl• radicals that catalyse ozone decomposition.
- Equations for the chain reaction; NO• radicals do the same (OCR A).
- Legislation to ban CFCs, and alternatives (HFCs).

### Explained

**Ozone is useful.** In the stratosphere, ozone absorbs UV-B and UV-C radiation, which would otherwise cause skin cancer, cataracts and crop damage. It forms and breaks down naturally: O₂ → 2O (UV); O + O₂ → O₃; O₃ → O₂ + O (UV).

**CFCs** (chlorofluorocarbons) are very unreactive, so they last long enough to reach the stratosphere. There, UV light breaks the C–Cl bond (weaker than C–F):

CF₂Cl₂ → •CF₂Cl + Cl•

Cl• catalyses ozone breakdown:

Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂
ClO• + O₃ → 2O₂ + Cl•

Overall: 2O₃ → 3O₂. Cl• is regenerated, so one radical can destroy many thousands of ozone molecules before termination.

**NO• radicals** from aircraft and lightning act the same way: NO• + O₃ → NO₂• + O₂; NO₂• + O → NO• + O₂ (overall O₃ + O → 2O₂).

**Action taken.** The Montreal Protocol (1987) phased out CFCs. Chemists developed replacements: HFCs (hydrofluorocarbons such as CH₂FCF₃), which contain no C–Cl bonds, and hydrocarbons such as butane for aerosols and refrigerators. HFCs are, however, greenhouse gases. The ozone layer is slowly recovering. This is an example of scientists' evidence informing legislation.

### Common mistakes

- Saying CFCs break down because C–F bonds break. C–Cl breaks; C–F is too strong.
- Writing Cl⁻ instead of Cl•.
- Confusing ozone depletion with global warming.

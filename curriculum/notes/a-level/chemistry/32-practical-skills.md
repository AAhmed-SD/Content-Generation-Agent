# Practical skills, apparatus, errors and data handling

Every board examines practical skills in the written papers and assesses a practical endorsement. This file covers planning, carrying out experiments, recording and analysing results, uncertainties and errors, and evaluation, and lists the apparatus and techniques students must use. The individual required practicals are in the topic files.

## Overview

Applies across the whole course. The practical activities for each board are listed in `map.md` with the file that covers each one.

### Board differences

- AQA: section 5 (practical assessment, apparatus and techniques AT a to l, practical skills) and section 6 (mathematical requirements). Required practicals RP1 to RP12 are in files 2, 4, 5, 9, 10, 11, 14, 16, 21, 22, 24 and 31.
- Edexcel: practical skills and mathematical skills in the appendices; core practicals CP1 to CP16 in files 2, 4, 9, 10, 11, 14, 15, 16, 19, 21 and 24.
- OCR A and OCR B: Module 1 (1.1.1 Planning, 1.1.2 Implementing, 1.1.3 Analysis, 1.1.4 Evaluation, 1.2.1 Practical skills for the endorsement, 1.2.2 Use of apparatus and techniques). Practical activity groups PAG1 to PAG12 sit in the topic files.
- Eduqas: estimating percentage error (C1.3(l)), the specified practical work listed through the topics, and Component 3, Chemistry in practice, a practical examination.

## 1.1.1 Planning

*Refs: OCR A 1.1.1(a) to (c) · OCR B 1.1.1(a) to (c) · AQA Section 5 · Edexcel Appendix · Eduqas Component 3. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Design experiments to test a hypothesis, including choosing apparatus, techniques and quantities.
- Identify independent, dependent and control variables.
- Assess risks and plan to reduce them.

### Explained

**Variables.** The independent variable is the one you change. The dependent variable is the one you measure. Control variables are kept constant so the test is fair.

**Choosing apparatus.** Pick apparatus with the right precision for the quantity:

| Quantity | Apparatus | Typical uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mass | Two-decimal balance | ±0.005 g (one reading); ±0.01 g by difference |
| Volume (fixed, accurate) | Pipette (25 cm³) | ±0.06 cm³ |
| Volume (variable) | Burette | ±0.05 cm³ per reading; ±0.10 cm³ for a titre |
| Volume (approximate) | Measuring cylinder | ±0.5 cm³ (100 cm³ cylinder) |
| Volume of solution made up | Volumetric flask (250 cm³) | ±0.2 cm³ |
| Gas volume | Gas syringe | ±0.5 cm³ |
| Temperature | Thermometer | ±0.5 °C (1 °C divisions) |
| Time | Stopwatch | Limited by reaction time, about ±0.5 s |

**Risk assessment.** For each hazard, say the risk and how to reduce it: flammable organic liquids (no naked flames, use a water bath or heating mantle); corrosive acids and alkalis (goggles, gloves, dilute where possible); toxic gases (fume cupboard); harmful or irritant chemicals (gloves, avoid skin contact); hot apparatus (allow to cool, tongs).

### Common mistakes

- Using a measuring cylinder when an accurate volume is needed.
- Writing "be careful" as a safety precaution. Name the hazard and the specific control.

## 1.1.2 Implementing

*Refs: OCR A 1.1.2(a) to (c) · OCR B 1.1.2(a) to (c) · AQA Section 5. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Use apparatus and techniques correctly and safely.
- Record results in tables with headings and units, to an appropriate precision.
- Use correct units and significant figures.

### Explained

**Results tables.** Independent variable in the first column. Column headings include the quantity and unit separated by a slash, for example "Volume of NaOH / cm³". Units go in the heading, not in each cell. Record all readings to the same precision, matching the apparatus (burette readings to 2 decimal places, ending in 0 or 5).

**Significant figures.** Give answers to the same number of significant figures as the least precise data used. Keep full values during calculation and round at the end.

**Units.** Know standard units: mol, mol dm⁻³, g mol⁻¹, kJ mol⁻¹, J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, Pa, m³, K, s⁻¹. Convert prefixes: kilo (10³), centi (10⁻²), milli (10⁻³), micro (10⁻⁶), nano (10⁻⁹).

## 1.1.3 Analysis

*Refs: OCR A 1.1.3(a) to (d) · OCR B 1.1.3(a) to (d) · AQA Section 6 · Edexcel Appendix · Eduqas C1.3(l). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Process data: means, rates, percentage yield, gradients, intercepts.
- Plot graphs with suitable scales and lines of best fit; tangents to curves.
- Calculate uncertainties and percentage uncertainties; combine them.
- Identify anomalies.

### Explained

**Graphs.** Independent variable on the x-axis. Use more than half the grid. Label axes with quantity and unit. Plot points accurately (small crosses). Draw a smooth line or curve of best fit with points evenly spread either side; ignore anomalies but identify them. For rates, draw a tangent and find its gradient with a large triangle.

**Uncertainty.** The uncertainty in a reading is usually half the smallest division. If two readings are needed (a titre is a final minus an initial reading, a temperature change is two readings), the uncertainties add.

Percentage uncertainty = (absolute uncertainty ÷ measured value) × 100.

For a calculated result that multiplies or divides measured quantities, add the percentage uncertainties.

**Reducing percentage uncertainty.** Measure a larger quantity: use a larger mass, a larger titre (more dilute titrant), or a bigger temperature change.

**Percentage error** compares an experimental result with an accepted value: (|experimental − accepted| ÷ accepted) × 100. If the percentage error is larger than the total percentage uncertainty, there are systematic errors as well.

### Worked example

A titre is 23.40 cm³ (burette ±0.05 cm³ per reading). The pipetted volume is 25.0 cm³ (±0.06 cm³). Find the percentage uncertainty in each, and the total.

Titre: ±0.10 ÷ 23.40 × 100 = 0.43%. Pipette: 0.06 ÷ 25.0 × 100 = 0.24%. Total = 0.67%.

## 1.1.4 Evaluation

*Refs: OCR A 1.1.4(a) to (e) · OCR B 1.1.4(a) to (e) · AQA Section 5. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Evaluate results and draw conclusions.
- Identify random and systematic errors and suggest improvements.
- Accuracy, precision, repeatability and reproducibility.

### Explained

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Accurate | Close to the true value |
| Precise | Repeated readings close to each other (small spread) |
| Repeatable | Same person, same method and equipment, gets similar results |
| Reproducible | Different people or methods get similar results |
| Random error | Unpredictable variation (reading a scale, judging an end point); reduced by repeating and averaging |
| Systematic error | Same direction and size each time (heat loss, a zero error, an uncalibrated meter); not reduced by repeating |

**Improving an experiment.** Name the specific problem and its fix: heat loss → insulate and add a lid, use extrapolation; gas escapes before the bung is fitted → put the solid in a small tube inside the flask and tip it over after sealing; end point hard to judge → use a colorimeter or a white tile; volatile product lost → use a condenser.

### Common mistakes

- Saying "human error". Name the error.
- Saying repeating improves accuracy. It improves precision and reduces random error.

## 1.2.1 Practical skills (practical endorsement)

*Refs: OCR A 1.2.1(a) to (j) · OCR B 1.2.1(a) to (j) · AQA Section 5 · Edexcel Appendix · Eduqas Component 3. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

The endorsement (common to all boards) is assessed against five competencies across at least 12 practical activities:

1. Follows written procedures.
2. Applies investigative approaches and methods when using instruments and equipment.
3. Safely uses a range of practical equipment and materials.
4. Makes and records observations.
5. Researches, references and reports.

Students keep a record of their practical work (a lab book). The endorsement is reported as Pass or Not classified, separately from the grade.

### Explained

Good lab records include the date, aim, method followed (and any changes), risk assessment, raw results with units, calculations, conclusions and references to sources used (with full details for websites and books).

## Check questions

1. A student weighs 0.250 g of solid on a balance reading to ±0.001 g, by difference. Calculate the percentage uncertainty. (2 marks)
2. A temperature rises from 21.5 °C to 27.0 °C, measured with a thermometer reading to ±0.5 °C. Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the temperature change and suggest how it could be reduced. (3 marks)
3. Explain the difference between accuracy and precision. (2 marks)
4. Identify one systematic error in measuring the enthalpy of combustion of ethanol with a spirit burner, and suggest an improvement. (2 marks)
5. Give the correct heading for a column of burette readings, and state the precision they should be recorded to. (2 marks)
6. A student's value for Ka was 1.9 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³; the accepted value is 1.74 × 10⁻⁵. The total uncertainty was 3%. Calculate the percentage error and comment. (3 marks)
7. Explain why a 250 cm³ measuring cylinder is unsuitable for making a standard solution. (1 mark)

## Mark scheme

1. Uncertainty = 2 × 0.001 = 0.002 g (1); 0.002 ÷ 0.250 × 100 = 0.8% (1).
2. ΔT = 5.5 °C, uncertainty ±1.0 °C (1); 18% (1); use more concentrated reagents / larger mass to give a larger temperature change, or a more precise thermometer (1).
3. Accuracy: closeness to the true value (1); precision: closeness of repeated values to each other (1).
4. Heat loss to surroundings / incomplete combustion (1); use a draught shield / lid / insulated can / bomb calorimeter / ensure good oxygen supply (1).
5. "Burette reading / cm³" (1); to 0.05 cm³, two decimal places (1).
6. (1.9 − 1.74) ÷ 1.74 × 100 = 9.2% (1)(1 for working); larger than the 3% uncertainty, so systematic errors present / method not accurate (1).
7. Measuring cylinder not precise enough / uncertainty too large compared with a volumetric flask (1).

# Introduction to organic chemistry: names, formulae, mechanisms and isomerism

Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds. This file sets up the language used in every later organic file: types of formula, homologous series and functional groups, IUPAC naming, types of reaction and bond fission, curly arrows, and structural and E/Z isomerism.

## Overview

AS content. Optical isomerism is in file 23 (A-level).

### Board differences

- AQA 3.3.1.1 to 3.3.1.3.
- Edexcel 6.1 to 6.7: includes classifying reactions as addition, elimination, substitution, oxidation, reduction and polymerisation (6.5), and E/Z and cis–trans isomerism with CIP rules (6.7).
- OCR A 4.1.1(a) to (i) and 4.1.3(c), (d): adds the terms aliphatic, alicyclic and aromatic, and homolytic and heterolytic fission.
- OCR B DF(b), (c), (l), (m), (r) to (t) and WM(a): σ and π bonds and shapes in organic molecules, formula types, structural and stereoisomerism, and the main homologous series.
- Eduqas C3.1(a) to (e) and C3.2(f): formulae, naming, effect of chain length and functional groups on physical properties, structural isomerism, electrophiles, nucleophiles and radicals, E/Z isomerism.

## 3.3.1.1 Nomenclature

*Refs: AQA 3.3.1.1 · Edexcel 6.1 to 6.4 · OCR A 4.1.1(a) to (d) · OCR B DF(l), DF(m), DF(r), WM(a) · Eduqas C3.1(a) to (c). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Types of formula: empirical, molecular, general, structural, displayed, skeletal.
- Homologous series and functional groups.
- IUPAC naming of compounds with up to six carbons in the main chain (larger ones for some boards), with one or more functional groups.
- Terms: saturated, unsaturated, aliphatic, alicyclic, aromatic.

### Explained

**Formulae for butan-1-ol.**

| Type | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Molecular | C₄H₁₀O | Actual number of atoms |
| Empirical | C₄H₁₀O | Simplest ratio |
| General | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH | Algebraic formula for the series |
| Structural | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH | Arrangement of atoms, carbon by carbon |
| Displayed | All atoms and bonds drawn | Every bond shown |
| Skeletal | Zig-zag line ending in OH | Carbon skeleton; C and H on carbon not shown; functional groups shown |

**Homologous series**: a family with the same functional group and general formula; each member differs by CH₂; similar chemical properties and a gradual trend in physical properties. **Functional group**: the group of atoms responsible for the characteristic reactions.

**Other terms.** Saturated: only C–C single bonds. Unsaturated: contains C=C (or C≡C). Aliphatic: carbon atoms in straight or branched chains or non-aromatic rings. Alicyclic: aliphatic arranged in a ring (cyclohexane). Aromatic: contains a benzene ring.

**Naming.**

1. Find the longest carbon chain containing the main functional group: stem meth (1), eth (2), prop (3), but (4), pent (5), hex (6).
2. Add the suffix or prefix for the functional group, with a number for its position, giving the lowest numbers.
3. Name side chains as prefixes (methyl, ethyl) with positions; list prefixes alphabetically; use di, tri for repeats.

| Series | Functional group | Prefix / suffix | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alkane | C–C | -ane | propane |
| Alkene | C=C | -ene | but-2-ene |
| Halogenoalkane | –X | chloro-, bromo-, iodo- | 2-bromopropane |
| Alcohol | –OH | -ol (hydroxy-) | propan-2-ol |
| Aldehyde | –CHO | -al | ethanal |
| Ketone | C=O in the chain | -one | butanone |
| Carboxylic acid | –COOH | -oic acid | propanoic acid |
| Ester | –COO– | -yl -oate | methyl ethanoate |
| Acyl chloride | –COCl | -oyl chloride | ethanoyl chloride |
| Amine | –NH₂ | -amine (amino-) | ethylamine / ethanamine |
| Nitrile | –C≡N | -nitrile | propanenitrile (C counted) |
| Amide | –CONH₂ | -amide | ethanamide |

When there are two groups, the higher-priority one gives the suffix: carboxylic acid > ester > acyl chloride > amide > nitrile > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol > amine > alkene > halogenoalkane. For example, HOCH₂CH₂COOH is 3-hydroxypropanoic acid.

**Physical properties (Eduqas C3.1(c)).** Boiling points rise with chain length (more London forces) and fall with branching. Polar functional groups raise boiling points (dipole–dipole forces), and –OH, –COOH and –NH₂ much more so (hydrogen bonds). Short-chain alcohols, acids and amines dissolve in water; solubility falls as the chain grows.

### Worked example

Name CH₃CH(CH₃)CH(Cl)CH₃.

Longest chain: 4 carbons (butane). Substituents: methyl and chloro on carbons 2 and 3, or 3 and 2. Lowest set: 2,3 either way; alphabetical first gets the lower number when there is a tie: 2-chloro-3-methylbutane.

### Common mistakes

- Choosing a chain that is not the longest one through the functional group.
- Writing commas and hyphens wrongly: numbers are separated by commas, numbers and words by hyphens (2,3-dimethylpentane).
- Counting the carbon of –CHO, –COOH or –CN as extra to the chain. It is part of the chain.

## 3.3.1.2 Reaction mechanisms

*Refs: AQA 3.3.1.2 · Edexcel 6.5 · OCR A 4.1.1(f) to (i) · OCR B DF(b) · Eduqas C3.1(e). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Reactions are classified as addition, elimination, substitution, oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis, condensation and polymerisation.
- Homolytic fission forms radicals; heterolytic fission forms ions.
- A radical has an unpaired electron; a nucleophile is an electron pair donor; an electrophile is an electron pair acceptor.
- Curly arrows show the movement of an electron pair. Half-headed arrows (fish hooks) show one electron (not required by all boards).
- σ and π bonds (OCR B, also file 20).

### Explained

**Bond fission.**

- **Homolytic:** each atom keeps one electron of the bond: Cl–Cl → 2Cl•. Needs energy, often UV light. Gives radicals, which are very reactive.
- **Heterolytic:** one atom takes both electrons: H–Br → H⁺ + Br⁻. Gives ions; the more electronegative atom takes the pair.

**Reagent types.**

| Type | Definition | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nucleophile | Donates a lone pair to form a bond; attracted to δ+ carbon | OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃, H₂O |
| Electrophile | Accepts a lone pair; attracted to areas of high electron density | H⁺, NO₂⁺, Br₂ (polarised), carbocations |
| Radical | Species with an unpaired electron | Cl•, CH₃• |

**Curly arrows.** An arrow starts from a lone pair or a bond (the source of the electron pair) and ends where the new bond forms or on the atom that receives the pair. Show the relevant lone pairs, δ+ and δ− charges and the full charges of ions.

**σ and π bonds (OCR B DF(b)).** A σ bond is formed by head-on overlap of orbitals between two nuclei. A π bond is formed by sideways overlap of p orbitals above and below the axis. A single bond is one σ bond; a double bond is one σ and one π bond. Shapes in organic molecules: carbon with four single bonds is tetrahedral (109.5°); carbon in C=C or C=O is trigonal planar (120°); carbon in C≡C or C≡N is linear (180°).

**Types of reaction.**

| Type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Addition | CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br |
| Substitution | CH₃CH₂Br + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂OH + Br⁻ |
| Elimination | CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O |
| Oxidation | CH₃CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃CHO + H₂O |
| Reduction | CH₃CHO + 2[H] → CH₃CH₂OH |
| Hydrolysis | Ester + water → acid + alcohol |
| Condensation | Two molecules join, losing a small molecule such as water |
| Polymerisation | Many monomers join |

### Common mistakes

- Starting a curly arrow from an atom or a charge instead of a lone pair or bond.
- Calling Cl⁻ a radical.
- Confusing nucleophile (attacks δ+ carbon) and electrophile (attacks the C=C electrons).

## 3.3.1.3 Isomerism

*Refs: AQA 3.3.1.3 · Edexcel 6.6, 6.7 · OCR A 4.1.1(e), 4.1.3(c), (d) · OCR B DF(c), DF(s), DF(t) · Eduqas C3.1(d), C3.2(f). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but different structural formulae: chain, position and functional group isomers.
- Stereoisomers have the same structural formula but a different arrangement of atoms in space.
- E/Z isomerism arises from restricted rotation about C=C with two different groups on each carbon.
- Cahn–Ingold–Prelog (CIP) priority rules.
- Cis–trans as a special case of E/Z when one group on each carbon is the same.

### Explained

**Structural isomers of C₄H₁₀O** (examples):

- Chain: butan-1-ol and 2-methylpropan-1-ol.
- Position: butan-1-ol and butan-2-ol.
- Functional group: butan-1-ol and ethoxyethane (an ether, C₂H₅OC₂H₅).

Other functional group pairs: aldehydes and ketones (propanal and propanone), carboxylic acids and esters (propanoic acid and methyl ethanoate), alkenes and cycloalkanes (but-1-ene and cyclobutane).

**E/Z isomerism.** The π bond stops rotation about the C=C. If each carbon of the double bond has two different groups, two isomers exist.

**CIP rules.**

1. On each carbon of the C=C, look at the two atoms directly attached. The atom with the higher atomic number has priority.
2. If they are the same, move along each chain to the first point of difference.
3. If the two higher-priority groups are on the same side, the isomer is Z (zusammen, together). If on opposite sides, it is E (entgegen, opposite).

Example: CH₃CH=CHCl (1-chloropropene). On C1: Cl (17) beats H (1). On C2: CH₃ (C, 6) beats H. If Cl and CH₃ are on the same side, it is Z.

But-2-ene: cis-but-2-ene is Z (both CH₃ on the same side). When the groups compared are the same on both carbons, cis = Z and trans = E. With different groups (for example 1-bromo-2-chloro-1-fluoroethene), cis/trans cannot be used and E/Z is needed.

E/Z isomers can have different physical properties: Z-but-2-ene boils at 4 °C, E-but-2-ene at 1 °C.

### Worked example

How many structural isomers are there with the formula C₅H₁₂? Draw and name them.

Three: pentane; 2-methylbutane; 2,2-dimethylpropane.

### Common mistakes

- Claiming E/Z isomers exist for but-1-ene (C1 has two H atoms).
- Using mass of the whole group instead of the atomic number of the first atom attached.
- Drawing the same molecule twice rotated and calling them isomers.

## Check questions

1. Give the skeletal formula and the name of CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH₃. (2 marks)
2. Name (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CHO. (1 mark)
3. Define the term homologous series. (2 marks)
4. Explain the difference between homolytic and heterolytic fission, giving an example of each. (4 marks)
5. Classify each reagent as nucleophile, electrophile or radical: NH₃, NO₂⁺, Br•, CN⁻. (2 marks)
6. Draw and name a chain isomer and a position isomer of pentan-1-ol. (2 marks)
7. Explain why pent-2-ene has E/Z isomers but pent-1-ene does not. (2 marks)
8. Assign E or Z to the isomer of 2-bromobut-2-ene in which Br and the CH₃ on carbon 3 are on the same side. Explain using CIP rules. (3 marks)
9. State the number of σ and π bonds in a molecule of propene. (2 marks)

## Mark scheme

1. Skeletal zig-zag of four carbons with OH on carbon 2 (1); butan-2-ol (1).
2. 3-methylbutanal (1).
3. Same functional group and general formula (1); each member differs by CH₂; similar chemical properties / trend in physical properties (1).
4. Homolytic: each atom gets one electron of the bonding pair, forming radicals (1); e.g. Cl₂ → 2Cl• in UV light (1). Heterolytic: one atom gets both electrons, forming ions (1); e.g. HBr → H⁺ + Br⁻ / C–Br bond in nucleophilic substitution (1).
5. NH₃ and CN⁻ nucleophiles (1); NO₂⁺ electrophile, Br• radical (1).
6. Chain: e.g. 2-methylbutan-1-ol / 3-methylbutan-1-ol (1). Position: pentan-2-ol / pentan-3-ol (1).
7. Restricted rotation about C=C (1); in pent-1-ene carbon 1 has two identical groups (H, H), so no isomers; in pent-2-ene each carbon has two different groups (1).
8. On C2: Br has higher priority than CH₃ (1); on C3: CH₃ has higher priority than H (1); same side, so Z (1).
9. 8 σ bonds (6 C–H, 2 C–C) (1); 1 π bond (1).

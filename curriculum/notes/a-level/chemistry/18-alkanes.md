# Alkanes: crude oil, cracking, combustion and radical substitution

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons and the main fuels. This file covers their structure and properties, fractional distillation of crude oil, thermal and catalytic cracking, complete and incomplete combustion, pollutants and catalytic converters, alternative fuels, and the free-radical substitution of alkanes by chlorine.

## 3.3.2.1 Fractional distillation of crude oil

*Refs: AQA 3.3.2.1 · Edexcel 6.8 to 6.10 · OCR A 4.1.2(a) to (d) · Eduqas C3.2(b). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons, general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ (cycloalkanes CₙH₂ₙ).
- Each carbon is tetrahedral with σ bonds; bond angle 109.5°.
- Crude oil is a mixture mostly of alkanes, separated by fractional distillation.
- Boiling points rise with chain length; branched chains boil lower.
- Alkanes are unreactive: C–C and C–H bonds are strong and non-polar.

### Explained

**Fractional distillation.** Crude oil is heated until most of it vaporises and fed into the bottom of a fractionating column, which is hot at the bottom and cooler at the top. Vapours rise; each fraction condenses at the level where the temperature falls below its boiling point and is drawn off. Short-chain alkanes (low boiling point) leave at the top as gases (refinery gas); long-chain ones condense lower (petrol, naphtha, kerosene, diesel, then fuel oil); the residue (bitumen) leaves at the bottom.

**Boiling point trend.** Longer chains have more electrons and more surface contact, so stronger London forces. Branched isomers cannot pack as closely, so have fewer points of contact and weaker London forces: 2,2-dimethylpropane boils at 10 °C; pentane at 36 °C.

**Reactivity.** Alkanes have strong C–C and C–H σ bonds with almost no polarity (C and H have similar electronegativities), so they do not attract nucleophiles or electrophiles. They burn and react with halogens in UV light.

**Reforming (Edexcel 6.10).** Straight-chain alkanes are converted to branched alkanes and cyclic hydrocarbons (including arenes) using a platinum catalyst. These burn more smoothly in engines (less knocking).

### Common mistakes

- Saying fractions boil off at the top. Each condenses at its level.
- Explaining boiling points with "stronger bonds". The C–C bonds do not break when an alkane boils.

## 3.3.2.2 Modification of alkanes by cracking

*Refs: AQA 3.3.2.2 · Edexcel 6.10 · OCR B DF(j) · Eduqas C3.2(a). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Cracking breaks C–C bonds in long-chain alkanes to make shorter, more useful alkanes and alkenes.
- Thermal cracking: high pressure and high temperature; mainly alkenes.
- Catalytic cracking: slight pressure, high temperature, zeolite catalyst; mainly motor fuels and aromatic hydrocarbons.
- Economic reasons for cracking.

### Explained

Fractional distillation gives more long-chain fractions than are needed, and too few short-chain ones (petrol, which is in high demand). Cracking matches supply to demand and also makes alkenes, which are feedstocks for polymers and other chemicals.

| | Thermal cracking | Catalytic cracking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conditions | 700 to 1200 K, up to 7000 kPa | About 720 K, slight pressure, zeolite catalyst |
| Main products | High proportion of alkenes | Branched alkanes, cycloalkanes, aromatics: motor fuels |
| Mechanism | Radicals | Carbocations (not needed) |

Example: C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄. There must be at least one alkene, because an alkane has the highest H : C ratio.

**Catalysts (OCR B DF(j)).** Zeolites are aluminosilicates with a honeycomb structure and a large surface area. They lower the temperature needed, saving energy.

### Common mistakes

- Writing a cracking equation with only alkane products.
- Mixing up the conditions of the two types.

## 3.3.2.3 Combustion of alkanes

*Refs: AQA 3.3.2.3 · Edexcel 6.11 to 6.14 · OCR A 4.1.2(e) · OCR B DF(k), DF(n), DF(u) · Eduqas C3.2(a). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Alkanes are used as fuels.
- Complete and incomplete combustion; products and their problems.
- Pollutants: CO, NOₓ, SO₂, carbon particulates, unburnt hydrocarbons.
- Catalytic converters remove CO, NOₓ and unburnt hydrocarbons.
- SO₂ removal from flue gases using CaO or CaCO₃.
- Edexcel: alternative fuels (biodiesel, alcohols) and carbon neutrality.

### Explained

**Complete combustion** (plenty of oxygen): C₈H₁₈ + 12½O₂ → 8CO₂ + 9H₂O.

**Incomplete combustion** (limited oxygen): C₈H₁₈ + 8½O₂ → 8CO + 9H₂O; or soot: CH₄ + O₂ → C + 2H₂O.

**Pollutants.**

| Pollutant | Source | Problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CO | Incomplete combustion | Toxic: binds to haemoglobin, reducing oxygen transport |
| Carbon particulates (soot) | Incomplete combustion | Breathing problems, cancer, global dimming |
| Unburnt hydrocarbons | Engines | Photochemical smog, with NOₓ |
| NOₓ | N₂ and O₂ react at the high temperature in engines: N₂ + O₂ → 2NO; 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂ | Acid rain, photochemical smog, toxic |
| SO₂ | Sulfur impurities in fuels | Acid rain: SO₂ + ½O₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₄ |
| CO₂ | All combustion | Greenhouse gas: global warming (file 35) |

**Catalytic converters** in car exhausts: honeycomb ceramic coated in platinum, palladium and rhodium.

- 2CO + 2NO → 2CO₂ + N₂
- C₈H₁₈ + 25NO → 8CO₂ + 12½N₂ + 9H₂O
- 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂

**Flue gas desulfurisation.** In power stations, SO₂ is removed by reacting with calcium oxide or calcium carbonate slurry: CaO + SO₂ → CaSO₃; CaCO₃ + SO₂ → CaSO₃ + CO₂. Calcium sulfite is oxidised to calcium sulfate (gypsum), used for plasterboard.

**Alternative fuels (Edexcel 6.14, OCR B DF(u)).**

- **Biodiesel** is made by reacting vegetable oils (triglycerides) with methanol to form methyl esters. **Bioethanol** is made by fermenting sugars.
- They are renewable. They are described as carbon neutral because the CO₂ released when they burn was absorbed by the plants as they grew. In practice they are not fully carbon neutral, because fossil fuels are used in farming, fertiliser production, processing and transport.
- Drawbacks: they need land that could grow food, may lead to deforestation, and need large amounts of water and fertiliser.
- Hydrogen is another option (file 10).

### Common mistakes

- Saying catalytic converters remove CO₂. They make CO₂.
- Saying NOₓ comes from nitrogen in the fuel. It comes from nitrogen in the air.
- Calling biofuels completely carbon neutral without qualification.

## 3.3.2.4 Chlorination of alkanes

*Refs: AQA 3.3.2.4 · Edexcel 6.15 to 6.17 · OCR A 4.1.2(f), (g) · OCR B OZ(o), OZ(p) · Eduqas C3.2(c). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Alkanes react with chlorine (or bromine) in UV light by free-radical substitution.
- The mechanism: initiation, propagation, termination.
- The reaction gives a mixture of products, which limits its use in synthesis.

### Explained

**Mechanism for methane and chlorine** (overall CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl).

*Initiation.* UV light breaks the Cl–Cl bond by homolytic fission:

Cl₂ → 2Cl•

*Propagation.* A radical is used and a new one is made, so the chain continues:

Cl• + CH₄ → HCl + •CH₃
•CH₃ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•

*Termination.* Two radicals join; radicals are removed:

Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂
•CH₃ + Cl• → CH₃Cl
•CH₃ + •CH₃ → C₂H₆

Why UV? The Cl–Cl bond (243 kJ mol⁻¹) is weaker than C–H (413), so UV light has enough energy to break Cl–Cl. Bromine reacts in the same way.

**Further substitution.** With excess chlorine, CH₃Cl reacts further:

Cl• + CH₃Cl → HCl + •CH₂Cl
•CH₂Cl + Cl₂ → CH₂Cl₂ + Cl•

and so on to CHCl₃ and CCl₄.

**Limitations.**

- A mixture of mono-, di-, tri- and tetrachloroalkanes forms.
- For longer alkanes, substitution happens at any carbon, giving position isomers (propane gives both 1-chloropropane and 2-chloropropane).
- Termination makes longer chains (ethane from methane).
- Products must be separated by fractional distillation, so yield of any one product is low. Using excess alkane favours the monosubstituted product.

### Common mistakes

- Showing H• as a product of propagation. The Cl• takes an H atom to form HCl.
- Writing initiation with a curly arrow drawn as a full arrow for both electrons; homolytic fission uses half-headed arrows if arrows are drawn.
- Forgetting to write the dot for the unpaired electron.

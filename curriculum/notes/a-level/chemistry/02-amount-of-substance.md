# Amount of substance, formulae, equations and titrations

The mole lets chemists count particles by weighing them. This file covers relative masses, the mole, gas volumes and the ideal gas equation, empirical and molecular formulae, balanced equations, concentration, percentage yield, atom economy and titrations. It includes AQA required practical 1.

## Overview

AS content for every board. Almost every calculation later in the course (energetics, equilibria, rates, acids) uses the ideas here. Percentage error and uncertainty are in file 32.

### Board differences

- AQA 3.1.2.1 to 3.1.2.5 and RP1. AQA uses the ideal gas equation with SI units and asks for atom economy and percentage yield.
- Edexcel 5.1 to 5.16 and CP1 (molar volume of a gas), CP2 (standard solution and titration), CP3 (finding the concentration of hydrochloric acid). Edexcel asks for concentrations in g dm⁻³ and ppm (5.11) and for the molar gas volume at room temperature (24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹, 5.9) as well as pV = nRT (5.10).
- OCR A 2.1.2 to 2.1.4 include naming and writing formulae of common ions (2.1.2), water of crystallisation (2.1.3(d)), sustainability and atom economy (2.1.3(j)) and the reactions of acids with bases, carbonates and metals (2.1.4).

## 3.1.2.1 Relative atomic mass and relative molecular mass

*Refs: AQA 3.1.2.1 · Edexcel 5.3 · OCR A 2.1.1(e), 2.1.3(a) · OCR B EL(a) · Eduqas C1.3(a). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Aᵣ and Mᵣ are measured relative to one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
- Mᵣ is the sum of the Aᵣ values in the formula. For ionic compounds the term is relative formula mass.
- Molar mass, M, is the mass of one mole, in g mol⁻¹. Numerically it equals Mᵣ.

### Explained

Relative masses have no units because they are ratios. Molar mass does have units: g mol⁻¹.

Mᵣ of CuSO₄·5H₂O = 63.5 + 32.1 + 4(16.0) + 5(18.0) = 249.6. The dot means the water is part of the crystal and is counted in the formula.

### Common mistakes

- Giving Mᵣ units of g. Mᵣ has no units; molar mass is in g mol⁻¹.
- Forgetting the water of crystallisation in a hydrated salt.

## 3.1.2.2 The mole and the Avogadro constant

*Refs: AQA 3.1.2.2 · Edexcel 5.1 to 5.3, 5.7, 5.11 · OCR A 2.1.3(a), (e) · OCR B EL(b), EL(c) · Eduqas C1.3(e) to (g). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The Avogadro constant, L or Nₐ, is 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹: the number of particles in one mole.
- The mole is the unit of amount of substance.
- n = m ÷ M, and number of particles = n × L.
- For solutions, n = c × V, with V in dm³ and c in mol dm⁻³.

### Explained

A mole of any substance contains the same number of particles. One mole of carbon-12 atoms has a mass of exactly 12 g. (Since 2019 the mole has been defined by fixing the value of the Avogadro constant; either definition gives the same numbers.)

Three key equations:

| To find | Use | Units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moles from mass | n = m ÷ M | m in g, M in g mol⁻¹ |
| Moles in solution | n = c × V | c in mol dm⁻³, V in dm³ (cm³ ÷ 1000) |
| Particles | N = n × L | L = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ |

**Concentration in g dm⁻³** = mol dm⁻³ × M.

**Parts per million (Edexcel).** ppm by mass = (mass of solute ÷ mass of solution) × 10⁶. In dilute water solutions 1 ppm is about 1 mg dm⁻³.

### Worked example

1. How many water molecules are in 9.0 g of water?

   n = 9.0 ÷ 18.0 = 0.50 mol. N = 0.50 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.0 × 10²³.

2. How many ions in total are in 0.20 mol of CaCl₂?

   Each formula unit gives three ions. 0.20 × 3 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.6 × 10²³ ions.

3. What mass of NaOH is needed to make 250 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ solution?

   n = 0.100 × 0.250 = 0.0250 mol. m = 0.0250 × 40.0 = 1.00 g.

### Common mistakes

- Using cm³ in n = cV. Divide by 1000 first.
- Counting particles in a compound as molecules when the question asks for atoms or ions.

## 3.1.2.3 The ideal gas equation

*Refs: AQA 3.1.2.3 · Edexcel 5.9, 5.10, CP1 · OCR A 2.1.3(a)(v), (e), (f) · OCR B DF(a) · Eduqas C1.3(h), (i). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- pV = nRT, with p in Pa, V in m³, T in K, R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
- Molar gas volume: one mole of any gas at room temperature and pressure occupies about 24.0 dm³ (Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas).
- Volumes of reacting gases are in the same ratio as their moles (Avogadro's law).

### Explained

Unit conversions are where marks go:

| Quantity | Convert to SI |
| --- | --- |
| Pressure | kPa × 1000 = Pa |
| Volume | cm³ × 10⁻⁶ = m³; dm³ × 10⁻³ = m³ |
| Temperature | °C + 273 = K |

The ideal gas equation assumes molecules have no volume and no forces between them. Real gases behave nearly ideally at high temperature and low pressure.

**Correcting a volume (Eduqas).** Rearranging for a fixed amount of gas: p₁V₁/T₁ = p₂V₂/T₂.

**Molar volume method (Edexcel CP1).** Gas volume = n × 24.0 dm³ at room temperature and pressure. To measure it, react a known mass of a metal such as magnesium (or a carbonate) with excess acid and collect the gas in a gas syringe or an upturned burette over water. Divide the volume collected by the moles of gas formed.

### Worked example

1. What volume does 0.350 g of nitrogen gas occupy at 25 °C and 101 kPa?

   n = 0.350 ÷ 28.0 = 0.0125 mol. V = nRT ÷ p = (0.0125 × 8.31 × 298) ÷ 101 000 = 3.06 × 10⁻⁴ m³ = 306 cm³.

2. A gas has mass 0.482 g and occupies 150 cm³ at 100 kPa and 300 K. Find Mᵣ.

   n = pV ÷ RT = (100 000 × 150 × 10⁻⁶) ÷ (8.31 × 300) = 6.02 × 10⁻³ mol. M = 0.482 ÷ 6.02 × 10⁻³ = 80.1 g mol⁻¹.

3. 50 cm³ of propane burns completely: C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O. What volume of oxygen is used, and what volume of CO₂ forms (same conditions)?

   O₂: 5 × 50 = 250 cm³. CO₂: 3 × 50 = 150 cm³.

### Common mistakes

- Leaving temperature in °C or pressure in kPa.
- Converting cm³ to m³ by dividing by 1000 (that gives dm³). Divide by 10⁶.
- Using 24 dm³ mol⁻¹ for a liquid or solid product (water at room temperature is a liquid).

## 3.1.2.4 Empirical and molecular formula

*Refs: AQA 3.1.2.4 · Edexcel 5.4, 5.5 · OCR A 2.1.3(b), (c), (d) · OCR B EL(b) · Eduqas C1.3(d). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Empirical formula: the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.
- Molecular formula: the actual number of atoms of each element in a molecule.
- Calculate empirical formula from masses or percentages, including combustion data, and the molecular formula from the empirical formula and Mᵣ.
- Anhydrous, hydrated and water of crystallisation; find the formula of a hydrated salt from heating data (OCR A, OCR B).

### Explained

Method for empirical formula:

1. Write mass (or %) of each element.
2. Divide by Aᵣ to get moles.
3. Divide all by the smallest.
4. If not whole numbers, multiply up (×2 for 0.5, ×3 for 0.33 or 0.67, ×4 for 0.25).

Molecular formula = empirical formula × (Mᵣ ÷ empirical formula mass).

**Water of crystallisation.** Heat a weighed hydrated salt in a crucible to constant mass (heat, cool, weigh, repeat until two readings agree). The mass lost is water. Moles of water ÷ moles of anhydrous salt gives x in MX·xH₂O.

### Worked example

1. A hydrocarbon is 85.7% carbon. Its Mᵣ is 56. Find its molecular formula.

   C: 85.7 ÷ 12.0 = 7.14. H: 14.3 ÷ 1.0 = 14.3. Ratio 1 : 2, so CH₂ (mass 14). 56 ÷ 14 = 4, so C₄H₈.

2. 0.600 g of an organic compound containing C, H and O burns to give 1.32 g CO₂ and 0.720 g H₂O. Find its empirical formula.

   C: 1.32 ÷ 44.0 = 0.0300 mol C = 0.360 g. H: 2 × (0.720 ÷ 18.0) = 0.0800 mol H = 0.0800 g. O: 0.600 − 0.360 − 0.080 = 0.160 g = 0.0100 mol. Ratio C : H : O = 3 : 8 : 1, so C₃H₈O.

3. 2.50 g of hydrated MgSO₄·xH₂O is heated to constant mass, leaving 1.22 g of anhydrous MgSO₄. Find x.

   Water = 1.28 g = 0.0711 mol. MgSO₄ = 1.22 ÷ 120.4 = 0.01013 mol. x = 0.0711 ÷ 0.01013 = 7.0, so MgSO₄·7H₂O.

### Common mistakes

- Rounding 1.5 to 2 instead of doubling all values.
- Using the mass of H₂O instead of the mass of H when working from combustion data, or forgetting there are two H in each H₂O.
- Not heating to constant mass, so some water remains and x comes out too low.

## 3.1.2.5 Balanced equations and associated calculations

*Refs: AQA 3.1.2.5, RP1 · Edexcel 5.6 to 5.8, 5.11 to 5.15, CP2, CP3 · OCR A 2.1.2(a), (b), 2.1.3(e), (g) to (j), 2.1.4(a) to (e) · OCR B EL(c), EL(d), EL(t), ES(a) · Eduqas C1.1(a), (c), C1.3(j), (k), C1.6(r), C2.1(d), (f). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Write balanced full and ionic equations with state symbols.
- Use balanced equations to find reacting masses, volumes of gases and solution concentrations.
- Percentage yield and percentage atom economy, and why high atom economy matters for sustainability and cost.
- Titration calculations, including making a standard solution.
- Formulae of common acids and their ions; neutralisation of acids by bases, alkalis and carbonates, and reactions of acids with metals (OCR A 2.1.4).
- Eduqas: preparing salts, back titrations, double titrations and gravimetric analysis.

### Explained

**Formulae of ions to learn.**

| Positive | Negative |
| --- | --- |
| NH₄⁺ ammonium | OH⁻ hydroxide |
| H⁺ hydrogen | NO₃⁻ nitrate |
| Zn²⁺, Ag⁺ | CO₃²⁻ carbonate, HCO₃⁻ hydrogencarbonate |
| | SO₄²⁻ sulfate, SO₃²⁻ sulfite |
| | PO₄³⁻ phosphate |

Common acids: HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃, CH₃COOH. Common alkalis: NaOH, KOH, NH₃(aq).

**Ionic equations** show only the species that change. Spectator ions are left out. For any strong acid with any strong alkali: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l). For silver nitrate with sodium chloride: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s).

**Reactions of acids (OCR A 2.1.4, Eduqas C2.1(d)).**

- Acid + metal hydroxide or oxide → salt + water.
- Acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide. Ionic: 2H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → H₂O + CO₂.
- Acid + reactive metal → salt + hydrogen. This is also a redox reaction (file 7).
- Acid + ammonia → ammonium salt: NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl.

An acid releases H⁺ ions in water; a base accepts H⁺; an alkali is a soluble base that releases OH⁻ ions. Strong acids fully dissociate in water; weak acids only partly dissociate (file 11).

**Percentage yield** = (actual mass of product ÷ theoretical mass) × 100. Yields are below 100% because of incomplete reactions, side reactions and losses on transfer and purification.

**Atom economy** = (Mᵣ of desired product ÷ total Mᵣ of all reactants) × 100. Addition reactions have 100% atom economy. A high atom economy means less waste, fewer raw materials used, and lower cost of disposal. It is a theoretical figure from the equation, and is not the same as yield.

**Titration (RP1, Edexcel CP2 and CP3, OCR A 2.1.4(d), Eduqas).**

*Making a standard solution:*

1. Weigh the solid accurately in a weighing boat on a two-decimal (or better) balance. Reweigh the boat after tipping out the solid so the mass transferred is known (weighing by difference).
2. Dissolve in a beaker in less distilled water than the final volume. Stir.
3. Transfer to a volumetric flask with a funnel, rinsing the beaker, rod and funnel into the flask.
4. Make up to the mark with distilled water so the bottom of the meniscus sits on the line at eye level.
5. Stopper and invert several times to mix.

*Doing the titration:*

1. Rinse the pipette with the solution it will deliver, and the burette with its solution. Rinse the conical flask with distilled water only.
2. Pipette 25.0 cm³ of one solution into the flask. Add 2 to 3 drops of indicator.
3. Fill the burette (below eye level, funnel removed, tap filled with no air bubble). Read the initial volume to 0.05 cm³.
4. Do a rough titration, then accurate ones, adding dropwise near the end point and swirling. Wash the sides with distilled water (this does not change the moles).
5. Repeat until two titres are concordant (within 0.10 cm³). Average the concordant titres.

Indicators: methyl orange (red in acid, yellow in alkali; end point orange) or phenolphthalein (colourless in acid, pink in alkali). Do not use universal indicator; its colour change is gradual.

**Back titration (Eduqas).** Used when a solid reacts slowly or is insoluble, such as calcium carbonate in limestone. Add a known excess of acid, then titrate the leftover acid with alkali. Acid used by the solid = acid added − acid left.

**Double titration (Eduqas).** For a mixture of NaOH and Na₂CO₃ titrated with HCl: phenolphthalein loses its colour when all the NaOH is neutralised and the carbonate has become hydrogencarbonate (CO₃²⁻ + H⁺ → HCO₃⁻). Methyl orange is then added; the second titre turns HCO₃⁻ into CO₂ and water. From the two titres the amounts of each base can be found.

**Gravimetric analysis (Eduqas).** Precipitate an ion as an insoluble compound (for example sulfate as BaSO₄ by adding barium chloride), filter, wash, dry to constant mass and weigh. The mass of precipitate gives the moles of the ion.

**Preparing a salt (Eduqas C1.6(r)).** Soluble salt from an alkali: titrate to find the exact volume, then repeat without indicator, evaporate to the point of crystallisation and leave to crystallise. Soluble salt from an insoluble base or carbonate: add excess solid to acid, filter off the excess, crystallise. Insoluble salt: mix two solutions, filter, wash, dry.

### Worked example

1. 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide is neutralised by 22.40 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sulfuric acid. Find the concentration of NaOH.

   H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O. n(H₂SO₄) = 0.100 × 0.02240 = 2.24 × 10⁻³ mol. n(NaOH) = 4.48 × 10⁻³ mol. c = 4.48 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = 0.179 mol dm⁻³.

2. Find the atom economy for making ethanol by fermentation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂.

   Atom economy = (2 × 46.0) ÷ 180.0 × 100 = 51.1%.

3. 5.00 g of impure limestone is added to 100 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl (excess). The solution is made up to 250 cm³. 25.0 cm³ portions need 14.50 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. Find the percentage of CaCO₃.

   HCl left in 25.0 cm³ = 0.200 × 0.01450 = 2.90 × 10⁻³ mol, so in 250 cm³ = 0.0290 mol. HCl added = 0.100 mol. HCl used = 0.0710 mol. CaCO₃ + 2HCl, so n(CaCO₃) = 0.0355 mol, mass = 3.55 g. Percentage = 3.55 ÷ 5.00 × 100 = 71.0%.

4. 4.80 g of magnesium reacts with excess HCl. Find the volume of hydrogen at room temperature and pressure.

   Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. n(Mg) = 4.80 ÷ 24.3 = 0.198 mol = n(H₂). V = 0.198 × 24.0 = 4.74 dm³.

### Common mistakes

- Forgetting the mole ratio from the equation.
- Using non-concordant titres in the mean.
- Rinsing the conical flask with the solution it will hold (this adds extra moles).
- Mixing up percentage yield (practical) and atom economy (from the equation).
- Writing spectator ions in an ionic equation.

### Practical

**AQA RP1: make up a volumetric solution and carry out a simple acid–base titration.** Method as above. Typical errors: balance ±0.005 g, pipette ±0.06 cm³, burette ±0.05 cm³ per reading (so ±0.10 cm³ for a titre), volumetric flask ±0.2 cm³ for 250 cm³. Percentage uncertainty = (uncertainty ÷ measured value) × 100 (file 32). To reduce the percentage uncertainty in the titre, use a more dilute titrant so the titre is larger.

**Edexcel CP1, CP2, CP3; OCR A PAG1, PAG2; Eduqas standardisation, back titration, double titration, gravimetric analysis, salt preparation.** Covered in the sections above.

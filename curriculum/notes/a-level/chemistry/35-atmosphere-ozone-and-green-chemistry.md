# The atmosphere, ozone, the greenhouse effect and green chemistry

Chemistry explains, and can help fix, the problems humans cause in the atmosphere. This file covers measuring gas concentrations in percentages and ppm, ozone in the stratosphere and troposphere, the radical chemistry of ozone depletion and the role of bond enthalpy, the greenhouse effect and how greenhouse gases absorb infrared, ocean acidification, and the principles of green chemistry and sustainability.

## OZ Gas concentrations, ozone and radicals

*Refs: OCR B OZ(i), OZ(q), OZ(r), OZ(s). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Calculate percentage by volume and parts per million (ppm) for gases.
- Ozone forms and breaks down naturally in the stratosphere, absorbing high-energy UV.
- In the troposphere, ozone is a pollutant that contributes to photochemical smog.
- Haloalkanes release halogen radicals by photodissociation; the ease depends on C–X bond enthalpy (C–I easiest, C–F hardest).
- Chlorine radicals catalyse ozone breakdown; other radicals (NO•, HO•) do too.
- The Sun emits mainly UV, visible and some IR; the Earth radiates IR.

### Explained

**Gas composition.** For gases, volume percentage = mole percentage. ppm = (volume of component ÷ total volume) × 10⁶. Air is about 78% N₂, 21% O₂, 0.9% Ar, and about 0.04% (420 ppm) CO₂.

**Ozone in the stratosphere** (about 10 to 50 km up).

- Formation: O₂ + UV → 2O; O + O₂ + M → O₃ + M (M is any third molecule that carries away energy).
- Breakdown: O₃ + UV → O₂ + O; O + O₃ → 2O₂.

These reactions absorb high-energy UV (UV-B and UV-C), acting as a sunscreen. UV-B causes sunburn, skin cancer and cataracts, and damages crops and plankton.

**Ozone depletion.** Halogen radicals formed when UV breaks C–X bonds in haloalkanes catalyse ozone breakdown:

CF₂Cl₂ → •CF₂Cl + Cl•
Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂
ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂
Overall: O₃ + O → 2O₂

**Ease of photodissociation.** The C–X bond enthalpy falls from C–F to C–I. C–F bonds are too strong to be broken by the UV that reaches the stratosphere, so fluoroalkanes (HFCs) do not deplete ozone. C–Cl bonds break, so CFCs do. C–Br and C–I break even more easily, but iodo- and bromoalkanes break down lower in the atmosphere before reaching the stratosphere; bromine from halons is still very damaging.

**Ozone in the troposphere.** Near the ground ozone is a pollutant: it irritates eyes and lungs and damages plants. It forms when sunlight breaks NO₂ (from vehicles): NO₂ → NO + O; O + O₂ → O₃. With unburnt hydrocarbons, this produces photochemical smog.

**Radiation from the Sun and Earth.** The Sun, being very hot, emits mostly visible light with some UV and IR. The Earth's surface, being much cooler, emits IR.

### Common mistakes

- Saying ozone is always harmful or always useful. It depends where it is.
- Saying CFCs deplete ozone because C–F bonds break.

## O(n) The greenhouse effect

*Refs: OCR A 4.2.4(b) · OCR B O(n). Level: AS & A (OCR B O(n) A-level).*

### What you need to know

- Solar energy reaches the Earth mainly as visible and UV radiation; the Earth warms and radiates IR.
- Greenhouse gases absorb some of this IR, increasing the vibrational energy of their bonds.
- They pass the energy to other molecules by collisions (raising the temperature) and re-emit IR in all directions, some back to Earth.
- Bonds such as C=O, O–H and C–H absorb IR; CO₂, H₂O and CH₄ are greenhouse gases. N₂ and O₂ are not.
- Human activity has increased CO₂ and CH₄ levels, enhancing the greenhouse effect; this links to global warming and climate change.

### Explained

A molecule absorbs IR only if its vibration changes its dipole. N₂ and O₂ are symmetrical diatomics with no change of dipole when they stretch, so they do not absorb IR. CO₂ (asymmetric stretch and bending), H₂O and CH₄ do.

How much a gas contributes depends on: how well each molecule absorbs IR, how much of the gas is present, and how long it stays in the atmosphere (residence time). Methane is a much stronger absorber per molecule than CO₂ but is present at much lower concentration and lasts about 12 years; CO₂ lasts for centuries.

Some IR wavelengths are not absorbed by water vapour (the "IR window"); CO₂ and CH₄ absorb in parts of this window, which is why rising levels matter.

**Responses.** International agreements (the Kyoto Protocol, the Paris Agreement) set targets to cut emissions. Approaches include renewable energy, carbon capture and storage (pumping CO₂ into old oil fields or deep rock, or reacting it with metal oxides to form carbonates), and more efficient processes.

**Ocean acidification (OCR B Oceans context).** The oceans absorb CO₂: CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq); CO₂(aq) + H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻; HCO₃⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + CO₃²⁻. More CO₂ makes the sea slightly more acidic, and H⁺ removes CO₃²⁻ ions, making it harder for shellfish and corals to build calcium carbonate shells. The HCO₃⁻/CO₃²⁻ system also buffers the oceans (file 11).

### Common mistakes

- Saying greenhouse gases "trap heat" without explaining absorption of IR by bond vibrations.
- Confusing the greenhouse effect with the hole in the ozone layer.

## C2.4 The wider impact of chemistry and green chemistry

*Refs: Eduqas C2.4(a), (b) · OCR B WM(g). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Chemical industry and energy production have social, economic and environmental impacts.
- Principles of green chemistry and how they improve sustainability; apply them to given processes.

### Explained

**Impacts.** Chemical industry provides medicines, fertilisers, materials and fuels, and employs many people. It also uses finite resources and energy, and can release waste and pollutants. Burning fossil fuels for energy releases CO₂, SO₂, NOₓ and particulates.

**Principles of green chemistry** (summarised; apply them to the information given):

1. Prevent waste rather than treat it afterwards.
2. Use reactions with high atom economy.
3. Use and make substances with low toxicity.
4. Use safer solvents (water, supercritical CO₂) or none.
5. Use less energy: lower temperatures and pressures, and catalysts.
6. Use renewable raw materials (feedstocks from plants).
7. Avoid unnecessary steps such as protecting groups.
8. Use catalysts rather than reagents used in large amounts.
9. Design products that break down after use (biodegradable).
10. Monitor processes to prevent pollution.
11. Design processes to reduce the risk of accidents.

**Examples.** Ibuprofen's industrial route was shortened from six steps to three, raising atom economy from about 40% to almost 80%. Supercritical CO₂ replaces organic solvents for decaffeinating coffee. Enzymes catalyse reactions at body temperature in water.

**Sustainability** means meeting present needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs.

### Common mistakes

- Treating percentage yield and atom economy as the same thing.
- Calling a process "green" just because it uses a catalyst, without considering raw materials, energy and waste.

# Group 2 (and Group 1): the s-block metals

The Group 2 metals, magnesium to barium, show clear trends down the group. This file covers trends in atomic radius, ionisation energy, melting point and reactivity, reactions with water and oxygen, solubility of hydroxides and sulfates, thermal stability of carbonates and nitrates, flame tests, and uses of Group 2 compounds. Group 1 is included for the boards that ask for it.

## 3.2.2 Group 2, the alkaline earth metals

*Refs: AQA 3.2.2 · Edexcel 4.1 to 4.8 · OCR A 3.1.2(a) to (e) · OCR B EL(p), EL(r), EL(s), EL(u) · Eduqas C1.6(e) to (k). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Trends down Group 2 in atomic radius, first ionisation energy, melting point and reactivity.
- Reactions of the elements with water (and oxygen) and the trend in reactivity.
- Solubility trends of the hydroxides and the sulfates.
- Uses of Group 2 compounds.
- Thermal stability of carbonates and nitrates (Edexcel, OCR B, Eduqas).
- Flame test colours (Edexcel, Eduqas).
- Group 1 reactivity trend (Edexcel, Eduqas).

### Explained

**Physical trends down the group (Mg → Ba).**

- Atomic radius increases: each element has an extra shell.
- First ionisation energy decreases: the outer electrons are further from the nucleus and more shielded, so less attracted, despite more protons.
- Melting point generally decreases (AQA): the metal ions are larger, so the attraction between the delocalised electrons and the ions is weaker. (Magnesium is a slight exception because of its different crystal structure.)

**Reactivity increases down the group.** Group 2 metals react by losing two electrons. The first and second ionisation energies both fall down the group, so the electrons are lost more easily.

**With water.**

- Mg + 2H₂O(l) → Mg(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g): very slow with cold water.
- Mg + H₂O(g) → MgO(s) + H₂(g): fast with steam, bright white glow.
- Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂: steady fizzing, a cloudy white suspension forms.
- Sr and Ba react faster still.

**With oxygen.** All burn to form white ionic oxides MO: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (brilliant white flame). Calcium burns with a brick-red flame, strontium crimson, barium apple green. Barium can also form the peroxide BaO₂ in excess oxygen.

**Oxides and hydroxides are basic.**

- Oxides react with water to form hydroxides: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂. The O²⁻ ion takes a proton from water.
- They neutralise acids: MgO + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O; Ca(OH)₂ + 2HNO₃ → Ca(NO₃)₂ + 2H₂O.
- The hydroxide solutions become more alkaline down the group because the hydroxides become more soluble, so [OH⁻] is higher.

**Solubility.**

| Compound | Mg | Ca | Sr | Ba | Trend |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hydroxide | Sparingly soluble | Slightly soluble | More soluble | Soluble | Increases down |
| Sulfate | Soluble | Slightly soluble | Sparingly soluble | Insoluble | Decreases down |

So adding NaOH(aq) to Mg²⁺(aq) gives a white precipitate of Mg(OH)₂; adding sulfate ions to Ba²⁺(aq) gives a white precipitate of BaSO₄. Ca²⁺ gives a slight white precipitate with both. Carbonates of Group 2 are all insoluble: Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺ and Ba²⁺ all give white precipitates with carbonate ions (Eduqas C1.6(f)).

Ionic equations: Mg²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Mg(OH)₂(s); Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s).

**Uses (AQA, OCR A).**

| Compound | Use | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mg(OH)₂ | Antacid (milk of magnesia) | Weak base, neutralises stomach acid, only slightly soluble so not too alkaline |
| CaCO₃ | Antacid; in farming | Neutralises acid, giving CO₂ |
| Ca(OH)₂ (slaked lime) | Neutralises acid soil | Base: Ca(OH)₂ + 2H⁺ → Ca²⁺ + 2H₂O |
| CaO or CaCO₃ | Removes SO₂ from power station flue gases | CaO + SO₂ → CaSO₃; then oxidised to CaSO₄ (gypsum) |
| BaSO₄ | "Barium meal" for X-ray of gut | Insoluble, so the toxic Ba²⁺ ions are not absorbed; absorbs X-rays |
| BaCl₂ (acidified) | Test for sulfate ions | White BaSO₄ precipitate (file 14) |
| Mg | Extracting titanium | TiCl₄ + 2Mg → Ti + 2MgCl₂, reduces TiCl₄ |

**Thermal stability of carbonates and nitrates (Edexcel, OCR B, Eduqas).**

Group 2 carbonates decompose on heating to the oxide and carbon dioxide: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. They become **more stable** down the group (need higher temperatures).

Group 2 nitrates decompose to the oxide, nitrogen dioxide (brown gas) and oxygen: 2Mg(NO₃)₂ → 2MgO + 4NO₂ + O₂. They also become more stable down the group.

**Explanation: charge density and polarisation.** The cation at the top of the group (Mg²⁺) is small with a 2+ charge, so it has a high charge density. It polarises (distorts) the large carbonate or nitrate anion, weakening the bonds inside it, so the anion breaks up more easily. Down the group the cation is larger, its charge density falls, it polarises the anion less, so the compound is more stable.

**Group 1** compounds are more stable than Group 2 because 1+ ions have lower charge density. Most Group 1 carbonates do not decompose at Bunsen temperatures; Li₂CO₃ does (Li⁺ is very small): Li₂CO₃ → Li₂O + CO₂. Group 1 nitrates (except lithium) decompose only to the nitrite and oxygen: 2NaNO₃ → 2NaNO₂ + O₂. Lithium nitrate behaves like Group 2: 4LiNO₃ → 2Li₂O + 4NO₂ + O₂.

**Experimental procedure (Edexcel 4.8).** Heat equal amounts of each carbonate in a boiling tube fitted with a delivery tube into limewater. Time how long until the limewater turns cloudy (CO₂). For nitrates, time until brown NO₂ appears (in a fume cupboard), or until a glowing splint relights (O₂). Keep the Bunsen flame, mass of solid and distance from flame constant.

**Group 1 reactivity (Edexcel, Eduqas).** Reactivity increases from Li to Cs: the single outer electron is further away and more shielded, so the first ionisation energy falls. Lithium fizzes steadily in water; sodium melts into a ball and moves around; potassium ignites with a lilac flame. 2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂. Group 1 oxides and hydroxides are strongly basic and soluble.

**Flame tests (Edexcel 4.7, Eduqas C1.6(g)).**

| Ion | Colour |
| --- | --- |
| Li⁺ | Red (crimson) |
| Na⁺ | Yellow-orange |
| K⁺ | Lilac |
| Rb⁺ | Red-violet |
| Cs⁺ | Blue-violet |
| Ca²⁺ | Brick red (orange-red) |
| Sr²⁺ | Crimson |
| Ba²⁺ | Apple green |
| Cu²⁺ | Blue-green |

Method: clean a nichrome wire by dipping in concentrated hydrochloric acid and holding it in a roaring Bunsen flame until no colour shows. Dip it in acid then into the solid, and hold in the edge of the flame.

Why: the heat promotes electrons to higher energy levels. When they fall back, they emit energy as light of specific frequencies (ΔE = hf). The energy gaps differ for each ion, so each gives its own colour. Magnesium gives no flame colour because its emitted light is outside the visible region.

### Worked example

1. Explain why a saturated solution of barium hydroxide has a higher pH than a saturated solution of magnesium hydroxide.

   Ba(OH)₂ is much more soluble than Mg(OH)₂, so more dissolves. The concentration of OH⁻ ions is higher, so the pH is higher.

2. 0.500 g of impure calcium carbonate is heated to constant mass. The mass falls by 0.198 g. Find the percentage of CaCO₃ (impurities do not decompose).

   CO₂ lost = 0.198 ÷ 44.0 = 4.50 × 10⁻³ mol = n(CaCO₃). Mass CaCO₃ = 4.50 × 10⁻³ × 100.1 = 0.450 g. Percentage = 90.1%.

**Solubility rules for precipitation tests (OCR B EL(s)).** Useful facts for predicting precipitates:

| Anion | Soluble with | Precipitates (colour) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NO₃⁻ | All cations | None |
| Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ | Most | AgCl white, AgBr cream, AgI yellow; PbCl₂ white, PbI₂ yellow |
| SO₄²⁻ | Most | BaSO₄ white, PbSO₄ white; CaSO₄ slightly soluble |
| CO₃²⁻ | Group 1 and NH₄⁺ | Most others: CaCO₃, BaCO₃ white; CuCO₃ blue-green; ZnCO₃ white |
| OH⁻ | Group 1, NH₄⁺, Ba²⁺ | Cu(OH)₂ blue, Fe(OH)₂ green, Fe(OH)₃ orange-brown, Zn(OH)₂, Al(OH)₃, Pb(OH)₂ white, Mg(OH)₂ white |

All Group 1 and ammonium compounds are soluble. These facts make the basis of ion tests: Ba²⁺ for SO₄²⁻, Ag⁺ for halides, OH⁻ for many metal ions (files 14 and 16).

### Common mistakes

- Saying reactivity increases because "more shells means more electrons to lose". Say the outer electrons are lost more easily because ionisation energy decreases.
- Reversing the solubility trends: hydroxides become more soluble down the group, sulfates less soluble.
- Saying BaSO₄ is safe because barium is not toxic. Barium ions are toxic; BaSO₄ is safe because it is insoluble.
- Explaining thermal stability without mentioning polarisation of the anion by the cation.
- Saying flame colours come from electrons being "given energy" without saying that light is emitted when they fall back.

## Mark scheme

1. Outer electrons further from nucleus / larger atomic radius (1); more shielding by inner shells (1); weaker attraction outweighs increase in nuclear charge (1).
2. Fizzing / bubbles; calcium sinks and dissolves; cloudy white solution forms (any two, 2); Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂ (1).
3. BaSO₄ is insoluble (1); so Ba²⁺ ions are not absorbed into the blood / it absorbs X-rays and outlines the gut (1).
4. CaO + SO₂ → CaSO₃ (1).
5. Solubility increases down the group (1); add NaOH(aq) to each solution: Mg²⁺ gives a white precipitate, Ba²⁺ none / least (1).
6. Mg²⁺ is smaller (same charge) so higher charge density (1); polarises / distorts the carbonate ion more (1); weakens C–O bonds so decomposes more easily (1).
7. 2Ca(NO₃)₂ → 2CaO + 4NO₂ + O₂ (1); brown gas / white solid melts / glowing splint relights (1).
8. Electrons are promoted / excited to higher energy levels by heat (1); they fall back to lower levels (1); emitting energy as light of a particular frequency / wavelength (1).
9. Reducing agent for TiCl₄ (1); TiCl₄ + 2Mg → Ti + 2MgCl₂ (1).

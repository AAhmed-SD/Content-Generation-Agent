# Colour, dyes, diazonium coupling and fats and oils

Why are some organic compounds coloured, and how are dyes made and fixed to cloth? This file covers the origin of colour in organic molecules (chromophores and delocalisation), making diazonium salts and coupling them to form azo dyes, how dye structure controls colour, solubility and binding to fibres, and the structure of fats and oils.

## Overview

A-level, OCR B and Eduqas only. Colour in transition metal complexes is in file 15; benzene and phenol in file 25; amines in file 26; esters and soaps in file 24.

### Board differences

- OCR B CD(a) to (c), (h), (m): dyes and fibres, chromophores and modifying groups, fats and oils as mixed esters of propane-1,2,3-triol, diazonium compounds and azo dyes, and the origins of colour and UV absorption.
- Eduqas OA3.1(e) to (h): primary amines with cold nitric(III) acid, coupling benzenediazonium salts with phenols and aromatic amines, the –N=N– chromophore, and colour from the wavelengths of visible light absorbed.

## CD Origin of colour in organic molecules

*Refs: OCR B CD(m) · Eduqas OA3.1(g), (h). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- A coloured compound absorbs some wavelengths of visible light; we see the complementary colour (the light not absorbed).
- Absorption promotes electrons to higher energy levels: ΔE = hf.
- The chromophore is the part of the molecule responsible for absorption; it usually contains a delocalised electron system (C=C, C=O, N=N, benzene rings).
- The more extensive the delocalisation, the smaller the energy gap, so the lower the frequency (longer wavelength) of light absorbed.

### Explained

Electrons in molecules occupy energy levels. Light whose photon energy matches a gap is absorbed and promotes an electron. In small molecules the gaps are large, so they absorb in the ultraviolet and look colourless (benzene absorbs only UV). As the delocalised system grows (more alternating double and single bonds, more rings joined through –N=N–), the gap between levels gets smaller, so lower-energy light is absorbed, moving from UV into the visible region. The compound then looks coloured.

**Complementary colours.** If a dye absorbs blue light (about 450 nm), it looks orange or yellow. If it absorbs green, it looks red or purple.

| Absorbed | Seen |
| --- | --- |
| Violet | Yellow-green |
| Blue | Orange / yellow |
| Green | Red / magenta |
| Yellow | Violet / blue |
| Red | Blue-green |

**The azo chromophore.** –N=N– linking two benzene rings gives an extended delocalised system across the whole molecule, which absorbs visible light. Azo dyes are typically yellow, orange or red.

**Modifying groups (OCR B CD(b)).** Groups attached to the chromophore change its colour and intensity: –OH, –NH₂ and –NR₂ have lone pairs that join the delocalised system, extending it and shifting absorption to longer wavelengths (deepening the colour). Changing pH can protonate or deprotonate such groups, which is why some dyes act as indicators (methyl orange is an azo dye).

### Common mistakes

- Saying a red dye absorbs red light.
- Saying more delocalisation means a bigger energy gap.

## OA3.1 Diazonium salts and azo dyes

*Refs: OCR B CD(h) · Eduqas OA3.1(e), (f). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Primary aromatic amines react with nitric(III) (nitrous) acid below 10 °C to form diazonium salts.
- Diazonium salts couple with phenols (in alkali) or aromatic amines to form azo dyes.
- Primary aliphatic amines with nitrous acid give nitrogen gas and alcohols (Eduqas).

### Explained

**Making nitrous acid.** It is unstable, so it is made in situ from sodium nitrite (sodium nitrate(III)) and hydrochloric acid: NaNO₂ + HCl → HNO₂ + NaCl.

**Diazotisation.** Phenylamine + nitrous acid + HCl, kept below 10 °C in an ice bath:

C₆H₅NH₂ + HNO₂ + HCl → C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + 2H₂O

The product is benzenediazonium chloride, containing –N≡N⁺. Below 10 °C because above that it decomposes, giving off nitrogen gas and forming phenol: C₆H₅N₂⁺ + H₂O → C₆H₅OH + N₂ + H⁺. (Dry diazonium salts can explode, so they are used in solution straight away.)

**Aliphatic amines (Eduqas OA3.1(e)).** Their diazonium ions are too unstable even when cold: nitrogen gas is given off immediately, and an alcohol forms. CH₃CH₂NH₂ + HNO₂ → CH₃CH₂OH + N₂ + H₂O. The steady effervescence of N₂ distinguishes primary aliphatic from aromatic amines.

**Coupling.** The diazonium ion is a weak electrophile. It attacks a very electron-rich ring (a phenol in alkaline solution, as the phenoxide ion, or an aromatic amine), substituting at the 4-position (or 2 if 4 is blocked):

C₆H₅N₂⁺ + C₆H₅O⁻ → C₆H₅–N=N–C₆H₄–OH (4-hydroxyazobenzene, a yellow-orange dye)

With naphthalen-2-ol in alkali, a bright red dye forms. With phenylamine, a yellow dye (4-aminoazobenzene) forms.

**Practical (OCR B).** Dissolve phenylamine in hydrochloric acid, cool in ice, add cold sodium nitrite solution dropwise, keeping below 10 °C. Add this to a cold alkaline solution of a phenol: a coloured precipitate forms at once. Filter and use to dye cloth. Phenylamine is toxic; wear gloves.

### Common mistakes

- Doing diazotisation at room temperature.
- Writing nitric(V) acid (HNO₃) instead of nitric(III) acid (HNO₂).
- Coupling with benzene, which is not reactive enough.

## CD Dyes and fibres

*Refs: OCR B CD(a), CD(b). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- How dyes attach to fibres: intermolecular forces (hydrogen bonds, London forces), ionic bonds and covalent bonds.
- The parts of a dye: the chromophore; groups that modify it; groups that affect solubility; groups that let it bond to fibres. (Structures are given in questions.)

### Explained

**Groups in a dye molecule.**

| Purpose | Groups |
| --- | --- |
| Chromophore (colour) | Extended delocalised system: –N=N– between rings, C=C, C=O |
| Modify colour | –OH, –NH₂, –NR₂ (extend delocalisation) |
| Make soluble in water (so it can be applied) | –SO₃⁻ Na⁺ (sulfonate), –COO⁻ Na⁺, –OH |
| Bond to the fibre | Depends on the fibre (below) |

**Fibres and how dyes attach.**

| Fibre | Structure | How dyes attach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cotton (cellulose) | Many –OH groups | Hydrogen bonds; reactive dyes form covalent bonds with –OH (very colourfast) |
| Wool, silk, nylon (proteins and polyamides) | –NH₃⁺ and –COO⁻ groups, amide links | Ionic bonds between –SO₃⁻ on the dye and –NH₃⁺ on the fibre (acid dyes); hydrogen bonds |
| Polyester | Few polar groups | Small non-polar ("disperse") dyes held by London forces and permanent dipole forces |

The stronger the bond between dye and fibre, the less the colour washes out (colourfastness): covalent > ionic > hydrogen bonds > London forces.

### Common mistakes

- Saying a dye attaches to all fibres in the same way.
- Saying the sulfonate group causes colour. It mainly makes the dye soluble (and can form ionic bonds).

## CD Fats and oils

*Refs: OCR B CD(c). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Fats and oils are mainly mixed triesters of propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol) with long-chain fatty acids, with different degrees of unsaturation.

### Explained

Each triglyceride has a glycerol backbone joined by three ester links to three fatty acid chains, which can differ (mixed esters). Fatty acids are saturated (no C=C, such as stearic acid, C₁₇H₃₅COOH), mono-unsaturated (one C=C) or polyunsaturated (several C=C).

- Saturated chains are straight and pack closely, with more London forces, so saturated fats (animal fats) are solid at room temperature.
- Unsaturated chains, especially with Z (cis) double bonds, have kinks that stop close packing, so unsaturated oils (vegetable oils) are liquid.
- Hydrogenation (H₂ with Ni catalyst) saturates some C=C to make spreads solid (file 20). Partial hydrogenation can create E (trans) fats, linked to heart disease.
- Degree of unsaturation can be compared by the volume of bromine water (or iodine) that a sample decolorises.
- Hydrolysis with alkali gives soap; transesterification with methanol gives biodiesel (file 24).

Some natural dyes and pigments (such as β-carotene) are fat-soluble because they are mostly hydrocarbon, which is why they dissolve in oils.

### Common mistakes

- Saying oils are liquid because they have lower Mᵣ. It is the kinked chains that pack less well.

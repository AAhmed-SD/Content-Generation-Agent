# Bonding, structure, shapes and intermolecular forces

How particles are held together decides melting points, conductivity, solubility and shape. This file covers ionic, covalent, dative and metallic bonding, the four types of structure, shapes of molecules by electron pair repulsion, bond polarity and the forces between molecules.

## 3.1.3.1 Ionic bonding

*Refs: AQA 3.1.3.1 · Edexcel 2.1 to 2.6 · OCR A 2.2.2(a), (b) · OCR B EL(i), EL(j), EL(l) · Eduqas C1.4(a), C1.5(a). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Ionic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a lattice.
- Formulae of compound ions (sulfate, hydroxide, nitrate, carbonate, ammonium).
- Predict the charge on an ion from the element's position in the periodic table.
- Dot-and-cross diagrams of ionic compounds.
- Ionic bond strength increases with ionic charge and decreases with ionic radius (Edexcel).
- Trends in ionic radii (Edexcel): ions get larger down a group; isoelectronic ions get smaller as nuclear charge rises.

### Explained

Metals lose electrons to form positive ions; non-metals gain electrons to form negative ions. Groups 1, 2, 3 form 1+, 2+, 3+ ions. Groups 5, 6, 7 form 3−, 2−, 1− ions.

The ions pack into a **giant ionic lattice**: each ion is surrounded by oppositely charged ions, and attractions act in all directions. In sodium chloride each Na⁺ has six Cl⁻ neighbours and each Cl⁻ has six Na⁺ (6 : 6 coordination). In caesium chloride the larger Cs⁺ ion has room for eight Cl⁻ neighbours (8 : 8 coordination, Eduqas).

**Ionic radius.** Positive ions are smaller than their atoms (they lose a shell). Negative ions are larger than their atoms (more electron repulsion, same nuclear charge). For the isoelectronic series N³⁻, O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ (all 10 electrons), radius decreases from N³⁻ to Al³⁺ because the nuclear charge rises from 7 to 13 while the electron arrangement stays the same.

**Strength.** Smaller, more highly charged ions attract more strongly, so MgO (2+, 2−) has a much higher melting point than NaCl (1+, 1−).

**Evidence for ions (Edexcel).** When a coloured salt such as copper(II) chromate(VI) is electrolysed on damp filter paper, the blue colour (Cu²⁺) moves to the negative electrode and the yellow colour (CrO₄²⁻) moves to the positive one.

### Common mistakes

- Calling an ionic lattice "molecules of NaCl".
- Saying the bond forms because the atoms "want a full shell". The bond is the attraction between the ions.
- Drawing ionic dot-and-cross diagrams without square brackets and charges.

## 3.1.3.2 Nature of covalent and dative covalent bonds

*Refs: AQA 3.1.3.2 · Edexcel 2.7 to 2.9 · OCR A 2.2.2(d), (e), (f) · OCR B EL(i) · Eduqas C1.4(b). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- A single covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons. Multiple bonds share two or three pairs.
- A dative covalent (coordinate) bond is a shared pair in which both electrons come from one atom.
- Dot-and-cross diagrams of molecules, including those with expanded octets (SF₆, PCl₅) and fewer than eight electrons (BF₃).
- Bond length and bond strength (Edexcel): shorter bonds are generally stronger.

### Explained

A covalent bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the nuclei of the two bonded atoms.

**Dative bonds.** In the ammonium ion, the lone pair on N in NH₃ forms a bond to H⁺: NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺. Once formed, all four N–H bonds are identical. Other examples: H₃O⁺ (from H₂O + H⁺), and the Al₂Cl₆ dimer (a lone pair on Cl of one AlCl₃ donates to the Al of another). Show a dative bond with an arrow from the donor to the acceptor.

**Breaking the octet rule.** Boron in BF₃ has only six outer electrons. Elements in Period 3 and beyond can use d orbitals and have more than eight: phosphorus in PCl₅ (10), sulfur in SF₆ (12).

**Length and strength.** C≡C (0.120 nm) is shorter and stronger than C=C (0.134 nm), which is shorter and stronger than C–C (0.154 nm). More shared electrons pull the nuclei closer.

### Common mistakes

- Forgetting lone pairs in dot-and-cross diagrams.
- Thinking a dative bond is weaker or different once made. In NH₄⁺ all four bonds are the same.

## 3.1.3.3 Metallic bonding

*Refs: AQA 3.1.3.3 · Edexcel 2.22 · OCR A 3.1.1(d) · OCR B EL(j) · Eduqas C1.5(d). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Metallic bonding is the attraction between a lattice of positive metal ions and delocalised electrons.

### Explained

Metal atoms lose their outer electrons into a "sea" of delocalised electrons shared by the whole structure. The positive ions sit in a regular giant lattice. Metallic bond strength increases with more delocalised electrons per atom and smaller ions with higher charge. So magnesium (2 electrons per atom, Mg²⁺) has a higher melting point than sodium (1, Na⁺).

- Metals conduct electricity because the delocalised electrons move through the structure when a voltage is applied.
- They conduct heat because the electrons and the vibrating ions pass on energy.
- They are malleable and ductile because layers of ions can slide over each other without breaking the bonding.

### Common mistakes

- Saying metals contain "free electrons" with no mention of the positive ions they attract.
- Saying metal ions move to conduct electricity. The electrons move.

## 3.1.3.4 Bonding and physical properties

*Refs: AQA 3.1.3.4 · Edexcel 2.6, 2.23 to 2.27 · OCR A 2.2.2(b), (c), (n), (o), 3.1.1(e), (f) · OCR B EL(j), EL(l) · Eduqas C1.5(a) to (e). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The four types of crystal structure: ionic, metallic, macromolecular (giant covalent) and molecular.
- Diamond and graphite structures; ice and iodine as molecular crystals.
- Explain energy changes in melting and boiling from the bonding and structure.
- Predict structure from physical data and the reverse.

### Explained

| Structure | Examples | Particles and forces | Melting point | Conducts? | Soluble in water? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Giant ionic | NaCl, MgO | Ions; strong attractions in all directions | High | Only when molten or dissolved (ions free to move) | Often |
| Giant metallic | Na, Fe | Positive ions and delocalised electrons | Usually high | Yes, solid and liquid | No |
| Giant covalent (macromolecular) | Diamond, graphite, SiO₂ | Atoms joined by covalent bonds throughout | Very high | No (except graphite) | No |
| Simple molecular | I₂, ice, CO₂ | Molecules; covalent inside, weak forces between | Low | No | Varies |

**Diamond.** Each carbon is covalently bonded to four others in a tetrahedral arrangement. All electrons are in bonds, so it does not conduct. Very hard and very high melting point because many strong covalent bonds must break.

**Graphite.** Each carbon bonds to three others in flat hexagonal layers. The fourth electron of each carbon is delocalised across the layer, so graphite conducts along the layers. Weak London forces between layers let them slide, so it is soft. High melting point because the covalent bonds in the layers must break.

**Graphene (Edexcel)** is a single layer of graphite: very strong, light and an excellent conductor. **Carbon nanotubes** are graphene rolled into a cylinder; strong, conducting, and used to carry drugs and in electronics.

**Silicon dioxide** is like diamond with an O between each pair of Si atoms.

**Iodine** forms a molecular lattice of I₂ molecules held by London forces. It sublimes on gentle heating.

**Ice** is a molecular lattice in which each water molecule forms hydrogen bonds to four others, in an open tetrahedral arrangement.

**Melting a molecular substance breaks the forces between molecules, not the covalent bonds.** When iodine sublimes, I₂ molecules separate; the I–I bond stays.

### Common mistakes

- Saying covalent bonds break when a simple molecular substance melts.
- Saying ionic solids conduct because electrons move. Ions move, and only when molten or in solution.
- Saying graphite conducts because of "free ions".

## 3.1.3.5 Shapes of simple molecules and ions

*Refs: AQA 3.1.3.5 · Edexcel 2.10 to 2.12 · OCR A 2.2.2(g), (h) · OCR B EL(k) · Eduqas C1.4(h), (i). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Electron pairs around the central atom repel and move as far apart as possible.
- Lone pair–lone pair repulsion > lone pair–bonding pair > bonding pair–bonding pair.
- Predict shapes and bond angles for molecules and ions with up to six electron pairs (including lone pairs) around the central atom.

### Explained

**Method.**

1. Count the outer electrons on the central atom.
2. Add one for each atom bonded (for single bonds, each bonded atom provides one electron).
3. Adjust for charge: add one per negative charge, subtract one per positive charge.
4. Divide by two to get the number of electron pairs.
5. Bonding pairs = number of bonded atoms. Lone pairs = the rest.
6. Treat a double bond as one "region" of electron density for shape.

| Pairs | Bonding | Lone | Shape | Angle | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 2 | 0 | Linear | 180° | BeCl₂, CO₂ |
| 3 | 3 | 0 | Trigonal planar | 120° | BF₃ |
| 4 | 4 | 0 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° | CH₄, NH₄⁺ |
| 4 | 3 | 1 | Trigonal pyramidal | 107° | NH₃ |
| 4 | 2 | 2 | Bent (V-shaped) | 104.5° | H₂O |
| 5 | 5 | 0 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 90° and 120° | PCl₅ |
| 6 | 6 | 0 | Octahedral | 90° | SF₆ |
| 6 | 4 | 2 | Square planar | 90° | XeF₄ |
| 3 | 2 | 1 | Bent | about 118° | SO₂ |

Each lone pair closes the angle between bonding pairs by about 2.5°, because lone pairs are held closer to the central atom and repel more.

To explain a shape in an exam: state the number of bonding pairs and lone pairs, say the pairs repel as far apart as possible, say lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs, then give the shape and angle.

### Worked example

Predict the shape of ClF₄⁻.

Cl has 7 outer electrons, + 4 from F, + 1 for the charge = 12, so 6 pairs. 4 bonding, 2 lone. Square planar, 90°.

### Common mistakes

- Forgetting to adjust for the charge of an ion.
- Calling water "linear". It has two lone pairs.
- Giving 109° for NH₃. It is 107°.

## 3.1.3.6 Bond polarity

*Refs: AQA 3.1.3.6 · Edexcel 2.13 to 2.15 · OCR A 2.2.2(i), (j) · OCR B OZ(a) · Eduqas C1.4(c), (d). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Electronegativity is the power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
- Electronegativity difference causes bond polarity; a big enough difference gives ionic bonding.
- A molecule with polar bonds is non-polar if it is symmetrical and the dipoles cancel.

### Explained

Electronegativity increases across a period (more protons, similar shielding, smaller atoms) and decreases down a group (larger atoms, more shielding). Fluorine is the most electronegative element (Pauling value 4.0), then oxygen (3.5), nitrogen and chlorine (3.0).

In H–Cl the electrons are pulled towards Cl, which becomes δ−; H becomes δ+. The bond has a dipole.

Bonding is a continuum: pure covalent (equal sharing, e.g. Cl₂) → polar covalent → ionic. Ionic compounds can have some covalent character when a small, highly charged positive ion distorts a large negative ion (file 8).

**Polar or non-polar molecule?** CO₂ is linear, so its two C=O dipoles cancel: non-polar. CCl₄ is tetrahedral and symmetrical: non-polar. H₂O is bent and CHCl₃ is not symmetrical: polar.

### Common mistakes

- Saying electronegativity is the attraction for "an electron". It is for a bonding pair in a covalent bond.
- Saying CO₂ has no polar bonds. It has polar bonds that cancel.

## 3.1.3.7 Forces between molecules

*Refs: AQA 3.1.3.7 · Edexcel 2.16 to 2.21 · OCR A 2.2.2(k), (l), (m) · OCR B OZ(b), OZ(c), OZ(d) · Eduqas C1.4(e) to (g), C1.5(c). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Three types of intermolecular force: induced dipole–dipole (London or van der Waals) forces, permanent dipole–dipole forces, and hydrogen bonding.
- How each arises, and their relative strength.
- Explain boiling points and other physical properties, including the anomalous properties of water.
- Edexcel: how intermolecular forces affect the choice of solvent and solubility.

### Explained

**London forces (induced dipole–dipole).** Electrons move constantly, so at any instant the electron cloud may be uneven, giving a temporary dipole. This induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule, and the two attract. They act between all molecules. They get stronger with more electrons (larger molecules) and with more contact between molecules (straight chains pack closer than branched ones).

**Permanent dipole–dipole forces** act between polar molecules, such as HCl or propanone: the δ+ end of one attracts the δ− end of another. They act as well as London forces.

**Hydrogen bonding** is the strongest. It needs:

1. H bonded directly to N, O or F (very electronegative, so the H is strongly δ+ and its electron is pulled away, leaving an almost bare proton);
2. a lone pair on an N, O or F of another molecule.

Draw it with the δ charges, the lone pair and a dashed line between the H and the lone pair; the O–H···O arrangement is close to 180°.

**Boiling point trends.**

- Noble gases and halogens: boiling point rises down the group as the number of electrons, and so London forces, increase.
- Alkanes: boiling point rises with chain length; branched isomers boil lower.
- Group 6 hydrides: H₂O boils far higher (100 °C) than H₂S (−60 °C), because of hydrogen bonds. From H₂S to H₂Te the boiling point rises with London forces.

**Anomalous properties of water.**

1. High melting and boiling points for its size: hydrogen bonds need extra energy to break.
2. Ice is less dense than liquid water: in ice, hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open lattice with more space than in the liquid, so ice floats.
3. Also high surface tension and high specific heat capacity.

**Solvents (Edexcel 2.21).** A substance dissolves when the new attractions to the solvent are comparable to the ones broken. Ionic solids dissolve in water because the ions are attracted to the polar water molecules (hydration). Alcohols dissolve in water because they hydrogen bond to it; solubility falls as the hydrocarbon chain gets longer. Non-polar substances dissolve in non-polar solvents such as hexane, because London forces are similar on both sides; they do not dissolve in water because they cannot break the hydrogen bonds between water molecules.

### Worked example

Explain why propan-1-ol (Mᵣ 60) boils at 97 °C but butane (Mᵣ 58) boils at −1 °C.

Both have similar numbers of electrons, so similar London forces. Propan-1-ol has an O–H group so it forms hydrogen bonds between molecules; butane only has London forces. More energy is needed to overcome hydrogen bonds.

### Common mistakes

- Saying hydrogen bonds form with any H atom. The H must be on N, O or F.
- Saying boiling breaks covalent bonds inside molecules.
- Ranking forces only by type without thinking about size: a large non-polar molecule can have stronger total London forces than a small polar one.

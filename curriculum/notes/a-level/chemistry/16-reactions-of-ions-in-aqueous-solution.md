# Reactions of metal aqua ions in aqueous solution

Metal ions in water are surrounded by water ligands. This file explains why some aqua ions are acidic, and gives the reactions of the common metal aqua ions with hydroxide ions, ammonia and carbonate ions: the observations, the equations and the amphoteric hydroxides. It includes AQA required practical 11.

## Overview

A-level only. You need complexes and ligand substitution from file 15 and Brønsted–Lowry acids from file 11.

## 3.2.6 Reactions of ions in aqueous solution

*Refs: AQA 3.2.6, RP11 · Edexcel 15.24, 15.25, CP15 · OCR A 5.3.1(j), 5.3.2(a) · Eduqas PI2.2(j). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- In water, metal ions form [M(H₂O)₆]²⁺ and [M(H₂O)₆]³⁺ (M = Fe, Cu, Al, Cr, Mn, Co, Ni, Zn).
- The acidity of [M(H₂O)₆]³⁺ is greater than that of [M(H₂O)₆]²⁺, explained by charge density and polarisation of water ligands.
- Some metal hydroxides are amphoteric: Al(OH)₃ (and Cr(OH)₃, Zn(OH)₂).
- Observations and equations for reactions with OH⁻, NH₃ and CO₃²⁻.

### Explained

**Acidity (hydrolysis).** The positive metal ion attracts electron density from the O–H bonds of its water ligands. The O–H bond weakens and an H⁺ can be released to a solvent water molecule:

[Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + H₂O ⇌ [Fe(H₂O)₅(OH)]²⁺ + H₃O⁺

M³⁺ ions are smaller and more highly charged than M²⁺ ions, so they have a higher charge density and polarise the water ligands more. More H⁺ is released, so solutions of M³⁺ are more acidic (pH about 2 to 3 for Fe³⁺, about 5 to 6 for Fe²⁺).

**With hydroxide ions or ammonia: precipitation.** OH⁻ (or NH₃ acting as a base) removes H⁺ from the water ligands until a neutral hydroxide forms. Neutral complexes are insoluble.

[M(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → M(H₂O)₄(OH)₂ + 2H₂O
[M(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → M(H₂O)₃(OH)₃ + 3H₂O

With ammonia: [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2NH₃ → Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂ + 2NH₄⁺. In small amounts, ammonia acts as a base, not a ligand.

**In excess:** some precipitates dissolve.

- Amphoteric hydroxides dissolve in excess NaOH: Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃ + OH⁻ → [Al(OH)₄]⁻ + 3H₂O. They also dissolve in acid: Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃ + 3H⁺ → [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺.
- Some dissolve in excess ammonia by ligand substitution: Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂ + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ + 2H₂O.

**With carbonate ions.**

- M²⁺ ions are weakly acidic, so a metal carbonate precipitates: [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → FeCO₃ + 6H₂O; [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → CuCO₃ + 6H₂O.
- M³⁺ ions are acidic enough to react with carbonate as an acid; the hydroxide precipitates and CO₂ is released (effervescence): 2[Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 3CO₃²⁻ → 2Fe(H₂O)₃(OH)₃ + 3CO₂ + 3H₂O. Al³⁺ and Cr³⁺ behave in the same way.

**Summary table.**

| Ion | Aqua ion colour | NaOH(aq) (a little) | NaOH excess | NH₃(aq) (a little) | NH₃ excess | Na₂CO₃(aq) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fe²⁺ | Pale green | Green ppt Fe(OH)₂; turns brown on standing in air | No change | Green ppt | No change | Green ppt FeCO₃ |
| Cu²⁺ | Blue | Pale blue ppt Cu(OH)₂ | No change | Pale blue ppt | Deep blue solution [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ | Blue-green ppt CuCO₃ |
| Fe³⁺ | Yellow / orange-brown (pale violet if not hydrolysed) | Orange-brown ppt Fe(OH)₃ | No change | Orange-brown ppt | No change | Orange-brown ppt and bubbles of CO₂ |
| Al³⁺ | Colourless | White ppt Al(OH)₃ | Dissolves: colourless [Al(OH)₄]⁻ | White ppt | No change | White ppt and bubbles |
| Cr³⁺ | Green (violet [Cr(H₂O)₆]³⁺) | Grey-green ppt Cr(OH)₃ | Dissolves: dark green [Cr(OH)₆]³⁻ | Grey-green ppt | Purple solution [Cr(NH₃)₆]³⁺ (slowly) | Grey-green ppt and bubbles |
| Mn²⁺ | Very pale pink | Pale brown ppt Mn(OH)₂, darkens in air | No change | Pale brown ppt | No change | — |
| Co²⁺ | Pink | Blue ppt, turns pink | No change | Blue ppt | Straw-yellow [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺, darkens in air | Pink / purple ppt |
| Ni²⁺ | Green | Green ppt | No change | Green ppt | Blue / violet [Ni(NH₃)₆]²⁺ | Green ppt |

Fe(OH)₂ turns brown in air because it is oxidised to Fe(OH)₃.

### Worked example

Write equations for the reaction of aqueous chromium(III) ions with a little sodium hydroxide, and then with excess.

[Cr(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Cr(H₂O)₃(OH)₃ + 3H₂O
Cr(H₂O)₃(OH)₃ + 3OH⁻ → [Cr(OH)₆]³⁻ + 3H₂O

### Common mistakes

- Writing CO₂ in the reaction of Fe²⁺ or Cu²⁺ with carbonate. Only 3+ ions give CO₂.
- Saying the pH difference is due to "more water molecules". It is the higher charge density of M³⁺.
- Forgetting that ammonia acts as a base first and then, in excess, as a ligand for Cu²⁺ (and Co²⁺, Ni²⁺, Zn²⁺, Cr³⁺).
- Leaving the charge off neutral hydroxides: they have no charge, which is why they precipitate.

## Mark scheme

1. Fe³⁺ smaller and higher charge, so higher charge density (1); polarises / attracts electrons from O–H bonds in water ligands more (1); more H⁺ released (1).
2. [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + H₂O ⇌ [Al(H₂O)₅(OH)]²⁺ + H₃O⁺ (1).
3. Pale blue precipitate (1); dissolves in excess to deep blue solution (1); [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2NH₃ → Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂ + 2NH₄⁺ (1); Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂ + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ + 2H₂O (1).
4. Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃ + 3H⁺ → [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ (1); Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃ + OH⁻ → [Al(OH)₄]⁻ + 3H₂O (1).
5. Fe²⁺: green precipitate of FeCO₃, no gas (1). Fe³⁺: orange-brown precipitate and effervescence (1). Fe³⁺ aqua ion more acidic (1), so reacts with carbonate to give CO₂ and Fe(OH)₃ (1).
6. Fe²⁺ (1); Fe(OH)₂ oxidised by air to Fe(OH)₃ (1).
7. Add NaOH(aq) to excess (1); Al³⁺ white precipitate dissolves in excess, Mg²⁺ precipitate does not (1).

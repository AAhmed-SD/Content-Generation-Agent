# Acids, bases, pH, Ka, titration curves and buffers

This file treats acids and bases as proton transfer equilibria. It covers Brønsted–Lowry acids and bases, pH and its calculation for strong acids, strong bases and weak acids, Kw, Ka and pKa, pH curves and indicators, and buffers. It includes AQA required practical 9.

A-level for most boards (Eduqas has pH at AS). You need equilibrium constants from file 6 and titration skills from file 2.

## 3.1.12.1 Brønsted–Lowry acid–base equilibria in aqueous solution

*Refs: AQA 3.1.12.1 · Edexcel 12.1 to 12.3, 12.7 · OCR A 5.1.3(a), (b) · OCR B O(i), O(j) · Eduqas PI5.2(a), C2.1(g). Level: A-level only (Eduqas C2.1(g) AS).*

### What you need to know

- An acid is a proton (H⁺) donor; a base is a proton acceptor.
- Acid–base equilibria involve the transfer of protons. Identify conjugate acid–base pairs.
- Strong acids dissociate fully in water; weak acids partly.

### Explained

HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻. HCl donates a proton to water.

In NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻, water is the acid and NH₃ the base. Water can act as either (amphoteric).

**Conjugate pairs** differ by one H⁺. In CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺:

- CH₃COOH (acid 1) and CH₃COO⁻ (base 1)
- H₂O (base 2) and H₃O⁺ (acid 2)

In aqueous solution H⁺(aq) and H₃O⁺(aq) mean the same thing.

**Strong and weak.** A strong acid (HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄ first proton) is fully dissociated; its equilibrium lies completely to the right. A weak acid (carboxylic acids, carbonic acid) only partly dissociates. Strong and weak describe the extent of dissociation; concentrated and dilute describe the amount of acid per dm³.

**Reactions of acids (OCR A 5.1.3(b)).** In all acid reactions with metals, carbonates, bases and alkalis, the H⁺ ion is what reacts: 2H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → H₂O + CO₂; H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O; 2H⁺ + Mg → Mg²⁺ + H₂.

### Common mistakes

- Pairing an acid with a species that does not differ by exactly one H⁺.
- Using "strong" to mean "concentrated".

## 3.1.12.2 Definition and determination of pH

*Refs: AQA 3.1.12.2 · Edexcel 12.4 to 12.6, 12.8 · OCR A 5.1.3(d), (f) · OCR B O(l) · Eduqas C2.1(e), PI5.2(d). Level: A-level only (Eduqas C2.1(e) AS).*

### What you need to know

- pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]; [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ.
- Calculate pH of strong monoprotic acids.
- A tenfold change in [H⁺] changes pH by 1.

### Explained

For a strong monoprotic acid, [H⁺] = [acid]. 0.010 mol dm⁻³ HCl has pH = −log(0.010) = 2.00.

For a strong diprotic acid assumed fully dissociated, [H⁺] = 2 × [acid] (for example sulfuric acid in simple questions).

pH values are usually given to 2 decimal places.

### Worked example

1. pH of 0.25 mol dm⁻³ HNO₃: −log(0.25) = 0.60.
2. [H⁺] in a solution of pH 3.40: 10⁻³·⁴⁰ = 3.98 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³.
3. 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl is diluted to 500 cm³. New pH: [H⁺] = 0.100 × 25.0/500 = 5.00 × 10⁻³; pH = 2.30.

### Common mistakes

- Using ln instead of log₁₀.
- Forgetting to account for dilution or volume change.

## 3.1.12.3 The ionic product of water, Kw

*Refs: AQA 3.1.12.3 · Edexcel 12.11 to 12.13 · OCR A 5.1.3(e), (f) · OCR B O(l) · Eduqas PI5.2(c), (d). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Water dissociates slightly: H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻.
- Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 298 K.
- In pure water [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = √Kw.
- Calculate pH of strong bases using Kw.
- Kw changes with temperature. pKw = −log Kw.

### Explained

Kw is derived from Kc: Kc = [H⁺][OH⁻] ÷ [H₂O], and [H₂O] is so large it is effectively constant, so it is absorbed into Kw.

Dissociation of water is endothermic, so Kw increases with temperature. At 50 °C, Kw ≈ 5.5 × 10⁻¹⁴, so pure water has pH ≈ 6.63. It is still neutral because [H⁺] = [OH⁻].

**Strong bases.** 0.050 mol dm⁻³ NaOH: [OH⁻] = 0.050. [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 0.050 = 2.0 × 10⁻¹³. pH = 12.70. For Ba(OH)₂ or Ca(OH)₂, [OH⁻] = 2 × concentration.

### Common mistakes

- Saying water at 50 °C is acidic because its pH is below 7.
- Forgetting to double [OH⁻] for Group 2 hydroxides.

## 3.1.12.4 Weak acids and bases, Ka

*Refs: AQA 3.1.12.4 · Edexcel 12.9, 12.10, 12.13 to 12.15, CP9 · OCR A 5.1.3(c), (g), (h) · OCR B O(k), O(l) · Eduqas PI5.2(b), (d). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- For HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] ÷ [HA]. Units mol dm⁻³.
- pKa = −log Ka; Ka = 10⁻ᵖᴷᵃ. A smaller pKa means a stronger acid.
- Calculate pH of a weak acid from Ka and concentration, and Ka from pH.
- The two approximations and their limits.

### Explained

For a weak acid alone in water, assume:

1. [H⁺] = [A⁻] (dissociation of water is negligible).
2. [HA] at equilibrium ≈ initial concentration (so little dissociates).

Then Ka = [H⁺]² ÷ [HA], so [H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA]).

**Limits (OCR A 5.1.3(h)).** Approximation 2 breaks down for "stronger" weak acids (Ka above about 10⁻²) or very dilute solutions, where a significant fraction dissociates. Approximation 1 breaks down for very weak or very dilute acids, where H⁺ from water matters.

**Weak bases** such as ammonia only partly accept protons; their pH is calculated with Kb at some boards, but AQA, OCR and Edexcel only require qualitative treatment.

### Worked example

1. pH of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid, Ka = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.

   [H⁺] = √(1.74 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.100) = √(1.74 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.32 × 10⁻³. pH = 2.88.

2. A 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ solution of a weak acid has pH 3.10. Find Ka and pKa.

   [H⁺] = 7.94 × 10⁻⁴. Ka = (7.94 × 10⁻⁴)² ÷ 0.0500 = 1.26 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. pKa = 4.90.

### Practical

**Edexcel CP9: finding Ka for a weak acid.** Method 1: measure the pH of a solution of known concentration with a calibrated pH meter and use Ka = [H⁺]² ÷ [HA]. Method 2: titrate a weak acid with NaOH to find the end-point volume, then add half that volume of NaOH to a fresh sample of acid; at half-neutralisation [HA] = [A⁻], so pH = pKa. Calibrate the pH meter with buffer solutions of known pH (for example pH 4, 7 and 10) first, and rinse the probe with distilled water between readings.

### Common mistakes

- Using the approximations in a buffer calculation (they only work for the weak acid alone).
- Forgetting to square-root.

## 3.1.12.5 pH curves, titrations and indicators

*Refs: AQA 3.1.12.5, RP9 · Edexcel 12.16, 12.17, 12.22, 12.23 · OCR A 5.1.3(n), (o) · Eduqas PI5.2(e), (h), (i). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The shapes of pH curves for strong acid–strong base, strong acid–weak base, weak acid–strong base and weak acid–weak base titrations.
- Equivalence point and the vertical section.
- Choose an indicator whose colour change (pH range) lies within the vertical section.
- For a weak acid–strong base titration, pH at half-neutralisation = pKa.
- Edexcel: why weak acids have less exothermic enthalpies of neutralisation.
- Eduqas: salt hydrolysis gives non-neutral solutions at the equivalence point.

### Explained

Typical curves (acid in the flask, base added):

| Titration | Start pH | Equivalence point pH | Vertical section | Indicator |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strong acid–strong base | ~1 | 7 | About 3 to 11 | Either |
| Weak acid–strong base | ~3 | ~9 | About 7 to 11 | Phenolphthalein (8.3 to 10) |
| Strong acid–weak base | ~1 | ~5 | About 3 to 7 | Methyl orange (3.1 to 4.4) |
| Weak acid–weak base | ~3 | ~7 | No sharp vertical section | None suitable; use a pH meter |

For a weak acid–strong base curve, there is a buffer region at the start where pH rises slowly. Half-way to the equivalence point, [HA] = [A⁻], so Ka = [H⁺] and pH = pKa.

**Indicators** are weak acids whose HIn and In⁻ forms differ in colour: HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻. The colour changes over about 2 pH units around the pKa of the indicator. The end point (colour change) should match the equivalence point.

**Salt hydrolysis (Eduqas).** At the equivalence point of a weak acid–strong base titration the solution contains A⁻ ions, which accept protons from water: A⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HA + OH⁻. So sodium ethanoate solution is alkaline. Ammonium chloride solution is acidic (NH₄⁺ ⇌ NH₃ + H⁺). Sodium chloride solution is neutral.

**Enthalpy of neutralisation (Edexcel 12.23).** Strong acid with strong base: about −57 kJ mol⁻¹, the same for all because the reaction is always H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O. A weak acid gives a less exothermic value, because some energy is used to dissociate the acid as it reacts.

### Practical

**AQA RP9: investigate how pH changes when a weak acid reacts with a strong base and when a strong acid reacts with a weak base.** Calibrate the pH meter. Pipette 25.0 cm³ of acid into a beaker with a magnetic stirrer. Add base from a burette in 1 cm³ portions, reading pH after each, and in 0.1 cm³ portions near the equivalence point. Plot pH against volume. Find the equivalence point (middle of the vertical section) and, for the weak acid, read pKa at half the equivalence volume.

**Eduqas: titration using a pH probe**, for example a weak acid against a weak base, shows the small change at equivalence.

### Common mistakes

- Choosing methyl orange for a weak acid–strong base titration.
- Saying the equivalence point is always pH 7.
- Reading pKa at the equivalence point instead of half-way.

## 3.1.12.6 Buffer action

*Refs: AQA 3.1.12.6 · Edexcel 12.18 to 12.21, 12.24 · OCR A 5.1.3(i) to (m) · OCR B O(m) · Eduqas PI5.2(f), (g). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- A buffer resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added, or on dilution.
- Acidic buffers: a weak acid and one of its salts (or excess weak acid partly neutralised by a strong alkali). Basic buffers: a weak base and its salt (ammonia and ammonium chloride).
- How buffers work, in terms of the equilibrium and the large reservoirs of HA and A⁻.
- Calculate the pH of acidic buffers, including after adding small amounts of acid or base.
- Uses: blood (carbonic acid–hydrogencarbonate), shampoos, biological washing powders, food, fermentation.

### Explained

**Ethanoic acid / sodium ethanoate buffer.** CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺. The sodium ethanoate supplies lots of CH₃COO⁻; the acid supplies lots of CH₃COOH.

- Add a little acid (H⁺): it reacts with CH₃COO⁻ to make CH₃COOH. The equilibrium shifts left; [H⁺] barely changes.
- Add a little alkali (OH⁻): it reacts with H⁺ (H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O); more CH₃COOH dissociates to replace the H⁺. Also CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O directly. [H⁺] barely changes.

Because both [HA] and [A⁻] are large, their ratio changes little, so pH changes little.

**Basic buffer.** NH₃ + H⁺ ⇌ NH₄⁺. Added acid is taken up by NH₃; added alkali reacts with NH₄⁺: NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O.

**Calculating buffer pH.** [H⁺] = Ka × [HA] ÷ [A⁻]. Do not use the weak-acid approximations; use the actual amounts of acid and salt. Because both are in the same volume, mole ratios can be used. (Equivalent form: pH = pKa + log([A⁻] ÷ [HA]).)

**Blood (Edexcel 12.24, OCR A 5.1.3(m), Eduqas).** Blood pH is kept at 7.35 to 7.45 mainly by H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. Extra acid reacts with HCO₃⁻ to form H₂CO₃; the H₂CO₃ is converted to CO₂ and water and the CO₂ is breathed out. Extra alkali reacts with H₂CO₃. Proteins and phosphate ions also buffer.

### Worked example

1. A buffer contains 0.200 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid and 0.150 mol dm⁻³ sodium ethanoate. Ka = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵. Find the pH.

   [H⁺] = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.200 ÷ 0.150 = 2.32 × 10⁻⁵. pH = 4.63.

2. To 1.00 dm³ of that buffer, 0.0100 mol of HCl is added. Find the new pH.

   H⁺ reacts with A⁻: A⁻ = 0.150 − 0.010 = 0.140 mol; HA = 0.200 + 0.010 = 0.210 mol. [H⁺] = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.210 ÷ 0.140 = 2.61 × 10⁻⁵. pH = 4.58. A change of only 0.05.

3. 50.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid is mixed with 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. Find the pH.

   HA = 0.0100 mol; OH⁻ = 0.00250 mol. After reaction: HA = 0.00750 mol, A⁻ = 0.00250 mol. [H⁺] = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.00750 ÷ 0.00250 = 5.22 × 10⁻⁵. pH = 4.28.

### Common mistakes

- Saying a buffer keeps the pH constant. It resists change; large additions overwhelm it.
- Using [H⁺] = √(Ka[HA]) for a buffer.
- Forgetting to subtract the reacted amounts before using the buffer equation.

# Kinetics: collision theory, Maxwell–Boltzmann and catalysts

Kinetics is about how fast reactions go. This file covers collision theory, the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, the effects of temperature, concentration, pressure and surface area, measuring rates, and how catalysts work. It includes AQA required practical 3.

## 3.1.5.1 Collision theory

*Refs: AQA 3.1.5.1 · Edexcel 9.1, 9.2 · OCR A 3.2.2(a) · OCR B OZ(e), OZ(f) · Eduqas C2.3(a), (c). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Reactions happen only when particles collide with enough energy (at least the activation energy) and in the right orientation.
- Activation energy, Eₐ, is the minimum energy for a reaction to occur.
- Most collisions do not lead to reaction.

### Explained

Rate of reaction is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time (mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹). It depends on the frequency of **successful** collisions. Anything that makes collisions more frequent, or gives a larger fraction of them enough energy, speeds up the reaction.

The energy is needed to start breaking bonds. Even exothermic reactions need it, which is why a match is needed to light methane.

### Common mistakes

- Saying "more collisions" without "per unit time" or "more frequent".
- Leaving out orientation or energy when defining a successful collision.

## 3.1.5.2 Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution

*Refs: AQA 3.1.5.2 · Edexcel 9.4 · OCR A 3.2.2(f) · Eduqas C2.3(d). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution shows the spread of molecular energies in a gas.
- Draw and explain the curve; mark Eₐ on it.

### Explained

The curve plots number of molecules against energy.

- It starts at the origin: no molecules have zero energy.
- It rises to a peak (the most probable energy), then falls.
- It never touches the energy axis at high energy (asymptote): a few molecules have very high energies.
- The area under the curve equals the total number of molecules.
- The area to the right of Eₐ is the number of molecules with enough energy to react.

The mean energy is slightly to the right of the peak.

### Common mistakes

- Starting the curve above zero or making it touch the axis at high energy.
- Drawing the curve symmetrical like a bell.

## 3.1.5.3 Effect of temperature on reaction rate

*Refs: AQA 3.1.5.3, RP3 · Edexcel 9.1, 9.4 · OCR A 3.2.2(g) · Eduqas C2.3(d), (h). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- A small rise in temperature gives a large increase in rate.
- Explain with the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution.
- Draw the higher-temperature curve.

### Explained

At a higher temperature the curve shifts to the right and becomes flatter and lower, with the same area (same number of molecules). The peak moves to higher energy.

The key point: **many more molecules have energy at least Eₐ.** The area to the right of Eₐ grows much more than the average energy does. That is why a 10 K rise can roughly double the rate. Collisions are also slightly more frequent because molecules move faster, but this is a small effect compared with the energy change.

### Common mistakes

- Making the higher-temperature curve taller.
- Saying the main reason is "more collisions". It is that more collisions have enough energy.
- Moving Eₐ. Temperature does not change the activation energy.

### Practical

**AQA RP3: investigate how the rate of a reaction changes with temperature.** A common method is sodium thiosulfate with hydrochloric acid: Na₂S₂O₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + SO₂ + S + H₂O. The sulfur makes the mixture cloudy. Time how long it takes for a cross under the flask to disappear. Warm the thiosulfate in a water bath to the chosen temperature before adding the acid, and measure the temperature of the mixture. Rate ∝ 1/time. Keep the volumes and concentrations constant. The end point is judged by eye, so use the same observer, lighting and cross. Work in a ventilated room because SO₂ is toxic.

**Eduqas: rate by gas collection.** Collect the gas (for example CO₂ from marble chips and acid, or H₂ from magnesium and acid) in a gas syringe and record the volume every 10 to 30 seconds. Alternatively place the flask on a balance and record the mass loss.

**Eduqas: iodine clock.** In one version, hydrogen peroxide oxidises iodide ions to iodine in acid. A small, fixed amount of thiosulfate turns the iodine back into iodide as fast as it forms. When the thiosulfate is used up, iodine builds up and turns starch blue-black suddenly. The time taken is the time to make a fixed amount of iodine, so 1/t is proportional to the initial rate.

## 3.1.5.4 Effect of concentration and pressure

*Refs: AQA 3.1.5.4 · Edexcel 9.1, 9.3 · OCR A 3.2.2(a), (b) · OCR B OZ(f) · Eduqas C2.3(a), (b). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Higher concentration (solutions) or higher pressure (gases) increases collision frequency, so increases rate.
- Larger surface area of a solid also increases rate (Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas).
- Calculate rate from the gradient of a concentration–time (or volume–time) graph.

### Explained

More particles in the same volume means more frequent collisions, so more successful collisions per second. The fraction of collisions with enough energy does not change.

For a solid, only particles on the surface can react. Powder has a much bigger surface area than lumps of the same mass.

**Rate from a graph.** On a graph of concentration (or volume of gas) against time, the rate at any time is the gradient of the tangent at that point. The initial rate is the gradient of the tangent at t = 0. The rate falls as the reaction proceeds because the reactant concentration falls. If a measurement is taken over a whole reaction, mean rate = change ÷ time.

### Worked example

A tangent drawn to a volume–time curve at 20 s passes through (0 s, 12 cm³) and (50 s, 62 cm³). Find the rate at 20 s.

Gradient = (62 − 12) ÷ (50 − 0) = 1.0 cm³ s⁻¹.

### Common mistakes

- Saying that higher concentration gives particles more energy.
- Drawing a tangent that cuts the curve instead of touching it.

## 3.1.5.5 Catalysts

*Refs: AQA 3.1.5.5 · Edexcel 9.5 to 9.9 · OCR A 3.2.2(c), (d), (g) · OCR B DF(h), DF(i), OZ(g), OZ(h) · Eduqas C2.3(e), (f). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- A catalyst increases rate without being used up overall, by providing an alternative reaction route with a lower activation energy.
- Show this on a reaction profile and on a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution.
- Homogeneous catalysts are in the same phase as the reactants; heterogeneous catalysts are in a different phase.
- How a heterogeneous catalyst works: adsorption, reaction, desorption; catalyst poisoning (OCR B, Edexcel).
- Economic and environmental benefits of catalysts.

### Explained

**On a reaction profile**, the catalysed route has a lower hump. ΔH is unchanged.

**On a Maxwell–Boltzmann curve**, the curve does not move. Mark a second, lower Eₐ to the left: a larger area lies to its right, so more molecules can react.

A catalyst takes part in the reaction (it forms an intermediate or bonds to the surface) but is regenerated at the end.

**Heterogeneous catalysis.** Usually a solid catalysing gas reactions. Reactant molecules are **adsorbed** on to active sites on the surface, forming weak bonds. Bonds in the reactants weaken and the molecules are held close together in a good orientation. They react, and the products **desorb**, freeing the site. A good catalyst adsorbs strongly enough to weaken bonds but not so strongly that products cannot leave. Catalysts are spread thinly on a support (a ceramic honeycomb in catalytic converters) to give a large surface area and reduce the amount of expensive metal.

**Catalyst poisons** are impurities that adsorb strongly to active sites and block them. Lead in old petrol poisoned catalytic converters; sulfur compounds poison the iron catalyst in the Haber process.

Examples:

| Catalyst | Type | Process |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Iron | Heterogeneous | Haber process, N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ |
| Platinum, rhodium, palladium | Heterogeneous | Catalytic converters |
| Nickel | Heterogeneous | Hydrogenation of alkenes |
| Concentrated sulfuric acid | Homogeneous | Esterification |
| Cl• radicals | Homogeneous | Ozone breakdown in the stratosphere (file 35) |
| Zeolites | Heterogeneous | Catalytic cracking |

**Benefits.** Catalysts let reactions run at lower temperatures, saving energy and fuel, cutting CO₂ emissions and costs. They can make a process selective, giving more of the wanted product and less waste, which improves atom economy. Enzymes work at body temperature.

### Common mistakes

- Saying catalysts "lower the activation energy of the reaction". Better: they provide an alternative route with a lower Eₐ.
- Moving the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve when a catalyst is added.
- Saying catalysts change the yield at equilibrium. They speed up both directions equally (file 6).

## Mark scheme

1. The minimum energy needed for a reaction to occur / for a collision to be successful (1).
2. Axes: number of molecules (y), energy (x) (1); curve starts at origin, peak, does not touch x-axis at high energy (1); higher T curve peak lower and to the right, same area (1).
3. Many more molecules have energy greater than or equal to Eₐ (1); so more frequent successful collisions (1).
4. Concentration of acid decreases (1); fewer collisions per unit time / less frequent collisions (1).
5. Larger surface area (1); more frequent collisions (1).
6. Reactants adsorb on to active sites on the surface (1); bonds weaken / molecules held in suitable orientation, so lower Eₐ route (1); products desorb (1).
7. Any two: lower temperature so less energy / fuel used; lower costs; less CO₂ emitted; faster production; higher selectivity / less waste.
8. Rate = 1/t: 0.0156 s⁻¹ and 0.0313 s⁻¹ (1); rate roughly doubles for a 10 °C rise (1).

# Amines: preparation, basicity and nucleophilic reactions

Amines are derivatives of ammonia in which hydrogen atoms are replaced by carbon groups. The lone pair on nitrogen makes them bases and nucleophiles. This file covers naming and classifying amines, making them, comparing their base strength, and their reactions as nucleophiles with halogenoalkanes and acyl chlorides.

## 3.3.11.1 Preparation

*Refs: AQA 3.3.11.1 · Edexcel 18.8, 18.11, 18.12 · OCR A 6.2.1(b) · Eduqas OA3.1(a), (b). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Primary, secondary and tertiary amines and quaternary ammonium ions.
- Primary aliphatic amines from halogenoalkanes and excess ammonia, and from reduction of nitriles.
- Aromatic amines from reduction of nitro compounds with tin and concentrated HCl.

### Explained

**Classification.**

| Type | Example | N bonded to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | CH₃NH₂ (methylamine) | One C |
| Secondary | (CH₃)₂NH (dimethylamine) | Two C |
| Tertiary | (CH₃)₃N (trimethylamine) | Three C |
| Quaternary ammonium ion | (CH₃)₄N⁺ | Four C, positive charge |

Names: ethylamine (or ethanamine); phenylamine (C₆H₅NH₂); N-methylethylamine for CH₃CH₂NHCH₃.

**From halogenoalkanes.** CH₃CH₂Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Br. Use excess ammonia in ethanol, heated in a sealed tube. The amine product still has a lone pair, so it can react with more halogenoalkane to form secondary and tertiary amines and a quaternary salt (section 3.3.11.3). Excess ammonia makes the primary amine the main product, but a mixture still forms.

**From nitriles.** Reduce with LiAlH₄ in dry ether, or with hydrogen and a nickel catalyst (catalytic hydrogenation, cheaper for industry):

CH₃CN + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂NH₂

This gives only the primary amine, with no mixture. The nitrile comes from a halogenoalkane and KCN (file 19), so the chain is one carbon longer than the halogenoalkane.

**Aromatic amines.** Nitrobenzene is reduced with tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid under reflux; then sodium hydroxide is added to release the free amine from its salt (C₆H₅NH₃⁺Cl⁻):

C₆H₅NO₂ + 6[H] → C₆H₅NH₂ + 2H₂O

Phenylamine is used to make azo dyes (file 37).

### Common mistakes

- Saying ammonia with a halogenoalkane gives only the primary amine.
- Writing 2[H] for the reduction of a nitrile or nitro group. Nitrile needs 4[H], nitrobenzene 6[H].

## 3.3.11.2 Base properties

*Refs: AQA 3.3.11.2 · Edexcel 18.9, 18.10 · OCR A 6.2.1(a) · OCR B PL(j) · Eduqas OA3.1(c). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Amines are weak bases: the lone pair on N accepts a proton.
- Relative base strength: primary aliphatic amines > ammonia > aromatic amines. Explain using the availability of the lone pair.
- Amines react with acids to form salts.

### Explained

CH₃NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃NH₃⁺ + OH⁻ (aqueous methylamine is alkaline; it smells fishy).

CH₃CH₂NH₂ + HCl → CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻ (ethylammonium chloride). Adding a strong alkali releases the amine again.

**Base strength depends on how available the N lone pair is to accept H⁺.**

- **Primary aliphatic amines** (methylamine) are stronger bases than ammonia. The alkyl group pushes electron density on to the nitrogen (positive inductive effect), making the lone pair more available.
- **Phenylamine** is a weaker base than ammonia. The N lone pair is partly delocalised into the benzene ring, so it is less available to accept a proton.

So: CH₃CH₂NH₂ > NH₃ > C₆H₅NH₂.

**With copper(II) ions (Edexcel 18.9).** A little butylamine solution gives a pale blue precipitate of copper(II) hydroxide (the amine acts as a base, like ammonia). Excess gives a deep blue solution as the amine acts as a ligand, like ammonia (file 16).

### Common mistakes

- Saying phenylamine is weaker because the benzene ring is "big".
- Saying alkyl groups "donate electrons to the hydrogen". They push electron density towards N.

## 3.3.11.3 Nucleophilic properties

*Refs: AQA 3.3.11.3 · Edexcel 18.9, 18.13 · Eduqas OA3.1(d). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Amines are nucleophiles; with halogenoalkanes they give secondary and tertiary amines and quaternary ammonium salts (mechanism).
- Quaternary ammonium salts are used as cationic surfactants.
- Primary amines react with acyl chlorides and anhydrides to give N-substituted amides (mechanism: nucleophilic addition–elimination, file 24).

### Explained

**With halogenoalkanes** (nucleophilic substitution, same mechanism as ammonia):

CH₃CH₂NH₂ + CH₃CH₂Br → (CH₃CH₂)₂NH + HBr (the HBr reacts with more amine)
(CH₃CH₂)₂NH + CH₃CH₂Br → (CH₃CH₂)₃N + HBr
(CH₃CH₂)₃N + CH₃CH₂Br → (CH₃CH₂)₄N⁺Br⁻

Mechanism (each step): the N lone pair attacks the δ+ carbon; C–Br breaks; the N–H⁺ intermediate loses H⁺ to another amine molecule (no H⁺ to lose in the last step, so a salt forms).

**Quaternary ammonium salts as cationic surfactants.** Salts with one long hydrocarbon chain, such as (CH₃)₃N⁺(CH₂)₁₅CH₃ Br⁻, have a positive head that sticks to negatively charged surfaces (hair, fabric) and a non-polar tail. They are used in fabric softeners and hair conditioners, and some are disinfectants.

**With acyl chlorides.** CH₃COCl + 2CH₃CH₂NH₂ → CH₃CONHCH₂CH₃ + CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻ (N-ethylethanamide). Ethanoylation of primary amines (Eduqas OA3.1(d)).

### Common mistakes

- Expecting the reaction of an amine and halogenoalkane to stop at one step.
- Using one amine molecule in the acyl chloride equation.

# Chromatography: TLC, column, GC and HPLC

Chromatography separates the components of a mixture so they can be identified or purified. This file covers the principle (a stationary phase and a mobile phase), thin-layer and paper chromatography with Rf values, column chromatography, gas chromatography with retention times and peak areas, high-performance liquid chromatography, and linking GC and HPLC to mass spectrometry. It includes AQA required practical 12.

## Overview

A-level only. Amino acid analysis links to file 28. Mass spectrometry is in file 22.

- AQA 3.3.16 and RP12 (separation of species by TLC): TLC, column chromatography and gas chromatography.
- Edexcel 19.6 to 19.8: Rf values from one-way chromatograms; HPLC and GC, and combining them with mass spectrometry.

## 3.3.16 Chromatography

*Refs: AQA 3.3.16, RP12 · Edexcel 19.6 to 19.8 · OCR A 6.3.1(a), (b) · OCR B CD(n) · Eduqas OA3.2(g), OA4(g). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- All chromatography uses a stationary phase and a mobile phase. Separation depends on the balance between solubility in the mobile phase and retention (adsorption or solubility) by the stationary phase.
- TLC: Rf = distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent front.
- Column chromatography: purification of a product.
- GC: retention time; peak area gives relative amount; GC–MS for identification.
- HPLC (Edexcel, Eduqas).

### Explained

**Principle.** Components that are held more strongly by the stationary phase (more adsorbed, or more soluble in it) move more slowly. Those more soluble in the mobile phase move faster. So they separate.

| Type | Stationary phase | Mobile phase | Measure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| TLC | Thin layer of silica or alumina on a plate | Liquid solvent | Rf |
| Paper | Water held in the cellulose fibres | Liquid solvent | Rf |
| Column | Silica or alumina powder packed in a column | Liquid solvent (eluent) | Fractions collected |
| GC | Liquid (high boiling) on a solid support inside a long coiled tube in an oven | Unreactive carrier gas (helium, nitrogen) | Retention time |
| HPLC | Small particles of solid packed in a column | Liquid pumped at high pressure | Retention time |

**TLC method (RP12).**

1. Draw a pencil base line about 1 cm from the bottom of the plate (pencil does not dissolve or move).
2. Spot small amounts of each sample and of known reference compounds on the line, using a capillary tube.
3. Stand the plate in a beaker with solvent below the base line (so spots do not dissolve into the solvent). Cover with a lid to keep the atmosphere saturated with solvent vapour.
4. When the solvent is near the top, remove the plate and mark the solvent front in pencil. Dry.
5. Locate colourless spots with UV light (the plate contains a fluorescent dye; spots appear dark) or with a chemical developer: iodine vapour, or ninhydrin for amino acids (purple spots).
6. Measure distances and calculate Rf. Compare with references run on the same plate under the same conditions.

Rf depends on the solvent, temperature and stationary phase, so compare only like with like. If two compounds have similar Rf values, a different solvent may separate them.

**Two-way chromatography (Eduqas).** Run the chromatogram in one solvent, dry, turn the plate or paper through 90°, and run in a second solvent. Spots that overlapped in one direction separate in the other.

**Column chromatography.** The mixture is added to the top of a column of silica or alumina, and solvent is run through. Components travel at different speeds and come out (are eluted) at different times; fractions are collected. Used to purify products in synthesis.

**Gas chromatography.** A small volume of a volatile sample is injected into the heated column. The carrier gas carries it through. Each compound has a characteristic retention time (the time from injection to detection) under fixed conditions: column, temperature, carrier gas flow rate.

- **Peak area** is proportional to the amount of the component. Relative amounts = each area ÷ total area.
- **Calibration (OCR A):** run standards of known concentration, plot peak area against concentration, and read off the unknown.
- Limits: compounds with similar retention times may not separate; unknown compounds cannot be identified from retention time alone.

**GC–MS and HPLC–MS.** The output of the chromatograph is fed into a mass spectrometer. Chromatography separates; the mass spectrum (compared with a database) identifies each component. Used in forensics, drug testing in sport, detecting pollutants and airport security.

**HPLC** is used for substances that would decompose when vaporised (not suitable for GC), such as drugs and food additives. The liquid is pumped through a column of tiny particles at high pressure, giving fast, sharp separation.

### Worked example

1. On a TLC plate the solvent front is 7.5 cm from the base line. A spot is 3.0 cm from the base line. Find Rf.

   Rf = 3.0 ÷ 7.5 = 0.40.

2. A gas chromatogram of a mixture of three esters has peak areas 24, 48 and 8. Find the percentage of the second ester.

   48 ÷ (24 + 48 + 8) × 100 = 60%.

### Practical

**AQA RP12: separation of species by thin-layer chromatography**, for example separating the components of an analgesic, or identifying amino acids from hydrolysis of a protein (file 28) by comparing Rf values with standards. Wear gloves when handling the plate to avoid contaminating it, and when using ninhydrin. Solvents are flammable: no naked flames.

**Eduqas: paper chromatography including two-way separation**, for example of amino acids or dyes.

### Common mistakes

- Drawing the base line in ink.
- Putting the base line below the solvent level.
- Measuring to the front edge of a spot instead of its centre.
- Saying the compound with the larger Rf is "heavier".
- Saying the largest GC peak is the compound with the highest concentration without comparing areas.

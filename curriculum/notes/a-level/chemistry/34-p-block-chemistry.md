# p-block chemistry: Groups 3, 4 and 5, amphoterism and the inert pair

The p block runs from Group 3 to Group 0. Going down Groups 3, 4 and 5, the elements change from non-metals to metals and the lower oxidation state becomes more stable. This file covers amphoteric aluminium and lead, the inert pair effect, compounds with incomplete and expanded octets, Al₂Cl₆ and donor–acceptor compounds, boron nitride, Group 4 oxides and chlorides, and reactions of lead(II) ions.

## PI2.1 Chemistry of the p-block

*Refs: Eduqas PI2.1(a) to (i). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Amphoteric behaviour of Al and Pb (and their ions).
- The inert pair effect: the lower oxidation state (two less than the group number) becomes more stable down Groups 3, 4 and 5.
- Group 3 compounds with fewer than eight outer electrons; Groups 5, 6 and 7 compounds with more than eight.
- Al₂Cl₆ and NH₃BF₃: donor–acceptor (dative) bonding.
- Hexagonal and cubic boron nitride: structure, properties and uses.
- Group 4: stability of +2 and +4 oxidation states; CO as a reducing agent; PbO₂ as an oxidising agent.
- CO₂ and PbO: structure and acid–base character.
- Bonding in CCl₄, SiCl₄ and PbCl₂ and their reactions with water.
- Pb²⁺(aq) with NaOH, Cl⁻ and I⁻.

### Explained

**Amphoterism (PI2.1(a)).** An amphoteric substance reacts with both acids and bases.

- Aluminium metal: 2Al + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂; 2Al + 2NaOH + 6H₂O → 2Na[Al(OH)₄] + 3H₂.
- Aluminium hydroxide: Al(OH)₃ + 3H⁺ → Al³⁺ + 3H₂O; Al(OH)₃ + OH⁻ → [Al(OH)₄]⁻.
- Lead(II) hydroxide: Pb(OH)₂ + 2H⁺ → Pb²⁺ + 2H₂O (use nitric acid, as PbCl₂ and PbSO₄ are insoluble); Pb(OH)₂ + 2OH⁻ → [Pb(OH)₄]²⁻.
- Lead(II) oxide behaves the same way: PbO + 2HNO₃ → Pb(NO₃)₂ + H₂O; PbO + 2NaOH + H₂O → Na₂[Pb(OH)₄].

**The inert pair effect (PI2.1(b)).** In Groups 3, 4 and 5, the outer configuration is ns² np¹, ns² np², ns² np³. Higher in the group, all outer electrons are used (+3, +4, +5). Down the group, the ns² pair is less and less likely to take part, so the oxidation state two below the group number becomes more stable:

| Group | Higher state stable at top | Lower state stable at bottom |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | Al(III) | Tl(I) |
| 4 | C(IV), Si(IV) | Pb(II) |
| 5 | N(V), P(V) | Bi(III) |

Reason: down the group the s electrons are held more tightly than expected (the poorly shielding d and f electrons in between increase the effective nuclear charge), and the bonds formed by the large atoms are weaker, so the energy released by forming two extra bonds no longer pays for using the s pair.

**Octet exceptions (PI2.1(c)).** Group 3 atoms have only three outer electrons, so in BF₃, BCl₃ and AlCl₃ the central atom has six electrons (electron deficient). Elements in Period 3 and below can expand the octet using d orbitals: PCl₅ (10), SF₆ (12), ClF₃ (10), IF₇ (14). Nitrogen and oxygen (Period 2) cannot: there is NCl₃ but no NCl₅.

**Donor–acceptor compounds (PI2.1(d)).** Electron-deficient compounds accept lone pairs.

- **NH₃BF₃:** the lone pair on N forms a dative bond to B. B becomes tetrahedral (four pairs) and N stays tetrahedral.
- **Al₂Cl₆:** aluminium chloride exists as a dimer in the vapour at lower temperatures (and in the solid structure is related). A lone pair on a Cl of one AlCl₃ forms a dative bond to the Al of the other, and vice versa. Each Al is surrounded tetrahedrally by four Cl; two Cl atoms bridge the two Al atoms.

AlCl₃ is covalent (Al³⁺ is small and highly charged, so it polarises Cl⁻ strongly). It reacts vigorously with water, giving steamy HCl fumes, and acts as the Friedel–Crafts catalyst because it accepts a lone pair (file 25).

**Boron nitride (PI2.1(e)).** BN is isoelectronic with C–C (B has 3, N has 5 outer electrons; total 8 per pair, like two carbons).

| | Hexagonal BN | Cubic BN |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structure | Layers of hexagons of alternating B and N, like graphite, with weak forces between layers | Tetrahedral 3D network, like diamond |
| Properties | Soft, slippery, high melting point; does not conduct (electrons are localised on N because N is more electronegative) | Extremely hard, very high melting point, does not conduct |
| Uses | High-temperature lubricant; in cosmetics; electrical insulator that withstands heat | Cutting tools and abrasives (more stable than diamond at high temperature in air; does not react with iron) |

**Group 4 oxidation states (PI2.1(f)).** For carbon, +4 is stable and +2 (in CO) is a reducing agent that is easily oxidised to +4:

- CO reduces metal oxides in the blast furnace: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂.
- CuO + CO → Cu + CO₂.

For lead, +2 is stable and +4 is an oxidising agent that is easily reduced:

- PbO₂ + 4HCl → PbCl₂ + Cl₂ + 2H₂O. Lead goes +4 → +2; chloride is oxidised to chlorine (yellow-green gas).

**CO₂ and PbO (PI2.1(g)).**

| | CO₂ | PbO |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structure and bonding | Simple linear molecules with C=O double bonds; London forces between molecules | Giant ionic lattice (with some covalent character) |
| Physical properties | Colourless gas; sublimes at −78 °C | Yellow or red solid, high melting point |
| Acid–base | Acidic: CO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O; dissolves in water to give carbonic acid | Amphoteric (see above) |

Down Group 4 the oxides change from acidic (CO₂, SiO₂) to amphoteric (SnO, PbO), matching the change from non-metal to metal.

**Group 4 chlorides (PI2.1(h)).** Down the group the bonding changes from covalent to ionic.

| Chloride | Bonding | State | With water |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CCl₄ | Covalent, simple molecular | Colourless liquid | No reaction: carbon has no available d orbitals and the C atom is shielded by four Cl atoms, so water cannot attack |
| SiCl₄ | Covalent, simple molecular | Colourless fuming liquid | Violent hydrolysis, steamy fumes of HCl: SiCl₄ + 2H₂O → SiO₂ + 4HCl. Si is larger, and water's lone pair can bond to it using an empty 3d orbital |
| PbCl₂ | Mainly ionic | White solid | Sparingly soluble in cold water, more soluble in hot; no hydrolysis |

**Lead(II) ion reactions (PI2.1(i)).** Use lead(II) nitrate solution (most lead salts are insoluble).

- With NaOH(aq): white precipitate of Pb(OH)₂: Pb²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Pb(OH)₂. It dissolves in excess to give a colourless solution: Pb(OH)₂ + 2OH⁻ → [Pb(OH)₄]²⁻.
- With Cl⁻ (dilute HCl or NaCl): white precipitate of PbCl₂, which dissolves on heating and reappears on cooling: Pb²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → PbCl₂.
- With I⁻ (KI): bright yellow precipitate of PbI₂: Pb²⁺ + 2I⁻ → PbI₂. It dissolves in hot water and forms shiny golden crystals on cooling.

### Common mistakes

- Saying lead's +4 state is more stable. Down Group 4, +2 is more stable.
- Saying CCl₄ does not hydrolyse because the C–Cl bond is too strong. The key point is that carbon has no low-energy empty orbital for water to bond to and is crowded.
- Drawing Al₂Cl₆ with normal bonds only and missing the two dative bonds.
- Saying hexagonal BN conducts like graphite.

## C1.2 Radioactivity and nuclear changes

*Refs: Eduqas C1.2(a) to (d) · OCR B EL(h). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Types of radioactive decay and the changes in atomic number and mass number: α, β⁻, β⁺ (positron emission), electron capture, γ.
- Behaviour of α, β and γ in electric and magnetic fields; penetrating power.
- Half-life, and simple calculations.
- Harmful effects of radiation and uses of radioisotopes.
- Fusion: light nuclei join to form heavier nuclei at very high temperature and pressure; this formed the elements in stars.

### Explained

**Types of decay.**

| Decay | Particle | Change in nucleus | Change in A | Change in Z |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alpha (α) | Helium nucleus, ⁴₂He | Loses 2 protons and 2 neutrons | −4 | −2 |
| Beta minus (β⁻) | Electron from the nucleus | A neutron becomes a proton | 0 | +1 |
| Positron (β⁺) | Positron (e⁺) | A proton becomes a neutron | 0 | −1 |
| Electron capture | Inner electron captured | A proton becomes a neutron | 0 | −1 |
| Gamma (γ) | High-energy electromagnetic radiation | No change in particles; nucleus loses energy | 0 | 0 |

Examples:

- ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He
- ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e
- ¹¹₆C → ¹¹₅B + ⁰₊₁e
- ⁴⁰₁₉K + ⁰₋₁e → ⁴⁰₁₈Ar (electron capture)

In nuclear equations both mass numbers and atomic numbers balance.

**Properties.**

| Radiation | Charge | Penetration | Stopped by | In electric field |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| α | +2 | Low; most ionising | Paper, a few cm of air | Deflected slightly towards negative plate (heavy) |
| β⁻ | −1 | Medium | A few mm of aluminium | Deflected strongly towards positive plate (light) |
| γ | 0 | High; least ionising | Thick lead or concrete reduces it | Not deflected |

In a magnetic field, α and β bend in opposite directions; β bends more because it is much lighter.

**Half-life** is the time for half the nuclei in a sample to decay (or for the activity to halve). It is constant for an isotope. After n half-lives, the fraction left is (½)ⁿ.

**Harm.** Ionising radiation damages DNA in cells, which can kill cells or cause mutations leading to cancer. α is most dangerous inside the body (swallowed or breathed in); γ and β are dangerous from outside.

**Uses of radioisotopes.**

- Medicine: technetium-99m (γ, short half-life) as a tracer for imaging; iodine-131 to treat thyroid cancer; cobalt-60 γ rays to kill cancer cells and sterilise equipment.
- Dating: carbon-14 (half-life about 5730 years) to date once-living material; potassium-40/argon-40 to date rocks.
- Industry: thickness gauges (β for paper), finding leaks in pipes (γ tracers), smoke alarms (americium-241, α).
- Analysis: radioactive tracers follow the path of atoms in reactions.

**Fusion (OCR B EL(h)).** Under the extreme temperatures and pressures in stars, light nuclei fuse to form heavier ones, releasing energy. Hydrogen fuses to helium: ²₁H + ²₁H → ³₂He + ¹₀n, and overall four protons become one helium-4 nucleus. In older stars helium fuses to carbon (3 ⁴₂He → ¹²₆C) and then oxygen and heavier elements up to iron. Elements heavier than iron form in supernova explosions. High temperature is needed so the positive nuclei have enough energy to overcome their repulsion.

### Worked example

A sample of a radioisotope with a half-life of 8 days has an activity of 640 Bq. What will its activity be after 32 days?

32 ÷ 8 = 4 half-lives. 640 × (½)⁴ = 40 Bq.

### Common mistakes

- Forgetting that in β⁻ decay the mass number does not change.
- Saying γ radiation changes the element.
- Saying half-life is half the time for all the nuclei to decay.

## C1.2 Atomic spectra and the electromagnetic spectrum

*Refs: Eduqas C1.2(h) to (l) · OCR B EL(v), EL(w), OZ(t), OZ(u). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The order of infrared, visible and ultraviolet radiation by frequency, energy and wavelength.
- E = hf (or E = hν) and c = fλ.
- Emission spectra arise when excited electrons fall to lower energy levels and emit photons; absorption spectra when electrons absorb photons and move up.
- The hydrogen emission spectrum: series of lines converging at higher frequency; the Lyman series (transitions to n = 1).
- The convergence limit of the Lyman series gives the ionisation energy of hydrogen.
- UV and visible radiation can promote electrons and sometimes break bonds.

### Explained

**The electromagnetic spectrum.** In order of increasing frequency and energy (decreasing wavelength): radio, microwave, infrared, visible (red to violet), ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma.

E = hf, where h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s. c = fλ, where c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. So E = hc/λ. Per mole, multiply by L (6.02 × 10²³).

**Energy levels.** Electrons in an atom can only have certain energies (quantised levels, n = 1, 2, 3 …). The levels get closer together as n increases.

- **Emission spectrum:** when atoms are heated or given electrical energy, electrons are excited to higher levels. When they drop back, each transition emits a photon with energy equal to the gap: ΔE = hf. This gives coloured lines on a black background.
- **Absorption spectrum:** when white light passes through a cool gas, electrons absorb photons of exactly the energies of the gaps. This gives black lines on a continuous coloured background.

Similarities: both are line spectra, with lines at the same frequencies for a given element, converging at higher frequency. Differences: bright lines on dark for emission; dark lines on bright for absorption.

Each element has its own pattern of lines, used to identify elements (for example in stars: the Sun's spectrum showed helium before it was found on Earth).

**The hydrogen spectrum.** Lines come in series, each ending on one level:

- Lyman series: electrons fall to n = 1; ultraviolet.
- Balmer series: electrons fall to n = 2; visible.

Within each series, lines get closer together at higher frequency because the energy levels get closer at high n.

**Convergence limit and ionisation energy.** The lines in the Lyman series converge to a limit. This corresponds to an electron falling from n = ∞ (outside the atom) to n = 1. The reverse, from n = 1 to n = ∞, is ionisation. So the frequency of the convergence limit gives the ionisation energy of hydrogen: IE = hf × L.

**Radiation and bonds (OCR B OZ(t)).** Visible and UV photons have enough energy to promote electrons to higher levels in molecules. UV photons can have enough energy to break covalent bonds (photodissociation), as when UV breaks Cl–Cl, O=O or the C–Cl bond in CFCs (files 18, 19, 35).

### Worked example

1. The convergence limit of the Lyman series is at a frequency of 3.29 × 10¹⁵ Hz. Calculate the ionisation energy of hydrogen in kJ mol⁻¹.

   E per atom = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.29 × 10¹⁵ = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J. Per mole: × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.31 × 10⁶ J mol⁻¹ = 1310 kJ mol⁻¹.

2. The bond enthalpy of Cl–Cl is 243 kJ mol⁻¹. Find the longest wavelength of light that can break the bond.

   E per bond = 243 000 ÷ 6.02 × 10²³ = 4.04 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. λ = hc/E = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸) ÷ 4.04 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 4.92 × 10⁻⁷ m = 492 nm (visible light).

### Common mistakes

- Saying lines are produced when electrons move up (for emission).
- Mixing up which series is UV and which is visible.
- Forgetting to multiply by the Avogadro constant to get a molar value.
- Using the wavelength in nm without converting to m.

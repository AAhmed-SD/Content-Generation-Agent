# Amino acids, proteins, enzymes, DNA and anticancer drugs

Living things are built from condensation polymers. This file covers amino acids and zwitterions, peptides and protein structure, enzymes and inhibitors, molecular recognition in drug action, the structure of DNA, and how cisplatin stops cancer cells dividing.

A-level only. Optical isomerism (amino acids other than glycine are chiral) is in file 23. Condensation polymerisation is in file 27. Chromatography of amino acids is in file 31. Cisplatin as a complex is in file 15.

## 3.3.13.1 Amino acids

*Refs: AQA 3.3.13.1 · Edexcel 18.16 · OCR A 6.2.2(a) · OCR B PL(a), PL(i) · Eduqas OA3.2(a), (b). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The general structure of α-amino acids: RCH(NH₂)COOH.
- Amino acids are both acidic and basic (amphoteric) and form zwitterions.
- Structures at different pH values.
- Most are chiral (glycine, R = H, is not).
- Reactions: the –COOH with bases and alcohols, the –NH₂ with acids (OCR A).

### Explained

In an α-amino acid, the NH₂ and COOH are on the same carbon. Glycine: H₂NCH₂COOH. Alanine: H₂NCH(CH₃)COOH. Systematic name of alanine: 2-aminopropanoic acid.

**Zwitterions.** The –COOH donates H⁺ to the –NH₂ in the same molecule: ⁺H₃N–CHR–COO⁻. This happens at the isoelectric point, the pH at which the amino acid has no overall charge. Strong ionic attractions between zwitterions give amino acids high melting points and make them soluble in water.

| Condition | Form |
| --- | --- |
| Acidic (low pH) | ⁺H₃N–CHR–COOH (the COO⁻ is protonated) |
| Isoelectric point | ⁺H₃N–CHR–COO⁻ |
| Alkaline (high pH) | H₂N–CHR–COO⁻ (the NH₃⁺ loses H⁺) |

**Reactions (OCR A 6.2.2(a)).**

- With NaOH: H₂NCH(R)COOH + NaOH → H₂NCH(R)COONa + H₂O.
- With an alcohol (acid catalyst): forms an ester, H₂NCH(R)COOCH₃ (the amine is protonated in the acid).
- With HCl: HOOCCH(R)NH₃⁺Cl⁻.

**Optical activity (Edexcel).** Naturally occurring amino acids are single enantiomers (L-amino acids).

### Common mistakes

- Drawing the zwitterion with NH₂ and COO⁻ (no positive charge).
- Getting the low-pH and high-pH forms backwards.

## 3.3.13.2 Proteins

*Refs: AQA 3.3.13.2 · Edexcel 18.17 · OCR B PL(a), PL(b) · Eduqas OA3.2(c) to (e). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Proteins are polypeptides formed by condensation of amino acids; the peptide (amide) link.
- Draw dipeptides and tripeptides.
- Hydrolysis of proteins by hot concentrated hydrochloric acid gives amino acids (protonated).
- Primary, secondary and tertiary structure and the bonds involved (hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges).

### Explained

**Peptide link.** The –COOH of one amino acid reacts with the –NH₂ of another, losing water: –CO–NH–. Two different amino acids give two possible dipeptides (Gly–Ala and Ala–Gly), because either can provide the –COOH.

**Hydrolysis.** Boil with 6 mol dm⁻³ HCl for several hours. The peptide bonds break, giving a mixture of amino acids as their ammonium salts. The amino acids can then be separated and identified by chromatography (file 31).

**Structure.**

| Level | What it is | Bonds that hold it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Sequence of amino acids | Peptide bonds (covalent) |
| Secondary | α-helix or β-pleated sheet | Hydrogen bonds between C=O and N–H of the backbone |
| Tertiary | Overall 3D folding | Interactions between R groups: hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds (–COO⁻ and –NH₃⁺), disulfide bridges (–S–S– between cysteines), and London forces |

Heat or extremes of pH break the weaker interactions and change the shape (denaturation).

### Common mistakes

- Saying secondary structure involves R groups.
- Forgetting the second dipeptide when two different amino acids join.

## 3.3.13.3 Enzymes

*Refs: AQA 3.3.13.3 · OCR B PL(e) to (g) · Eduqas OA3.2(f). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Enzymes are proteins that catalyse reactions. The active site is stereospecific.
- Inhibitors with a similar shape block the active site; drugs can act as enzyme inhibitors; computer modelling helps design them.
- OCR B: the rate–substrate concentration curve; specificity, temperature, pH and competitive inhibition; molecular recognition and pharmacophores.

### Explained

The active site has a specific 3D shape formed by the tertiary structure. A substrate with a complementary shape binds by intermolecular forces (hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, London forces) to form an enzyme–substrate complex, the reaction happens with lower activation energy, and the products leave.

**Stereospecificity.** Because the active site is chiral, an enzyme usually acts on only one enantiomer.

**Rate and substrate concentration (OCR B PL(f)).** At low substrate concentration, rate is proportional to concentration (first order in substrate): more substrate, more collisions with free active sites. At high concentration, all active sites are occupied, so rate stays constant (zero order in substrate).

**Temperature and pH (OCR B PL(g)).** Rate rises with temperature up to an optimum; above it, the tertiary structure is disrupted (denatured) and the active site changes shape. Changes in pH alter the charges on R groups (–COO⁻, –NH₃⁺), breaking ionic and hydrogen bonds.

**Competitive inhibition.** An inhibitor with a similar shape to the substrate binds to the active site, blocking it. Many drugs work like this. Computer modelling of the active site lets chemists design molecules that fit it.

**Molecular recognition and pharmacophores (OCR B PL(e)).** The pharmacophore is the part of a drug molecule that binds to the receptor and causes the effect. Other groups modify how well it dissolves, how well it is absorbed, or how strongly it binds. Binding depends on size, shape, the types of bond formed (hydrogen bonds, ionic attractions, London forces) and orientation in three dimensions.

### Common mistakes

- Saying enzymes are "killed" by heat. They are denatured.
- Saying an inhibitor reacts with the substrate.

## 3.3.13.4 DNA

*Refs: AQA 3.3.13.4 · OCR B PL(c), PL(d). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- DNA is a condensation polymer of nucleotides. Each nucleotide is a phosphate, the sugar 2-deoxyribose and a base: adenine, cytosine, guanine or thymine (RNA uses ribose and uracil).
- The sugar–phosphate backbone; two strands form a double helix held by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases: A with T (two hydrogen bonds), C with G (three).
- OCR B: base pairing and replication; how DNA codes for RNA, which codes for the amino acid sequence.

### Explained

**Nucleotide.** The phosphate joins C5 of the sugar; the base joins C1. Nucleotides join by condensation between the phosphate of one and the C3 –OH of the next sugar, forming the sugar–phosphate backbone (phosphodiester links).

**Base pairing.** The two strands run in opposite directions. Bases point inwards and pair by hydrogen bonds: A–T (two) and C–G (three). The shapes and the positions of the N–H and C=O groups mean only these pairs fit.

**Replication (OCR B).** The hydrogen bonds are weak enough to break when the strands separate. Each strand acts as a template: free nucleotides pair with complementary bases, so two identical copies form.

**From DNA to protein (OCR B).** A sequence of three bases (a triplet, or codon) codes for one amino acid. DNA is copied into messenger RNA, and the RNA sequence is read to assemble the amino acids in order.

### Common mistakes

- Pairing A with G.
- Saying the strands are held by covalent bonds.

## 3.3.13.5 Action of anticancer drugs

*Refs: AQA 3.3.13.5. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Cisplatin binds to DNA and prevents replication.
- The balance between benefits and side effects.

### Explained

Cisplatin, cis-[Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂], enters the cell. A Cl⁻ ligand is replaced by water, and then the platinum forms a coordinate bond to a nitrogen atom on a guanine base. The second ligand is replaced by a bond to a nitrogen on a neighbouring guanine. This kinks the DNA so it cannot be copied, and the cell cannot divide (the cell then dies).

Side effects: cisplatin also affects healthy cells that divide rapidly (hair, blood, gut lining), causing hair loss, nausea and kidney damage. Doctors balance the benefit of killing cancer cells against these effects, using low doses and other drugs alongside.

### Common mistakes

- Saying cisplatin binds through its chloride ligands. The chlorides are replaced; Pt bonds to guanine N atoms.

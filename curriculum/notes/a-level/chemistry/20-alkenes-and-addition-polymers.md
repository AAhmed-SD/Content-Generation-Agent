# Alkenes, electrophilic addition and addition polymers

The C=C double bond makes alkenes far more reactive than alkanes. This file covers the σ and π bonds in alkenes, electrophilic addition of bromine, hydrogen halides and sulfuric acid, Markovnikov's rule and carbocation stability, other addition reactions, the bromine water test, addition polymers, and dealing with waste polymers.

## 3.3.4.1 Structure, bonding and reactivity

*Refs: AQA 3.3.4.1 · Edexcel 6.18 to 6.21 · OCR A 4.1.3(a), (b), (e), (g) · OCR B DF(q) · Eduqas C3.2(d), (e). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons, CₙH₂ₙ, with a C=C bond made of one σ and one π bond.
- The C=C is a region of high electron density, so alkenes are attacked by electrophiles.
- The π bond has a lower bond enthalpy than the σ bond, so it breaks more readily.

### Explained

Each carbon in C=C uses three orbitals for σ bonds (trigonal planar, 120°). The remaining p orbitals overlap sideways above and below the plane to form the π bond. The π electrons are further from the nuclei and exposed, so they attract electrophiles. The π bond also prevents rotation (E/Z isomerism).

C=C bond enthalpy (612 kJ mol⁻¹) is less than twice C–C (348), so the π bond is weaker than the σ bond.

An **electrophile** is an electron pair acceptor: H⁺, Br⁺ (from polarised Br₂), NO₂⁺.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the C=C bond is weaker than C–C. The whole double bond is stronger; the π part is weaker than the σ part.

## 3.3.4.2 Addition reactions of alkenes

*Refs: AQA 3.3.4.2 · Edexcel 6.22 to 6.25 · OCR A 4.1.3(f), (h), (i) · OCR B DF(o), DF(q) · Eduqas C3.2(g) to (j). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Electrophilic addition of HBr, Br₂ and concentrated H₂SO₄; mechanisms.
- Bromine water test for C=C.
- Markovnikov's rule and carbocation stability (primary < secondary < tertiary).
- Hydration with steam (H₃PO₄ catalyst) and hydrogenation (Ni catalyst).
- Edexcel, Eduqas: oxidation by acidified KMnO₄ to a diol (and as a test).

### Explained

**Bromine.** CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br (1,2-dibromoethane).

Mechanism:

1. As Br₂ approaches the C=C, the π electrons repel the electrons in Br–Br, inducing a dipole: Brᵟ⁺–Brᵟ⁻.
2. Curly arrow from the C=C to the δ+ Br; curly arrow from the Br–Br bond to the δ− Br.
3. A carbocation forms (CH₂Br–CH₂⁺) and a Br⁻ ion.
4. Curly arrow from a lone pair on Br⁻ to the positive carbon.

**Test for C=C.** Shake with bromine water: orange to colourless. (In bromine water, some bromohydrin CH₂BrCH₂OH also forms.) Alkanes do not decolorise it without UV.

**Hydrogen bromide.** CH₂=CH₂ + HBr → CH₃CH₂Br. H–Br is permanently polar: H is δ+. Arrow from C=C to H; arrow from H–Br bond to Br; carbocation forms; Br⁻ lone pair attacks the carbocation.

**Unsymmetrical alkenes: Markovnikov's rule.** With propene and HBr, two carbocations could form:

- CH₃CH⁺CH₃ (secondary): more stable.
- CH₃CH₂CH₂⁺ (primary): less stable.

Alkyl groups push electron density towards the positive carbon (positive inductive effect), spreading the charge and stabilising the ion. More alkyl groups, more stable: tertiary > secondary > primary. The more stable carbocation forms faster, so the major product is 2-bromopropane and the minor product 1-bromopropane. In words: the H adds to the carbon that already has more hydrogens.

**Concentrated sulfuric acid.** CH₂=CH₂ + H₂SO₄ → CH₃CH₂OSO₂OH (ethyl hydrogensulfate). Same mechanism with H⁺ from H–O. Adding water then gives ethanol and regenerates the acid: CH₃CH₂OSO₂OH + H₂O → CH₃CH₂OH + H₂SO₄. Sulfuric acid is a catalyst overall.

**Steam.** CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃CH₂OH. Conditions: 300 °C, 60 to 70 atm, phosphoric acid catalyst (file 21).

**Hydrogen.** CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃CH₃. Nickel catalyst, about 150 °C (Eduqas C3.2(j)). Used to harden vegetable oils into margarine.

**Other halogens.** Cl₂ adds in the same way to give dichloroalkanes; I₂ adds slowly.

**Acidified potassium manganate(VII) (Edexcel, Eduqas).** Shaken with cold dilute acidified KMnO₄, alkenes are oxidised to diols: CH₂=CH₂ + [O] + H₂O → HOCH₂CH₂OH (ethane-1,2-diol). The purple solution goes colourless. This is another test for C=C.

### Worked example

Predict the major product of 2-methylpropene with HBr and explain.

The H adds to the CH₂ end, giving the tertiary carbocation (CH₃)₂C⁺CH₃, which is more stable than the primary one. Major product: 2-bromo-2-methylpropane.

### Common mistakes

- Drawing the first arrow from the Br to the C=C. It goes from the C=C to the electrophile.
- Forgetting the δ charges on Br₂ or HBr.
- Saying the major product forms because the minor carbocation "cannot form". Both form; the more stable one forms faster.

## 3.3.4.3 Addition polymers

*Refs: AQA 3.3.4.3 · Edexcel 6.26 to 6.29 · OCR A 4.1.3(j) to (l) · OCR B DF(p) · Eduqas C3.2(k). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Addition polymers form from alkenes and substituted alkenes.
- Draw repeat units from monomers and identify monomers from polymers.
- Properties of polymers depend on chain length and the forces between chains.
- Poly(chloroethene) (PVC) and the use of plasticisers.
- Addition polymers are unreactive and non-biodegradable.
- Disposal and recycling of polymers.

### Explained

n CH₂=CHCl → –[CH₂–CHCl]ₙ–

To draw a repeat unit: change C=C to C–C, keep the substituents on the same carbons, add bonds sticking out each side, put brackets and n.

| Monomer | Polymer | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ethene | Poly(ethene) | Bags, bottles |
| Propene | Poly(propene) | Crates, ropes |
| Chloroethene | Poly(chloroethene), PVC | Window frames, pipes, cable insulation |
| Tetrafluoroethene | PTFE | Non-stick coatings |
| Phenylethene | Poly(phenylethene), polystyrene | Packaging, insulation |

**Properties.** Long chains with more contact have stronger London forces, so higher melting points and strength. PVC has polar C–Cl bonds, so permanent dipole–dipole forces between chains: rigid PVC is hard. Adding a **plasticiser** (small molecules that fit between the chains) pushes the chains apart, so they slide more easily: flexible PVC for cable insulation and clothing.

**Unreactive.** Saturated C–C and C–H backbones with non-polar bonds, so most addition polymers are non-biodegradable and last for centuries in landfill.

**Dealing with waste polymers.**

| Method | Benefits | Drawbacks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Landfill | Cheap | Uses land; persists for centuries |
| Recycling (sorting by type, melting, remoulding) | Saves crude oil and energy; reduces landfill | Polymers must be sorted (by density or infrared), quality can fall |
| Incineration for energy | Energy used to make steam and electricity | Releases CO₂; PVC releases HCl (removed by scrubbers with a base), and possibly dioxins |
| Feedstock recycling (cracking back to monomers or small molecules) | Products used to make new polymers | Energy-intensive |
| Biodegradable and photodegradable polymers (OCR A 4.1.3(l)) | Break down naturally (from starch, or with light) | May need special composting conditions; made from crops that need land |

**Life cycle (Edexcel 6.28).** Judging a polymer's environmental impact means looking at energy and resources used in extraction, manufacture, use and disposal.

### Common mistakes

- Including the C=C in the repeat unit.
- Putting substituents on the wrong carbon, or leaving out the side bonds.
- Saying addition polymers are biodegradable because they "contain carbon".

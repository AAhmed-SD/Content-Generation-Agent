# Alcohols: production, oxidation and elimination

Alcohols contain the –OH group. This file covers their properties and classification, making ethanol by hydration and by fermentation, combustion, oxidation with acidified dichromate, dehydration to alkenes, substitution to halogenoalkanes, other reactions, and the practical techniques of reflux, distillation and purifying a liquid. It includes AQA required practical 5.

## 3.3.5.1 Alcohol production

*Refs: AQA 3.3.5.1 · OCR A 4.2.1(a) · OCR B WM(b) · Eduqas C3.4(a), (b), (d), OA2.1(a). Level: AS & A (Eduqas OA2.1 A-level).*

### What you need to know

- Alcohols are primary, secondary or tertiary depending on how many carbons are attached to the carbon bearing the –OH.
- Ethanol is made industrially by the hydration of ethene with steam (phosphoric acid catalyst) and by fermentation of glucose.
- Advantages and disadvantages of each method; biofuels and carbon neutrality.
- Alcohols are polar and form hydrogen bonds, so small alcohols are soluble in water and have relatively high boiling points.

### Explained

**Classification.** Primary: CH₃CH₂OH (the C–OH carbon has one carbon attached). Secondary: CH₃CH(OH)CH₃. Tertiary: (CH₃)₃COH.

**Hydration of ethene.** CH₂=CH₂(g) + H₂O(g) ⇌ CH₃CH₂OH(g). Conditions: 300 °C, 6 to 7 MPa, phosphoric acid on silica. Mechanism is electrophilic addition; H⁺ from the acid catalyst adds first:

1. Arrow from C=C to H⁺ (from H₃PO₄ / H₃O⁺): carbocation forms.
2. Lone pair on a water molecule attacks the carbocation.
3. The oxonium ion (CH₃CH₂OH₂⁺) loses H⁺, regenerating the catalyst.

**Fermentation.** C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) → 2C₂H₅OH(aq) + 2CO₂(g). Conditions: yeast enzymes, 30 to 40 °C (too cold is slow; too hot denatures the enzymes), anaerobic (with air, the ethanol would be oxidised to ethanoic acid). Stops at about 15% ethanol when the yeast dies; the ethanol is then separated by fractional distillation.

| | Hydration of ethene | Fermentation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raw material | Crude oil (finite) | Sugar from crops (renewable) |
| Process | Continuous | Batch |
| Rate | Fast | Slow |
| Purity | Pure ethanol | Dilute; needs distilling |
| Atom economy | 100% | 51% (CO₂ waste) |
| Energy cost | High temperature and pressure | Low temperature, but distillation uses energy |

**Carbon neutrality of bioethanol (AQA).** Plants take in 6CO₂ in photosynthesis to make one glucose. Fermentation releases 2CO₂ and burning the two ethanols releases 4CO₂: total 6. So in theory it is carbon neutral. In practice, energy for farming machinery, fertilisers, transport and distillation usually comes from fossil fuels, so overall CO₂ is released.

**Other routes (Eduqas OA2.1(a)).** Halogenoalkane + aqueous NaOH under reflux gives an alcohol (file 19). Reduction of aldehydes by NaBH₄ gives primary alcohols and of ketones gives secondary alcohols (file 23).

**Properties (OCR A 4.2.1(a)).** The O–H bond is polar. Alcohols form hydrogen bonds with each other (high boiling points compared with alkanes of similar Mᵣ) and with water (small alcohols mix completely with water). As the chain gets longer, the non-polar part dominates and solubility falls.

### Common mistakes

- Saying fermentation is carbon neutral without qualification.
- Using phosphoric acid for fermentation or yeast for hydration.
- Classifying an alcohol by the number of H atoms rather than C atoms.

## 3.3.5.2 Oxidation of alcohols

*Refs: AQA 3.3.5.2 · Edexcel 6.38, CP5 · OCR A 4.2.1(b), (c) · OCR B WM(d) · Eduqas C3.4(e), (f). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Primary alcohols are oxidised to aldehydes, then to carboxylic acids. Secondary alcohols are oxidised to ketones. Tertiary alcohols are not oxidised.
- Oxidising agent: potassium dichromate(VI) acidified with dilute sulfuric acid; orange Cr₂O₇²⁻ turns green Cr³⁺.
- Distil to collect the aldehyde; reflux with excess oxidant for the carboxylic acid.
- Combustion of alcohols (OCR A, Edexcel).

### Explained

Use [O] for the oxidising agent in equations.

- Primary to aldehyde: CH₃CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃CHO + H₂O.
- Aldehyde to acid: CH₃CHO + [O] → CH₃COOH.
- Primary to acid in one: CH₃CH₂OH + 2[O] → CH₃COOH + H₂O.
- Secondary to ketone: CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ + [O] → CH₃COCH₃ + H₂O.

**Why tertiary alcohols are not oxidised.** Oxidation removes the H from the O–H and an H from the carbon bearing the OH. A tertiary alcohol has no H on that carbon.

**Getting the aldehyde.** Use excess alcohol and heat gently with the dichromate, distilling off the product as it forms. The aldehyde has the lowest boiling point in the mixture (no hydrogen bonding between its molecules), so it leaves before it can be oxidised further.

**Getting the acid.** Use excess oxidising agent and heat under reflux, so any aldehyde vapour condenses back and is oxidised to the acid. Then distil.

**Combustion.** C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O. Alcohols burn with a clean blue flame and are used as fuels.

### Practical

**Edexcel CP5: the oxidation of ethanol.** Add acidified potassium dichromate dropwise to ethanol with cooling (exothermic), then (a) set up for distillation to collect ethanal, or (b) reflux then distil to collect ethanoic acid. Test the distillate: ethanal gives a red precipitate with Fehling's or Benedict's solution, or a silver mirror with Tollens' (file 22); ethanoic acid fizzes with sodium carbonate.

### Common mistakes

- Refluxing when the aldehyde is wanted.
- Writing the colour change backwards. Orange to green.
- Using [O] as O₂.

## 3.3.5.3 Elimination

*Refs: AQA 3.3.5.3, RP5 · Edexcel 6.38, 6.39, CP6 · OCR A 4.2.1(d), (e), 4.2.3(a) · OCR B WM(h) · Eduqas C3.4(c), (i), OA2.1(b). Level: AS & A (Eduqas OA2.1 A-level).*

### What you need to know

- Alcohols are dehydrated to alkenes by heating with concentrated phosphoric or sulfuric acid (or passing the vapour over hot Al₂O₃). Mechanism (AQA).
- Alkenes made from renewable ethanol can make polymers without crude oil.
- Substitution of –OH by halogen (OCR A, Edexcel, Eduqas).
- Reaction with sodium (Edexcel).
- Techniques: reflux, distillation, separating funnel, drying, boiling point.

### Explained

**Dehydration.** CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O. Conditions: excess concentrated H₃PO₄ (or H₂SO₄) at about 170 °C, or vapour over hot Al₂O₃.

Mechanism (acid-catalysed elimination):

1. A lone pair on the O of the –OH bonds to H⁺: the alcohol is protonated.
2. Water leaves, taking the C–O bond electrons, to give a carbocation.
3. A C–H bond on the neighbouring carbon breaks; its electrons form the C=C; H⁺ is lost (regenerating the catalyst).

Secondary alcohols can give more than one alkene: butan-2-ol gives but-1-ene, E-but-2-ene and Z-but-2-ene.

This route makes ethene from bioethanol, so poly(ethene) can be made without crude oil.

**Substitution to halogenoalkanes.**

| Reagent | Product | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HCl (concentrated), shaken | Chloroalkane | Works well for tertiary alcohols (Edexcel CP6) |
| KBr + concentrated (or 50%) H₂SO₄ | Bromoalkane | HBr made in situ |
| PCl₅ | Chloroalkane | Steamy fumes of HCl: ROH + PCl₅ → RCl + POCl₃ + HCl. Also a test for –OH. |
| Red phosphorus + iodine | Iodoalkane | PI₃ made in situ |

**With sodium (Edexcel).** 2CH₃CH₂OH + 2Na → 2CH₃CH₂O⁻Na⁺ + H₂. Fizzing, less vigorous than with water. Sodium ethoxide forms.

**With ethanoyl chloride and carboxylic acids (Eduqas OA2.1(b)).** Esters form (file 24).

**Purifying a liquid organic product (Edexcel 6.39, OCR A 4.2.3(a)).**

1. **Reflux** to complete the reaction (file 19).
2. **Distil** to separate the crude product from the mixture: heat in a flask with a still head, thermometer at the side arm and a condenser sloping down to a collecting flask; collect the fraction at the product's boiling point.
3. **Separating funnel** to wash: shake with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution to remove acid (release pressure often, CO₂ forms), then with water. Run off and keep the organic layer (check densities: the layer with the lower density is on top).
4. **Dry** with an anhydrous salt (anhydrous MgSO₄, CaCl₂ or Na₂SO₄) until the liquid is clear and the drying agent no longer clumps; decant or filter.
5. **Redistil**, collecting only within a narrow range around the boiling point.
6. **Boiling point** close to the data value, over a narrow range, shows purity.

### Practical

**AQA RP5: distillation of a product from a reaction.** For example making cyclohexene from cyclohexanol and concentrated phosphoric acid: heat and distil off the cyclohexene (b.p. 83 °C), wash in a separating funnel, dry with anhydrous CaCl₂, redistil. Or distil ethanal from the oxidation of ethanol. Anti-bumping granules give smooth boiling; heat with a heating mantle or water bath because the products are flammable; do not seal the apparatus.

**Edexcel CP6: chlorination of 2-methylpropan-2-ol.** Shake the alcohol with concentrated hydrochloric acid in a separating funnel, releasing pressure. Separate the upper organic layer, wash with sodium hydrogencarbonate, dry with anhydrous sodium sulfate, and distil, collecting 2-chloro-2-methylpropane at about 50 °C.

**Eduqas: preparing an ester and separating by distillation** (file 24 for the reaction).

### Common mistakes

- Sealing the apparatus during distillation or reflux.
- Putting the thermometer bulb in the liquid when distilling. It should be at the side arm, measuring vapour temperature.
- Keeping the wrong layer in a separating funnel.
- Saying the drying agent is "dehydrating" the product chemically. It absorbs water.

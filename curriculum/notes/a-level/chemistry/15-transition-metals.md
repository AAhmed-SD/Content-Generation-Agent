# Transition metals: complexes, colour, oxidation states and catalysts

The transition metals Ti to Cu form complexes, coloured ions, several oxidation states and good catalysts, all because of their partly filled d sub-shell. This file covers complexes and their shapes, ligand substitution and the chelate effect, the origin of colour, variable oxidation states with vanadium, chromium, cobalt and manganese, redox titrations, and catalysis.

## Overview

A-level only. Precipitation reactions of aqua ions with hydroxide, ammonia and carbonate are in file 16. You need electron configurations (file 1), redox (file 7) and electrode potentials (file 10).

## 3.2.5.1 General properties of transition metals

*Refs: AQA 3.2.5.1 · Edexcel 15.1 to 15.3, 15.6 · OCR A 5.3.1(a) to (c) · OCR B DM(g), DM(h) · Eduqas PI2.2(a), (b). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Transition metal characteristics come from an incomplete d sub-shell in the atom or ions.
- The characteristics: complex formation, coloured ions, variable oxidation state, catalytic activity.
- A complex is a central metal atom or ion surrounded by ligands. A ligand is a molecule or ion that forms a coordinate bond with a transition metal by donating a pair of electrons. Coordination number is the number of coordinate bonds to the central metal.

### Explained

**Definition.** A transition element is a d-block element that forms at least one stable ion with an incomplete d sub-shell. Scandium (only Sc³⁺, [Ar] 3d⁰) and zinc (only Zn²⁺, [Ar] 3d¹⁰) are in the d block but not transition elements.

Configurations: remove 4s electrons first. Fe [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s²; Fe²⁺ [Ar] 3d⁶; Fe³⁺ [Ar] 3d⁵. Cr [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹; Cu [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹; Cu²⁺ [Ar] 3d⁹.

**Why variable oxidation states?** The 4s and 3d sub-shells are close in energy, so different numbers of electrons can be lost (or shared) for similar energy.

### Common mistakes

- Calling zinc a transition metal.
- Removing 3d electrons before 4s.

## 3.2.5.2 Substitution reactions

*Refs: AQA 3.2.5.2 · Edexcel 15.4, 15.5, 15.12, 15.17 to 15.19, 15.26 to 15.28, CP12 · OCR A 5.3.1(d), (h), (i) · OCR B DM(i), DM(j) · Eduqas PI2.2(e). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- H₂O, NH₃ and Cl⁻ are monodentate ligands. H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂ (en) and C₂O₄²⁻ (ethanedioate) are bidentate. EDTA⁴⁻ is hexadentate (multidentate).
- Ligand substitution with similar-sized ligands (H₂O and NH₃) has no change in coordination number. Substitution by Cl⁻ (larger) changes coordination number from 6 to 4.
- Incomplete substitution: [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ with excess ammonia gives [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺.
- The chelate effect, explained by entropy.
- Haem: an iron(II) complex with a multidentate ligand; O₂ binds to Fe(II); CO binds more strongly and is toxic.

### Explained

**Ligands** have at least one lone pair. Bidentate ligands have two donor atoms and form two coordinate bonds; en bonds through both N atoms, ethanedioate through two O⁻ atoms. EDTA⁴⁻ has six donor atoms (two N and four O⁻).

**Substitution, no change in coordination number.**

[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 6NH₃ → [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺ + 6H₂O (pink to straw-yellow)

[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ + 4H₂O (pale blue to deep blue). This is incomplete substitution: only four waters are replaced.

[Cr(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 6NH₃ → [Cr(NH₃)₆]³⁺ + 6H₂O (in excess concentrated ammonia; purple to violet) (OCR A).

**Substitution with chloride.** Cl⁻ is larger than H₂O, so only four fit around the metal:

[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CuCl₄]²⁻ + 6H₂O (blue to yellow-green to yellow, with concentrated HCl). Octahedral to tetrahedral.

[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CoCl₄]²⁻ + 6H₂O (pink to blue).

**The chelate effect.** Multidentate ligands form more stable complexes than monodentate ones.

[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 3en → [Cu(en)₃]²⁺ + 6H₂O

[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + EDTA⁴⁻ → [Cu(EDTA)]²⁻ + 6H₂O

The bonds broken and made are similar (Cu–O and Cu–N), so ΔH is small. But the number of particles increases (4 → 7 and 2 → 7), so ΔS is large and positive. So ΔG = ΔH − TΔS is negative: the reaction is feasible and the complex is stable. Reversing it would decrease entropy. EDTA is used to remove metal ions (in water softening, in treating metal poisoning).

**Haem and haemoglobin.** Haem is an iron(II) ion held by four nitrogen atoms of a flat multidentate ring (a porphyrin). The fifth position bonds to the protein globin. The sixth position can bind O₂ (coordinate bond to Fe²⁺), carrying oxygen round the body. Carbon monoxide binds to the same site more strongly than O₂ and is not released, so less oxygen is carried: CO is toxic. This is a ligand substitution (Edexcel 15.19).

### Practical

**Edexcel CP12: preparation of a transition metal complex**, for example making a copper(II) ammine complex or a chromium(II) ethanoate complex. Typical steps: dissolve, react, crystallise by adding ethanol or cooling, filter by suction, wash with a little cold solvent, dry, weigh and work out the percentage yield. For chromium(II), oxygen must be kept out because Cr(II) is readily oxidised.

### Common mistakes

- Writing [CuCl₄]²⁻ as octahedral.
- Forgetting the charge on a complex when ligands change (Cl⁻ is negative).
- Saying the chelate effect is due to stronger bonds. It is due to the entropy increase.

## 3.2.5.3 Shapes of complex ions

*Refs: AQA 3.2.5.3 · Edexcel 15.11, 15.13 to 15.16 · OCR A 5.3.1(e) to (g) · OCR B DM(b) · Eduqas PI2.2(c). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Small ligands (H₂O, NH₃) usually give octahedral complexes (coordination number 6). Larger ligands (Cl⁻) give tetrahedral complexes (4).
- Square planar complexes (coordination number 4) such as cisplatin. Linear complexes such as [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ (Tollens' reagent).
- Cis–trans isomerism in square planar and octahedral complexes. Optical isomerism in octahedral complexes with three bidentate ligands.
- Cisplatin as an anticancer drug (also file 28).

### Explained

| Shape | Coordination number | Angle | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Octahedral | 6 | 90° | [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺, [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺ |
| Tetrahedral | 4 | 109.5° | [CuCl₄]²⁻, [CoCl₄]²⁻ |
| Square planar | 4 | 90° | [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂], [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻ |
| Linear | 2 | 180° | [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ |

**Cis–trans isomerism.** In square planar [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂], the two Cl can be next to each other (cis, 90°) or opposite (trans, 180°). In octahedral [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]⁺ the same applies.

**Optical isomerism.** [Ni(en)₃]²⁺ and [Co(en)₃]³⁺ exist as two non-superimposable mirror images, like left and right hands (OCR A 5.3.1(f), Edexcel). Octahedral complexes with two bidentate ligands in cis positions, such as cis-[Co(en)₂Cl₂]⁺, are also optically active.

**Cisplatin.** cis-[Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]. In the cell, the Cl⁻ ligands are replaced by water, and the platinum then bonds to two nitrogen atoms on adjacent guanine bases in DNA. This bends the DNA, stopping it unwinding and replicating, so cancer cells cannot divide. It also affects healthy cells that divide quickly (hair follicles, gut lining), causing side effects. Small doses and targeted delivery reduce these. Only the cis isomer works; in the trans isomer the two binding sites are too far apart to bond to neighbouring bases.

### Common mistakes

- Drawing a square planar complex as tetrahedral.
- Saying both isomers of [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] are used as drugs.

## 3.2.5.4 Formation of coloured ions

*Refs: AQA 3.2.5.4 · Edexcel 15.7 to 15.10 · OCR A 5.3.1(c) · OCR B DM(m), DM(n) · Eduqas PI2.2(d), (f). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Transition metal ions are coloured because d electrons absorb visible light and move to a higher d orbital: ΔE = hν = hc/λ.
- Changes in oxidation state, coordination number and ligand change the colour.
- The colour seen is the complementary colour of the light absorbed.
- Colorimetry (visible spectroscopy) to find concentrations.
- Why Sc³⁺ and Zn²⁺ (and Cu⁺) are colourless.

### Explained

In an isolated ion the five 3d orbitals have the same energy. When ligands bond, they split into two groups with an energy gap ΔE (in an octahedral complex, two higher and three lower). An electron in a lower d orbital can absorb a photon whose energy matches ΔE and jump up. The light that is not absorbed is transmitted or reflected, and that is the colour we see.

[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ absorbs in the red/orange region, so it looks blue.

ΔE = hν, where h is the Planck constant (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s) and ν the frequency. Also ν = c/λ.

The size of ΔE, and so the colour, depends on:

- the metal and its oxidation state ([Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ pale green, [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ pale violet but usually seen yellow-brown due to hydrolysis);
- the ligand ([Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ blue, [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ deep blue);
- the coordination number and shape ([CuCl₄]²⁻ yellow).

**No colour.** Sc³⁺ (3d⁰) has no d electrons to promote. Zn²⁺ and Cu⁺ (3d¹⁰) have a full d sub-shell, so there is no space for an electron to move into.

**Colorimetry.** Choose a filter of the complementary colour (the colour absorbed most). Make standard solutions of known concentration, measure their absorbance and plot a calibration graph. Measure the unknown and read off the concentration. If the ion is pale, add a ligand that gives a more intensely coloured complex (such as thiocyanate with Fe³⁺, or ammonia with Cu²⁺).

### Worked example

A complex absorbs light of wavelength 500 nm. Calculate ΔE for one ion, and per mole.

ν = c/λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ = 6.00 × 10¹⁴ Hz. ΔE = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 6.00 × 10¹⁴ = 3.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Per mole: × 6.02 × 10²³ = 2.40 × 10⁵ J mol⁻¹ = 240 kJ mol⁻¹.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the electrons emit the colour we see. They absorb; we see what is left.
- Saying the electron is promoted from 3d to 4s. It moves between split d orbitals.

## 3.2.5.5 Variable oxidation states

*Refs: AQA 3.2.5.5 · Edexcel 14.18, 14.19, 15.20 to 15.23, CP11 · OCR A 5.2.3(d), (e), 5.3.1(k), (l) · OCR B DM(a), ES(f) · Eduqas PI1.2(c), (d), PI2.2(b). Level: A-level only (OCR B ES(f) AS).*

### What you need to know

- Transition elements show variable oxidation states.
- Vanadium(V) to (II) by zinc in acid, with colours.
- The redox potential of a change depends on pH and ligand.
- Tollens' reagent, [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺, reduced to silver by aldehydes.
- Redox titrations with KMnO₄ (Fe²⁺, ethanedioate) and K₂Cr₂O₇; iodine–thiosulfate titrations; calculations.
- Chromium (Edexcel, OCR A, Eduqas): Cr³⁺ ⇌ Cr₂O₇²⁻ interconversion; Cr³⁺ to Cr²⁺ with zinc; dichromate–chromate equilibrium.
- Iron(II)/iron(III) interconversion (OCR A); cobalt(II) oxidised in ammonia (Eduqas, AQA context).

### Explained

**Vanadium.** Ammonium vanadate(V) in acid contains VO₂⁺ (yellow). Add zinc and acid; zinc is a strong reducing agent.

| Ion | Oxidation state | Colour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| VO₂⁺ | +5 | Yellow |
| VO²⁺ | +4 | Blue |
| V³⁺ | +3 | Green |
| V²⁺ | +2 | Violet |

The mixture goes yellow → green (yellow + blue mix) → blue → green → violet. Half-equations:

- VO₂⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ → VO²⁺ + H₂O (E⦵ +1.00 V)
- VO²⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ → V³⁺ + H₂O (+0.34 V)
- V³⁺ + e⁻ → V²⁺ (−0.26 V)
- Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn (−0.76 V)

Zinc can reduce all three because all three are more positive than −0.76 V. V²⁺ is readily reoxidised by air, so the flask is fitted with a loose cotton wool plug or a valve.

**Effect of pH.** Many oxidations are easier in alkali and many reductions easier in acid. Half-equations with H⁺ shift right in acid (MnO₄⁻ is a stronger oxidising agent in acid). Cr(III) is oxidised to Cr(VI) by hydrogen peroxide in alkaline solution: 2[Cr(OH)₆]³⁻ + 3H₂O₂ → 2CrO₄²⁻ + 2OH⁻ + 8H₂O (green to yellow). In acid, the reverse happens.

**Effect of ligand.** Co²⁺ in water is hard to oxidise, but [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺ is readily oxidised by air to [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺ (straw to darker brown). Changing the ligand changes E.

**Chromium (Edexcel 15.22, 15.23; OCR A 5.3.1(k); Eduqas).**

- Dichromate(VI) in acid is reduced by zinc: Cr₂O₇²⁻ (orange) → Cr³⁺ (green) → Cr²⁺ (blue). Cr²⁺ is easily oxidised back by air.
- Cr³⁺ is oxidised to chromate(VI) by H₂O₂ in alkali (above). Adding acid turns chromate(VI) (yellow) to dichromate(VI) (orange): 2CrO₄²⁻ + 2H⁺ ⇌ Cr₂O₇²⁻ + H₂O. This is not a redox reaction: Cr stays +6.

**Iron (OCR A 5.3.1(k)).** Fe²⁺ (pale green) is oxidised to Fe³⁺ (yellow/orange-brown) by acidified MnO₄⁻, by H₂O₂ or slowly by air. Fe³⁺ is reduced to Fe²⁺ by I⁻ (iodine forms) or by zinc. Test: with NaOH, Fe²⁺ gives a green precipitate, Fe³⁺ an orange-brown one.

**Copper (OCR A, Eduqas).** Cu²⁺ is reduced by iodide: 2Cu²⁺ + 4I⁻ → 2CuI + I₂ (white precipitate of CuI in a brown iodine solution). Cu⁺ in water disproportionates to Cu and Cu²⁺.

**Main oxidation states (Eduqas PI2.2(b)).** Cr: +3, +6. Mn: +2, +4 (MnO₂), +7 (MnO₄⁻). Fe: +2, +3. Co: +2, +3. Cu: +1, +2.

**Tollens' reagent.** [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ is reduced to silver (a mirror) by aldehydes, which are oxidised: Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag. Ketones give no change (file 23).

**Redox titrations.**

*Manganate(VII)* is self-indicating: its purple colour disappears while it reacts; the end point is the first permanent pale pink. It is used in acid (dilute sulfuric acid; not HCl, which MnO₄⁻ would oxidise to Cl₂; not nitric, which is itself an oxidant).

- With iron(II): MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O + 5Fe³⁺ (ratio 1 : 5).
- With ethanedioate: 2MnO₄⁻ + 16H⁺ + 5C₂O₄²⁻ → 2Mn²⁺ + 8H₂O + 10CO₂ (ratio 2 : 5). Done warm (about 60 °C) because it is slow at the start.

*Dichromate(VI)* with iron(II): Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6Fe²⁺ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O + 6Fe³⁺ (ratio 1 : 6). Needs an indicator (such as sodium diphenylaminesulfonate) because the green Cr³⁺ hides the orange.

*Iodine–thiosulfate* (OCR A, OCR B, Eduqas). An oxidising agent reacts with excess iodide to release iodine, which is titrated with sodium thiosulfate: I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻. Add starch near the end point, when the solution is pale straw: it goes blue-black, then colourless at the end point. Adding starch too early can trap iodine.

*Estimating copper (Eduqas).* Neutralise the copper(II) solution, add excess KI, then titrate the iodine released with thiosulfate. 2Cu²⁺ → I₂ → 2S₂O₃²⁻, so n(Cu²⁺) = n(S₂O₃²⁻).

### Worked example

1. A 2.50 g iron tablet is dissolved in acid and made up to 250 cm³. 25.0 cm³ needs 12.40 cm³ of 0.0100 mol dm⁻³ KMnO₄. Find the percentage of iron.

   n(MnO₄⁻) = 1.24 × 10⁻⁴ mol. n(Fe²⁺) = 5 × 1.24 × 10⁻⁴ = 6.20 × 10⁻⁴ in 25.0 cm³; 6.20 × 10⁻³ in 250 cm³. Mass Fe = 6.20 × 10⁻³ × 55.8 = 0.346 g. Percentage = 13.8%.

2. 0.600 g of a brass is dissolved and treated with excess KI. The iodine needs 23.60 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ thiosulfate. Find the percentage of copper.

   n(S₂O₃²⁻) = 2.36 × 10⁻³ = n(Cu). Mass = 2.36 × 10⁻³ × 63.5 = 0.150 g. Percentage = 25.0%.

### Practical

**Edexcel CP11, Eduqas simple redox titration**, for example the iron content of iron tablets with KMnO₄. **Eduqas estimating copper** by iodine–thiosulfate as above. **OCR B DM(n)**: colorimetry to find the concentration of a coloured transition metal ion.

### Common mistakes

- Using the 1 : 1 ratio for MnO₄⁻ and Fe²⁺.
- Adding starch at the start of an iodine–thiosulfate titration.
- Saying the chromate/dichromate change is redox.
- Reading the top of the meniscus for KMnO₄ (it is so dark you read the top, which is correct; reading the bottom is the mistake).

## 3.2.5.6 Catalysts

*Refs: AQA 3.2.5.6 · Edexcel 15.29 to 15.35 · OCR A 5.3.1(c) · OCR B DM(l) · Eduqas PI2.2(g) to (i). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Transition metals and their compounds are good catalysts because they can change oxidation state readily.
- Heterogeneous catalysts: surface, adsorption, support media, poisoning. Examples: V₂O₅ in the Contact process, Fe in the Haber process, Ni in hydrogenation, Pt/Rh in catalytic converters.
- Homogeneous catalysts: intermediate species. Examples: Fe²⁺ in the reaction of I⁻ with S₂O₈²⁻; autocatalysis by Mn²⁺ in MnO₄⁻ with C₂O₄²⁻.

### Explained

**V₂O₅ in the Contact process** (2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃):

- SO₂ + V₂O₅ → SO₃ + V₂O₄ (V goes +5 to +4)
- 2V₂O₄ + O₂ → 2V₂O₅ (back to +5)

The variable oxidation state lets the catalyst take part and be regenerated.

**Iron in the Haber process** and **nickel in hydrogenation** of vegetable oils to margarine (Eduqas PI2.2(h)) are heterogeneous: reactants adsorb on the metal surface.

**Catalytic converters (Edexcel 15.32).** A ceramic honeycomb coated with a thin layer of platinum, palladium and rhodium. 2CO + 2NO → 2CO₂ + N₂. Unburnt hydrocarbons are also oxidised to CO₂ and H₂O. Large surface area for the small amount of expensive metal.

**Poisoning.** Impurities adsorb strongly on the active sites and block them: lead poisoned catalytic converters (so unleaded petrol was introduced); sulfur poisons the iron in the Haber process. Poisoning means the catalyst must be replaced or cleaned, adding cost; purifying the reactants helps.

**Fe²⁺ catalysing peroxodisulfate and iodide.** S₂O₈²⁻ + 2I⁻ → 2SO₄²⁻ + I₂. Without a catalyst this is slow, because two negative ions repel each other. With Fe²⁺:

- S₂O₈²⁻ + 2Fe²⁺ → 2SO₄²⁻ + 2Fe³⁺
- 2Fe³⁺ + 2I⁻ → 2Fe²⁺ + I₂

Each step involves oppositely charged ions, so the activation energy is lower. Fe³⁺ also works, because the steps simply happen in the other order.

**Autocatalysis by Mn²⁺.** In the MnO₄⁻/C₂O₄²⁻ titration, the reaction starts slowly (two negative ions). Mn²⁺ is a product and catalyses the reaction:

- 4Mn²⁺ + MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ → 5Mn³⁺ + 4H₂O
- 2Mn³⁺ + C₂O₄²⁻ → 2Mn²⁺ + 2CO₂

So the rate increases as Mn²⁺ builds up, then falls as the reactants run out: an S-shaped concentration–time curve for the product.

### Common mistakes

- Saying a catalyst is "not involved" in the reaction.
- Saying the Fe²⁺ catalysis works because Fe²⁺ "attracts" both ions; explain with the two steps between oppositely charged ions.
- Forgetting that both Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ can catalyse this reaction.

# Optical isomerism, aldehydes and ketones

This file covers chirality and optical isomers, then the carbonyl compounds: how aldehydes and ketones are made, how they are told apart, their reduction by NaBH₄, nucleophilic addition of HCN (which links back to optical isomerism), and the 2,4-DNPH and iodoform tests.

## 3.3.7 Optical isomerism

*Refs: AQA 3.3.7 · Edexcel 17.1 to 17.5 · OCR A 6.2.2(c), (d) · OCR B PL(q) · Eduqas OA1.1(a) to (d). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Optical isomerism is a form of stereoisomerism. It occurs when a molecule has a chiral centre: a carbon atom with four different groups attached.
- Enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images.
- Enantiomers rotate plane-polarised light in opposite directions by the same angle.
- A racemic mixture (racemate) contains equal amounts of both enantiomers and does not rotate plane-polarised light.
- Draw enantiomers in 3D (wedge and dashed bonds).
- Edexcel: use optical activity of products to tell SN1 from SN2.

### Explained

**Chiral centre.** Butan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃: the second carbon has H, OH, CH₃ and C₂H₅, all different, so it is chiral. Mark it with an asterisk.

**Drawing.** Draw the chiral carbon with two bonds in the plane of the paper, one wedge (towards you) and one dashed (away). Draw the mirror image beside it with a dotted mirror line.

**Properties.** Enantiomers have identical physical properties (melting point, boiling point) and identical chemical properties with non-chiral reagents. They differ in:

- the direction they rotate plane-polarised light (one clockwise, +; the other anticlockwise, −). This is optical activity;
- how they react with other chiral molecules, such as enzymes and receptors in the body.

**Polarimeter.** Light is passed through a polariser so it vibrates in one plane, then through the sample, then an analyser that is turned to find the angle of rotation.

**Racemic mixtures.** Equal amounts of the two enantiomers rotate light equally in opposite directions, so there is no overall rotation. Reactions that go through a planar intermediate (carbocation, or a C=O carbon) usually give a racemic mixture, because attack from either face is equally likely.

**Using optical activity (Edexcel 17.5).** If a single enantiomer of a halogenoalkane is hydrolysed:

- by **SN2**, the nucleophile attacks from the opposite side to the leaving group, inverting the configuration. The product is a single enantiomer, still optically active (rotation of light changes).
- by **SN1**, the planar carbocation is attacked equally from both sides. The product is a racemic mixture, optically inactive.

**Why it matters.** Drugs often work because their shape fits a receptor. One enantiomer may be active and the other inactive or harmful. Thalidomide is the famous case: one enantiomer treated morning sickness, the other caused birth defects (and they interconvert in the body). Pharmaceutical companies now aim to make single enantiomers, which is costly but reduces side effects and dose (file 29).

### Common mistakes

- Marking a carbon with two identical groups as chiral.
- Saying enantiomers have different boiling points.
- Saying a racemic mixture "has no isomers". It has both, in equal amounts.

## 3.3.8 Aldehydes and ketones

*Refs: AQA 3.3.8 · Edexcel 17.6 to 17.8 · OCR A 6.1.2(a) to (e) · OCR B CD(i), CD(k) · Eduqas OA2.2(a) to (f). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Aldehydes are made by oxidising primary alcohols; ketones by oxidising secondary alcohols.
- Aldehydes are further oxidised to carboxylic acids; ketones are not.
- Tollens' and Fehling's (or Benedict's) tests.
- Reduction by NaBH₄ to alcohols; mechanism (nucleophilic addition with H⁻).
- Nucleophilic addition of HCN (from KCN and acid) to form hydroxynitriles; mechanism; racemic product.
- Hazards of KCN.
- 2,4-DNPH test and identifying the carbonyl from melting points of derivatives.
- Iodoform test for CH₃CO– (Edexcel, Eduqas).
- LiAlH₄ as a reducing agent (Edexcel).

### Explained

**Structure.** Aldehydes have C=O at the end of the chain (–CHO). Ketones have C=O inside the chain. The C=O bond is polar (C δ+), so nucleophiles attack the carbon. Unlike in C=C, the electron-rich region is not attractive to electrophiles in the same way; carbonyls undergo nucleophilic addition.

Small aldehydes and ketones dissolve in water because water hydrogen bonds to their oxygen, but they have lower boiling points than alcohols because they cannot hydrogen bond to each other.

**Oxidation.**

- RCHO + [O] → RCOOH, using acidified dichromate under reflux (orange to green).
- **Tollens'**: [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ is reduced to silver: Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag. Silver mirror. In alkali, RCHO is oxidised to RCOO⁻.
- **Fehling's** (or Benedict's): Cu²⁺ complex (blue) reduced to Cu₂O, a brick-red precipitate.
- Ketones give no reaction with any of these.

**Reduction with NaBH₄.** In aqueous or methanol solution, NaBH₄ provides hydride ions (H⁻), a nucleophile.

- Aldehyde → primary alcohol: CH₃CHO + 2[H] → CH₃CH₂OH.
- Ketone → secondary alcohol: CH₃COCH₃ + 2[H] → CH₃CH(OH)CH₃.

Mechanism (nucleophilic addition):

1. Lone pair on H⁻ attacks the δ+ carbon of C=O. Curly arrow from H⁻ to C; curly arrow from the C=O π bond to O.
2. An alkoxide ion forms (O⁻).
3. The O⁻ lone pair takes H⁺ from water (curly arrow from O⁻ lone pair to H of H₂O).

**LiAlH₄ (Edexcel).** A stronger reducing agent, in dry ether. Reduces aldehydes and ketones, and also carboxylic acids (to primary alcohols, Eduqas OA2.3(c)).

**Addition of HCN.** CH₃CHO + HCN → CH₃CH(OH)CN (2-hydroxypropanenitrile). Conditions: KCN with dilute acid (provides CN⁻ and H⁺); HCN itself is a very toxic gas, so it is made in solution as needed and handled in a fume cupboard. KCN is also highly toxic.

Mechanism:

1. Lone pair on C of CN⁻ attacks the δ+ carbon. Arrow from the C=O π bond to O.
2. Alkoxide ion forms.
3. O⁻ takes H⁺ (from HCN or H⁺).

The carbonyl carbon is trigonal planar, so CN⁻ attacks from above or below with equal chance. If the product has a chiral centre (aldehydes other than methanal, and unsymmetrical ketones), a racemic mixture forms.

This reaction adds one carbon to the chain, and the nitrile can be hydrolysed to a carboxylic acid (2-hydroxy acid) or reduced to an amine (file 29).

**2,4-Dinitrophenylhydrazine (Brady's reagent).** Reacts with the C=O of aldehydes and ketones (but not carboxylic acids or esters) to give an orange or yellow precipitate, a 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazone. It is a condensation (addition then loss of water). To identify the compound: recrystallise the precipitate, measure its melting point and compare with a data table of derivatives.

**Triiodomethane (iodoform) test (Edexcel, Eduqas).** Warm with iodine in sodium hydroxide solution (or KI and NaClO). A pale yellow precipitate of CHI₃ with an antiseptic smell shows a CH₃CO– group (methyl ketones and ethanal), or a CH₃CH(OH)– group (ethanol and secondary alcohols like propan-2-ol, which are first oxidised to CH₃CO–). The other product is the carboxylate with one fewer carbon: CH₃COCH₃ + 3I₂ + 4NaOH → CHI₃ + CH₃COONa + 3NaI + 3H₂O.

### Practical

**Eduqas: identifying aldehydes and ketones with 2,4-DNPH.** Add a few drops of the carbonyl to 2,4-DNPH solution. Filter the precipitate, recrystallise from the minimum volume of hot ethanol, dry, and measure the melting point. Compare with tables.

### Worked example

A compound C₄H₈O gives an orange precipitate with 2,4-DNPH, no silver mirror with Tollens', and a yellow precipitate with iodine in alkali. Identify it.

2,4-DNPH: carbonyl. No Tollens': ketone. Iodoform positive: CH₃CO–. So butanone, CH₃COCH₂CH₃.

### Common mistakes

- Saying NaBH₄ "adds hydrogen". Say hydride ion acts as a nucleophile.
- Drawing the first arrow from the C=O to the nucleophile.
- Saying HCN addition gives one optical isomer.
- Saying 2,4-DNPH distinguishes aldehydes from ketones. It reacts with both.

## Mark scheme

1. Two 3D mirror images with C bonded to H, OH, CH₃ and COOH (1); chiral centre marked (1).
2. Equal amounts of each enantiomer (1); rotate light by equal amounts in opposite directions, so effects cancel (1).
3. SN2 (1); one-step attack from opposite side gives a single (inverted) enantiomer (1); SN1 would give a planar carbocation and a racemic, inactive product (1).
4. Tollens' (or Fehling's) (1); propanal silver mirror (red precipitate), propanone no change (1).
5. H⁻ from NaBH₄ attacks δ+ C (1); arrow from C=O π bond to O (1); alkoxide takes H⁺ from water (1); propan-2-ol (1).
6. CN⁻ lone pair attacks δ+ C (1); arrow from C=O to O (1); intermediate takes H⁺ (1); product 2-hydroxypropanenitrile. C=O planar, so attack equally likely from either side (1); racemic mixture formed (1).
7. Fume cupboard / gloves (1); HCN is a toxic gas, so it is made in solution as needed (1).
8. Add 2,4-DNPH: orange precipitate (1); recrystallise and measure melting point (1); compare with known melting points of derivatives (1).
9. Propanone (1); propan-2-ol (1).

# Energetics: enthalpy changes, calorimetry, Hess's law and bond enthalpies

Reactions give out or take in heat. This file covers enthalpy change, the standard enthalpy definitions, measuring enthalpy changes by calorimetry, Hess's law cycles, and mean bond enthalpies. It includes AQA required practical 2.

## 3.1.4.1 Enthalpy change

*Refs: AQA 3.1.4.1 · Edexcel 8.1 to 8.4 · OCR A 3.2.1(a) to (d) · OCR B DF(d) · Eduqas C2.2(a). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Enthalpy change, ΔH, is the heat energy change measured at constant pressure.
- Exothermic reactions have negative ΔH; endothermic reactions have positive ΔH.
- Standard conditions: 100 kPa and a stated temperature, usually 298 K. Substances in their standard states.
- Definitions of standard enthalpy of combustion, ΔcH⦵, and standard enthalpy of formation, ΔfH⦵ (AQA). Others: reaction, neutralisation, atomisation (other boards).
- Enthalpy profile (level) diagrams with activation energy.

### Explained

**Sign convention.** Exothermic: the system loses energy to the surroundings, the surroundings warm up, ΔH is negative. Endothermic: the system takes energy in, the surroundings cool, ΔH is positive.

**Definitions** (learn the wording exactly in meaning):

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| ΔrH⦵ | Enthalpy change when the amounts in the equation react under standard conditions, all in standard states |
| ΔfH⦵ | Enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states under standard conditions |
| ΔcH⦵ | Enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely burned in oxygen under standard conditions, all in standard states |
| ΔneutH⦵ | Enthalpy change when acid and alkali react to form one mole of water under standard conditions |
| ΔatH⦵ | Enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms is formed from the element in its standard state |

ΔfH⦵ of an element in its standard state is zero by definition.

Example equations:

- ΔfH⦵ of ethanol: 2C(s) + 3H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → C₂H₅OH(l).
- ΔcH⦵ of ethanol: C₂H₅OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l).
- ΔatH⦵ of chlorine: ½Cl₂(g) → Cl(g).

**Enthalpy profile diagrams** show reactants and products as horizontal lines with ΔH as the vertical gap. A hump between them shows the activation energy, Eₐ: the minimum energy needed for a reaction to happen. For an exothermic reaction the products are below the reactants.

### Common mistakes

- Defining ΔfH⦵ for "one mole of product" without "from its elements in their standard states".
- Writing ΔcH⦵ equations with water as a gas. Under standard conditions water is a liquid.
- Using fractions wrongly: one mole of the substance of interest must appear, so other species may need fractions.

## 3.1.4.2 Calorimetry

*Refs: AQA 3.1.4.2, RP2 · Edexcel 8.5, 8.6, CP8 · OCR A 3.2.1(e), (h) · OCR B DF(f), DF(g) · Eduqas C2.2(d), (e). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- q = mcΔT, where m is the mass of the substance heated (usually water or solution), c its specific heat capacity, ΔT the temperature change.
- ΔH = −q ÷ n, in kJ mol⁻¹, with n the moles of the limiting reactant (or of fuel burned).
- Methods for reactions in solution and for combustion, and their sources of error.
- Using a cooling curve to correct ΔT.

### Explained

**Reactions in solution.** Use a polystyrene cup with a lid (to cut heat loss) in a beaker for stability. Measure the starting temperature of each solution, mix, stir, and record the temperature every 30 seconds. Assume the density of the solution is 1.00 g cm⁻³ and c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.

**Combustion.** Burn a fuel in a spirit burner under a copper can of water. Weigh the burner before and after to find the mass burned. Measure the temperature rise of the water. Shield from draughts. Large heat losses and incomplete combustion make the result far less exothermic than data-book values.

**Correcting for heat loss (extrapolation).** Record the temperature for a few minutes before mixing, then after. Plot temperature against time and extend the cooling line back to the moment of mixing. The difference between this extrapolated value and the starting temperature is a better ΔT, because it estimates the rise if no heat were lost.

**Sources of error.** Heat lost to surroundings (the biggest), heat absorbed by the container and thermometer, incomplete combustion (soot on the can), evaporation of fuel from the wick, assuming the solution has the same c and density as water.

### Worked example

1. 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl and 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ NaOH are mixed. The temperature rises by 6.8 K. Find ΔneutH.

   q = 100 × 4.18 × 6.8 = 2842 J. n(H₂O) = 0.0500 mol. ΔH = −2842 ÷ 0.0500 = −56 800 J mol⁻¹ = −56.8 kJ mol⁻¹.

2. Burning 0.46 g of ethanol heats 200 g of water from 20.0 °C to 35.5 °C. Find ΔcH.

   q = 200 × 4.18 × 15.5 = 12 958 J = 12.96 kJ. n = 0.46 ÷ 46.0 = 0.0100 mol. ΔcH = −12.96 ÷ 0.0100 = −1296 kJ mol⁻¹ (−1.3 × 10³ kJ mol⁻¹). The data value is −1367 kJ mol⁻¹; heat losses explain the difference.

### Common mistakes

- Using the mass of the fuel or solid in mcΔT. Use the mass of the water or solution.
- Forgetting the negative sign for an exothermic reaction.
- Forgetting to convert J to kJ.
- Using the total volume of one solution only, instead of both added together.

### Practical

**AQA RP2: measurement of an enthalpy change.** Typical task: displacement (zinc powder and copper(II) sulfate), neutralisation, or dissolving. Use excess of the solid so the solution is limiting. Plot temperature against time and extrapolate. Percentage uncertainty in a thermometer reading of ±0.5 °C on a rise of 6.8 °C: each reading contributes, so ±1.0 °C over ΔT, giving 15%. Use a larger rise (more concentrated solutions) or a more precise thermometer to reduce it.

**Edexcel CP8 and Eduqas indirect determination.** Find ΔH for a reaction that cannot be measured directly, for example the thermal decomposition of potassium hydrogencarbonate, or MgO + CO₂ → MgCO₃, by measuring the enthalpy changes of two reactions with hydrochloric acid and combining them in a Hess cycle (see 3.1.4.3).

**Eduqas: enthalpy change of combustion** of a series of alcohols with a spirit burner and calorimeter.

## 3.1.4.3 Applications of Hess's law

*Refs: AQA 3.1.4.3 · Edexcel 8.7, 8.8, CP8 · OCR A 3.2.1(g) · OCR B DF(g) · Eduqas C2.2(b), (d). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Hess's law: the enthalpy change for a reaction is independent of the route taken, provided the initial and final conditions are the same.
- Construct cycles and calculate ΔH from enthalpies of formation, from enthalpies of combustion, and from experimental data.

### Explained

Hess's law follows from conservation of energy. If you could go round a cycle and gain energy, you would have made energy from nothing.

**From enthalpies of formation:** ΔrH = ΣΔfH(products) − ΣΔfH(reactants). The arrows in the cycle point up from the elements to both reactants and products.

**From enthalpies of combustion:** ΔrH = ΣΔcH(reactants) − ΣΔcH(products). The arrows point down from both reactants and products to the combustion products.

Multiply each value by the number of moles in the equation.

### Worked example

1. Find ΔrH for C₂H₄(g) + H₂O(g) → C₂H₅OH(l). Data (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔfH C₂H₄ +52, H₂O(g) −242, C₂H₅OH(l) −277.

   ΔrH = −277 − (+52 + (−242)) = −277 + 190 = −87 kJ mol⁻¹.

2. Find ΔfH of methane. ΔcH (kJ mol⁻¹): C(s) −394, H₂(g) −286, CH₄(g) −890.

   C + 2H₂ → CH₄. ΔfH = [−394 + 2(−286)] − (−890) = −966 + 890 = −76 kJ mol⁻¹.

3. (Indirect method) MgO + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O, ΔH₁ = −146 kJ mol⁻¹. MgCO₃ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂, ΔH₂ = −98 kJ mol⁻¹. Find ΔH for MgCO₃ → MgO + CO₂.

   ΔH = ΔH₂ − ΔH₁ = −98 − (−146) = +48 kJ mol⁻¹.

### Common mistakes

- Reversing the subtraction for combustion data.
- Forgetting the stoichiometric multipliers.
- Including elements in their standard states with a non-zero ΔfH.

## 3.1.4.4 Bond enthalpies

*Refs: AQA 3.1.4.4 · Edexcel 8.9 to 8.11 · OCR A 3.2.1(f) · OCR B DF(e) · Eduqas C2.2(c). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Mean bond enthalpy: the enthalpy needed to break one mole of a covalent bond in gaseous molecules, averaged over many compounds.
- Breaking bonds is endothermic; making bonds is exothermic.
- ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds made).
- Why values calculated from mean bond enthalpies differ from data values.

### Explained

A C–H bond in methane is not quite the same as one in ethanol, so tables list an average. Results from mean bond enthalpies are therefore approximate. They are also only valid for gases; if a substance is a liquid, energy to vaporise it is ignored.

A reaction is exothermic if the bonds made release more energy than the bonds broken take in.

### Worked example

1. Use mean bond enthalpies to find ΔH for CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g). Data (kJ mol⁻¹): C–H 413, O=O 498, C=O 805, O–H 464.

   Broken: 4(413) + 2(498) = 1652 + 996 = 2648. Made: 2(805) + 4(464) = 1610 + 1856 = 3466. ΔH = 2648 − 3466 = −818 kJ mol⁻¹.

2. Find the mean N–H bond enthalpy given ΔfH(NH₃, g) = −46 kJ mol⁻¹, N≡N 945 and H–H 436 kJ mol⁻¹.

   ½N₂ + 1½H₂ → NH₃: broken = 472.5 + 654 = 1126.5. −46 = 1126.5 − 3E(N–H). E = 390.8 ≈ 391 kJ mol⁻¹.

### Common mistakes

- Doing made − broken.
- Forgetting that a double bond (O=O, C=O) is a single entry with its own value.
- Comparing a value calculated for gaseous water with a data value for liquid water and not commenting on it.

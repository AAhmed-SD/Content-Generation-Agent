# Aromatic chemistry: benzene, electrophilic substitution and phenol

Benzene's ring of delocalised electrons makes it unusually stable. This file covers the Kekulé and delocalised models and the evidence for delocalisation, why benzene undergoes substitution not addition, nitration, halogenation, Friedel–Crafts acylation and alkylation, sulfonation, and the chemistry of phenol, including directing effects.

## 3.3.10.1 Bonding

*Refs: AQA 3.3.10.1 · Edexcel 18.1 to 18.3 · OCR A 6.1.1(a) to (c), (f) · OCR B CD(d), CD(e), CD(f) · Eduqas OA1.2(a), (b). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Benzene, C₆H₆, is a flat hexagon with all C–C bonds the same length (intermediate between single and double), with delocalised π electrons.
- The Kekulé structure and why it was replaced: bond lengths, enthalpy of hydrogenation, and resistance to addition.
- Naming aromatic compounds.

### Explained

**Delocalised model.** Each carbon forms three σ bonds (two to C, one to H) at 120°. The remaining p orbital on each carbon overlaps sideways with both neighbours, forming a ring of π electron density above and below the plane. The six π electrons are spread over all six carbons. Drawn as a hexagon with a circle.

**Evidence against Kekulé (alternating single and double bonds):**

1. **Bond lengths.** X-ray diffraction shows all C–C bonds are 0.139 nm, between C–C (0.154 nm) and C=C (0.134 nm). Kekulé predicts two lengths.
2. **Enthalpy of hydrogenation.** Cyclohexene + H₂ → cyclohexane, ΔH = −120 kJ mol⁻¹. Kekulé benzene with three C=C would give 3 × −120 = −360 kJ mol⁻¹. Actual benzene: −208 kJ mol⁻¹. Benzene is about 152 kJ mol⁻¹ more stable than Kekulé predicts: the delocalisation (resonance) energy.
3. **Reactivity.** Kekulé benzene would decolorise bromine water like an alkene. Benzene does not; it reacts with bromine only with a catalyst, and by substitution.
4. Infrared spectra show no C=C absorption typical of alkenes (OCR A).

**Why substitution, not addition.** Addition would break the delocalised ring and lose the extra stability. Substitution replaces an H and keeps the ring.

**Why benzene is less reactive than alkenes towards electrophiles (Edexcel 18.3, OCR A 6.1.1(f)).** In an alkene, the π electrons are concentrated between two carbons: high electron density that can polarise Br₂. In benzene, the π electrons are spread over six carbons: lower electron density, not enough to polarise Br₂. A halogen carrier is needed.

**Naming.** Substituents as prefixes: chlorobenzene, nitrobenzene, methylbenzene. Some take benzene as a suffix-stem: phenol (C₆H₅OH), phenylamine (C₆H₅NH₂), benzoic acid (C₆H₅COOH), benzaldehyde. The C₆H₅– group is "phenyl". Number the ring to give lowest numbers: 2,4,6-tribromophenol; 1,3-dinitrobenzene.

**Combustion (Edexcel).** Arenes burn with a very smoky flame because of the high C : H ratio.

### Common mistakes

- Saying benzene has "three double bonds".
- Saying the hydrogenation enthalpy is "more exothermic" than predicted. It is less exothermic, so benzene is more stable.
- Saying delocalisation makes benzene more reactive.

## 3.3.10.2 Electrophilic substitution

*Refs: AQA 3.3.10.2 · Edexcel 18.4 to 18.7 · OCR A 6.1.1(d), (e), (g) to (l), 6.2.4(d) · OCR B WM(c), CD(g) · Eduqas OA1.2(c), (d), OA2.1(c), (d). Level: A-level only (OCR B WM(c) AS).*

### What you need to know

- Nitration (conc. HNO₃ and conc. H₂SO₄, below 55 °C): formation of NO₂⁺ and mechanism.
- Halogenation with a halogen carrier (FeBr₃, AlCl₃, or Fe): mechanism.
- Friedel–Crafts acylation (acyl chloride and AlCl₃): mechanism. Friedel–Crafts alkylation (halogenoalkane and AlCl₃) (Edexcel, OCR A, OCR B).
- Sulfonation (OCR B).
- Uses of nitration: explosives (TNT) and making aromatic amines for dyes.
- Phenol: weak acid; reactions with NaOH, Na, bromine water and dilute nitric acid; ethanoyl chloride; FeCl₃ test. Why phenol reacts more easily than benzene.
- Directing effects (OCR A).

### Explained

**General mechanism.**

1. The electrophile, E⁺, is generated.
2. A curly arrow from the delocalised ring (inside the circle) to E⁺. The intermediate has a horseshoe of delocalisation over five carbons with a + charge; the sixth carbon is bonded to both E and H.
3. A curly arrow from the C–H bond back into the ring restores delocalisation; H⁺ is lost.

**Nitration.** Electrophile: nitronium ion, NO₂⁺.

HNO₃ + 2H₂SO₄ → NO₂⁺ + H₃O⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻

Sulfuric acid is the stronger acid and protonates nitric acid, which then loses water. The H⁺ released at the end reforms H₂SO₄ with HSO₄⁻ (catalyst). Below 55 °C to prevent multiple substitution.

C₆H₆ + HNO₃ → C₆H₅NO₂ + H₂O

Uses: nitrobenzene is reduced to phenylamine for dyes (files 26, 37); methylbenzene is nitrated three times to make TNT.

**Halogenation.** Electrophile: Br⁺ (as a polarised complex). The halogen carrier accepts a lone pair from Br₂:

Br₂ + FeBr₃ → Br⁺ + FeBr₄⁻

After substitution, H⁺ + FeBr₄⁻ → HBr + FeBr₃ (catalyst regenerated). Iron filings work because they react with bromine to form FeBr₃. AlCl₃ with Cl₂ gives chlorobenzene.

**Friedel–Crafts acylation.** Electrophile: acylium ion, RCO⁺.

CH₃COCl + AlCl₃ → CH₃CO⁺ + AlCl₄⁻

C₆H₆ + CH₃COCl → C₆H₅COCH₃ + HCl (phenylethanone). Conditions: anhydrous (AlCl₃ reacts with water), reflux at about 50 °C. Then H⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ → AlCl₃ + HCl.

**Friedel–Crafts alkylation.** Electrophile: carbocation from a halogenoalkane: CH₃CH₂Cl + AlCl₃ → CH₃CH₂⁺ + AlCl₄⁻. Gives ethylbenzene. It forms a C–C bond to the ring (OCR A 6.2.4(d)). Drawback: the alkylbenzene is more reactive than benzene, so it substitutes again.

**Sulfonation (OCR B).** Heat benzene with concentrated (or fuming) sulfuric acid: C₆H₆ + H₂SO₄ → C₆H₅SO₃H + H₂O. The electrophile is SO₃. Benzenesulfonic acids are used to make detergents and to make dyes water-soluble.

**Phenol.**

- **Acidity.** C₆H₅OH ⇌ C₆H₅O⁻ + H⁺. A lone pair on O overlaps with the ring's π system, so the negative charge on phenoxide is partly delocalised: phenol is a weak acid (stronger than alcohols, weaker than carboxylic acids). It reacts with NaOH (C₆H₅OH + NaOH → C₆H₅ONa + H₂O) and sodium (fizzing), but not with carbonates.
- **FeCl₃ test.** Neutral iron(III) chloride gives a purple colour.
- **Bromine water.** At room temperature, no catalyst: C₆H₅OH + 3Br₂ → 2,4,6-tribromophenol (white precipitate) + 3HBr. Bromine water decolorises.
- **Dilute nitric acid** at room temperature gives a mixture of 2-nitrophenol and 4-nitrophenol (OCR A).
- **Ethanoyl chloride** gives the ester phenyl ethanoate (Eduqas): C₆H₅OH + CH₃COCl → CH₃COOC₆H₅ + HCl. (Carboxylic acids react too slowly with phenol to make esters.)

**Why phenol reacts more easily than benzene.** A lone pair from the O is partly delocalised into the ring, increasing the electron density. The ring can now polarise Br₂ itself, so no halogen carrier is needed, and three H atoms are substituted.

**Directing effects (OCR A 6.1.1(k), (l)).** Electron-donating groups (–OH, –NH₂, –CH₃) activate the ring and direct new substituents to the 2- and 4-positions. Electron-withdrawing groups (–NO₂, –COOH, –CHO) deactivate the ring and direct to the 3-position. So nitrobenzene with Br₂/FeBr₃ gives 3-bromonitrobenzene; phenylamine with bromine water gives 2,4,6-tribromophenylamine.

### Worked example

Suggest a two-step synthesis of phenylamine from benzene.

Step 1: concentrated HNO₃ and H₂SO₄ below 55 °C: nitrobenzene. Step 2: reduce with tin and concentrated HCl, then NaOH (file 26).

### Common mistakes

- Drawing the intermediate with a full circle. It is a horseshoe (broken circle) with a + inside, not covering the carbon bonded to E and H.
- Starting the first arrow from a carbon or a bond in a Kekulé drawing. Start inside the delocalised ring.
- Forgetting to regenerate the catalyst.
- Saying phenol reacts with carbonates.

1. Each C forms three σ bonds (1); p orbitals overlap sideways (1); ring of delocalised π electrons above and below the plane (1).
2. Kekulé: 3 × −120 = −360 kJ mol⁻¹ expected (1); actual −208, less exothermic by 152 kJ mol⁻¹ (1); delocalisation makes benzene more stable (1).

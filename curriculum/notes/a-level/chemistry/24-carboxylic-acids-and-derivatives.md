# Carboxylic acids, esters, acyl chlorides, anhydrides and amides

Carboxylic acids and their derivatives all contain the C=O group with something else on the same carbon. This file covers the acidity and reactions of carboxylic acids, making and hydrolysing esters, fats, soaps and biodiesel, acyl chlorides and acid anhydrides with the nucleophilic addition–elimination mechanism, making aspirin, and amides. It includes AQA required practical 10.

## Overview

A-level for most boards. Condensation polymers (polyesters, polyamides) are in file 27; nitriles are in file 29; amines are in file 26.

## 3.3.9.1 Carboxylic acids and esters

*Refs: AQA 3.3.9.1 · Edexcel 17.9 to 17.12, 17.15 · OCR A 6.1.3(a) to (d) · OCR B PL(h), PL(k) to (m) · Eduqas C3.4(g), (h), OA2.3(a) to (f). Level: A-level only (Eduqas C3.4 AS).*

### What you need to know

- Carboxylic acids are weak acids; they react with carbonates to release CO₂, and with metals, bases and alkalis.
- Esterification with an alcohol and acid catalyst; naming esters.
- Uses of esters: perfumes, flavourings, solvents, plasticisers.
- Hydrolysis of esters in acid and in alkali.
- Fats and oils are esters of glycerol (propane-1,2,3-triol); alkaline hydrolysis makes soap; biodiesel is a mixture of methyl esters made from vegetable oils and methanol.

### Explained

**Physical properties.** The –COOH group forms hydrogen bonds, so acids have high boiling points (often forming dimers) and small ones dissolve in water.

**Acidity.** CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺. Weak: only partly dissociated. The ethanoate ion is stabilised because the negative charge is delocalised over both oxygens.

**Relative acidity (Eduqas OA2.3(a)).** Carboxylic acids > phenols > water > alcohols. Phenol is acidic because the phenoxide ion's charge is partly delocalised into the ring (less than in carboxylate). Ethanol is not acidic because the ethyl group pushes electrons towards O⁻, destabilising the ion. Consequence: carboxylic acids react with carbonates (fizz), phenol does not; phenol reacts with NaOH; alcohols react with neither but do react with sodium.

**Reactions of acids.**

- 2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂ (test for –COOH).
- CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O.
- 2CH₃COOH + Mg → (CH₃COO)₂Mg + H₂.

**Other reactions (Edexcel, Eduqas).**

- Reduction to primary alcohols with LiAlH₄ in dry ether: CH₃COOH + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂OH + H₂O.
- With PCl₅ (or SOCl₂) to give acyl chlorides: CH₃COOH + PCl₅ → CH₃COCl + POCl₃ + HCl.
- **Decarboxylation (Eduqas):** heating the sodium salt with soda lime (NaOH/CaO) removes CO₂: CH₃COONa + NaOH → CH₄ + Na₂CO₃. Benzoate gives benzene.
- **Aromatic acids (Eduqas):** methylbenzene is oxidised to benzoic acid by heating under reflux with alkaline KMnO₄, then acidifying.

**Esterification.** Carboxylic acid + alcohol ⇌ ester + water, with a concentrated sulfuric acid catalyst, warm. It is reversible, so yields are moderate.

CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂OH ⇌ CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O (ethyl ethanoate).

Naming: the alcohol part first (ethyl), the acid part second (-oate). HCOOCH₃ is methyl methanoate; CH₃CH₂COOCH₃ is methyl propanoate.

Esters can also be made from acid anhydrides (OCR A 6.1.3(c)(ii)) or acyl chlorides (below), which go to completion.

**Uses of esters.** Sweet, fruity smells: perfumes and food flavourings. Polar but cannot hydrogen bond to each other, so they are volatile solvents for glues and nail varnish. Plasticisers for PVC.

**Hydrolysis.**

- Acid hydrolysis (reflux with dilute H₂SO₄ or HCl): reversible, gives the acid and alcohol. CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂OH.
- Alkaline hydrolysis (reflux with NaOH(aq)): goes to completion, gives the carboxylate salt and alcohol. CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + CH₃CH₂OH. Add acid afterwards to get the free acid.

**Fats and oils.** Triglycerides: esters of propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol) with three long-chain fatty acids. Oils are liquid (unsaturated chains with C=C kinks pack less well); fats are solid.

- Hydrolysis with hot NaOH gives glycerol and sodium salts of the fatty acids: **soap** (saponification). The long hydrocarbon tail dissolves in grease and the ionic head in water.
- **Biodiesel**: the oil reacts with methanol (KOH catalyst) to give methyl esters of the fatty acids and glycerol (transesterification).

### Worked example

Name the ester formed from propan-1-ol and ethanoic acid, and give the products of its hydrolysis with sodium hydroxide.

Propyl ethanoate, CH₃COOCH₂CH₂CH₃. Hydrolysis gives sodium ethanoate and propan-1-ol.

### Common mistakes

- Naming esters with the acid part first.
- Forgetting that alkaline hydrolysis gives the salt, not the acid.
- Saying carboxylic acids are strong because they fizz with carbonates.

## 3.3.9.2 Acylation

*Refs: AQA 3.3.9.2, RP10 · Edexcel 17.13, 17.14, CP16 · OCR A 6.1.3(c)(ii), (e), (f), 6.2.2(b) · OCR B PL(l), PL(n) · Eduqas OA2.3(f), (g). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Acyl chlorides (RCOCl) and acid anhydrides ((RCO)₂O) react with water, alcohols, ammonia and primary amines.
- The nucleophilic addition–elimination mechanism (acyl chlorides).
- Advantages of ethanoic anhydride over ethanoyl chloride in making aspirin.
- Structures of primary and secondary amides; making amides.
- Preparing a pure organic solid and testing purity by melting point.

### Explained

**Making acyl chlorides (OCR A 6.1.3(e)).** RCOOH + SOCl₂ → RCOCl + SO₂ + HCl. Both by-products are gases, so they leave the mixture.

**Reactions of ethanoyl chloride.** All are vigorous at room temperature with steamy fumes of HCl.

| Nucleophile | Product | Equation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water | Carboxylic acid | CH₃COCl + H₂O → CH₃COOH + HCl |
| Alcohol | Ester | CH₃COCl + CH₃OH → CH₃COOCH₃ + HCl |
| Ammonia | Primary amide | CH₃COCl + 2NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + NH₄Cl |
| Primary amine | N-substituted (secondary) amide | CH₃COCl + 2CH₃NH₂ → CH₃CONHCH₃ + CH₃NH₃⁺Cl⁻ |
| Phenol | Phenyl ester (file 25) | CH₃COCl + C₆H₅OH → CH₃COOC₆H₅ + HCl |

With ammonia and amines, the HCl reacts with a second molecule of base, so two are used.

**Mechanism (nucleophilic addition–elimination)**, ethanoyl chloride with methanol:

1. Addition: the lone pair on the O of methanol attacks the δ+ carbon of C=O (the carbon is very δ+ because both O and Cl pull electrons). Arrow from the C=O π bond to O.
2. Elimination: the O⁻ lone pair reforms C=O (arrow from O⁻ to the C–O bond); the C–Cl bond breaks (arrow to Cl), releasing Cl⁻.
3. Cl⁻ removes H⁺ from the oxygen that attacked (arrow from the O–H bond to O), giving HCl.

For ammonia and amines the nucleophile is the N lone pair, and H⁺ is removed in the same way.

**Acid anhydrides** react in the same way but more slowly, with RCOOH as the by-product instead of HCl:

(CH₃CO)₂O + CH₃OH → CH₃COOCH₃ + CH₃COOH

**Aspirin.** 2-hydroxybenzoic acid (salicylic acid) is reacted with ethanoic anhydride (with a little concentrated acid catalyst) to acylate the –OH: the product is 2-ethanoyloxybenzoic acid. Ethanoic anhydride is used industrially rather than ethanoyl chloride because it is cheaper, less corrosive, reacts less violently (safer, easier to control), does not react as fast with water, and its by-product is ethanoic acid rather than toxic, corrosive HCl gas.

**Amides.**

- Primary amide: RCONH₂ (ethanamide).
- Secondary (N-substituted) amide: RCONHR' (N-methylethanamide).
- Made from acyl chlorides with ammonia or amines, or by heating ammonium salts of carboxylic acids (Eduqas OA2.3(g)): CH₃COONH₄ → CH₃CONH₂ + H₂O. Amides can be dehydrated to nitriles with P₄O₁₀ (file 29).
- Amides are hydrolysed by acid (giving the acid and NH₄⁺) or alkali (giving the carboxylate and NH₃) (OCR B PL(m)).

### Practical

**AQA RP10 (and Edexcel CP16): preparing a pure organic solid and testing its purity, and preparing a pure organic liquid.**

*Solid (aspirin):*

1. Warm 2-hydroxybenzoic acid with excess ethanoic anhydride and a few drops of concentrated phosphoric or sulfuric acid, in a water bath, for about 20 minutes.
2. Add cold water to destroy excess anhydride, cool in ice to crystallise.
3. Filter under reduced pressure (Büchner funnel and flask), washing with a little ice-cold water.
4. **Recrystallise:** dissolve in the minimum volume of hot solvent (so the solution is saturated when cooled; impurities stay in solution), filter hot if there are insoluble impurities, cool slowly, filter under reduced pressure, wash with a little cold solvent, and dry.
5. **Melting point:** pack a little dry solid in a capillary tube and heat slowly in a melting point apparatus. A pure solid melts sharply at the data-book value (aspirin about 136 °C); impurities lower the melting point and widen the range.

*Liquid (ester, such as ethyl ethanoate):* reflux ethanol, ethanoic acid and concentrated sulfuric acid; distil; wash with sodium carbonate solution in a separating funnel (venting CO₂); wash with calcium chloride solution to remove ethanol; dry with anhydrous CaCl₂; redistil, collecting at the boiling point (77 °C).

### Common mistakes

- Missing the H⁺ loss step at the end of the addition–elimination mechanism.
- Using one ammonia molecule in the equation with acyl chlorides.
- Using too much solvent in recrystallisation (yield falls).
- Saying a broad melting range means a higher melting point. Impurities lower it.

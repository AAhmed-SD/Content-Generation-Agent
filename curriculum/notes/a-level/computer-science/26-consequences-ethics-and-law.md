# Consequences of computing: ethics and law

This file covers the moral, social, ethical, legal and cultural opportunities and risks of computing: monitoring, collecting and spreading personal data, the power and responsibility of software engineers, values embedded in algorithms, scale, challenges for lawmakers, the UK laws on data protection, computer misuse, copyright and surveillance, freedom of information, professional codes of conduct, employment, and OCR's listed issues (AI, automated decisions, environment, censorship, piracy and offensive communications, and layout, colour and character sets).

Builds on [GCSE: Impacts, ethics and law](../../gcse/computer-science/19-impacts-ethics-and-law.md). At A-level, longer discussion answers are expected: weigh both sides with specific examples and reach a justified conclusion.

## 4.8.1 Individual (moral), social (ethical), legal and cultural issues and opportunities

*Refs: AQA 4.8.1 · OCR 1.5.2(a) · Eduqas C1.9: Social and economic change.*

### What you need to know

- Digital technology has transformed how information flows, giving huge new capacity to:
  - monitor behaviour: CCTV with face recognition, location tracking from phones, workplace monitoring of keystrokes and emails, websites tracking browsing with cookies, smart devices recording use;
  - amass and analyse personal information: companies and governments build detailed profiles from purchases, searches, social media and sensors, and use data mining and machine learning to predict behaviour (credit scores, targeted adverts, insurance prices);
  - distribute, publish, communicate and disseminate personal information: social media lets anyone publish instantly to millions; information is copied and cannot be fully removed; data breaches expose millions of records.
- Computer scientists and software engineers therefore have power, and responsibilities that go with it, through the algorithms they design and the code they deploy.
- Software and algorithms embed moral and cultural values: what a system optimises for, which data it is trained on, and what its designers assumed. Examples: recruitment or loan algorithms trained on past biased decisions repeat that bias; face recognition that is less accurate for some groups; recommendation systems designed to maximise engagement that spread extreme content.
- Scale: one piece of software can be used by billions of people, so one engineer can do great good (free education, medical diagnosis, disaster maps) and also great harm (a security flaw, a biased algorithm, a viral misinformation feature).
- Challenges facing legislators:
  - technology changes faster than laws can be written;
  - the internet crosses borders: a website legal in one country may break another's laws, and offenders may be abroad;
  - balancing privacy against security and crime prevention, and free speech against harm;
  - enforcing laws against anonymous users and huge global companies;
  - lawmakers may not fully understand the technology;
  - new questions, such as who is responsible when an AI or self-driving car causes harm.

### OCR's listed issues

- Computers in the workforce: automation replaces some jobs (manufacturing, checkouts, call centres, now some office and creative work) and creates others (software, data, maintenance); needs retraining; can remove dangerous or repetitive work; flexible and remote working. Also Eduqas: effect on employment in the computing industry (new specialist roles, global outsourcing) and wider society (deskilling, a digital divide between those with and without skills).
- Automated decision making: decisions made by algorithms without a person (credit approval, insurance prices, exam grading, stock trading). Fast, consistent and cheap, but can be biased, opaque ("why was I refused?"), and errors are repeated at scale. Data protection law gives people rights over solely automated decisions with significant effects.
- Artificial intelligence: systems that perform tasks needing human-like intelligence (recognising images and speech, translation, generating text, driving). Opportunities: medicine, science, accessibility, productivity. Risks: bias, job losses, deepfakes and misinformation, accountability, privacy, military use.
- Environmental effects: manufacturing uses rare metals and energy; data centres use large amounts of electricity and water; e-waste is often dumped or recycled unsafely; devices are replaced quickly. Positive effects: smart grids, modelling climate, remote working cutting travel, optimising transport.
- Censorship and the internet: governments or organisations controlling what people can access or publish (blocking sites, filtering content). Arguments for: protecting children, stopping terrorism and illegal content. Against: free speech, political suppression, over-blocking, who decides.
- Monitoring behaviour and analysing personal information: as above, including the trade-off between convenience (personalised services) and privacy.
- Piracy and offensive communications: illegal copying and sharing of software, music and films (easy, hard to police; loses creators income); online harassment, trolling and hate speech (anonymity encourages it; platforms' responsibilities; laws such as the Communications Act 2003 and Malicious Communications Act 1988, and the Online Safety Act 2023).
- Layout, colour paradigms and character sets: designing for a global, diverse audience. Colours carry different meanings in different cultures (white for weddings in some, mourning in others; red for danger or luck); some languages read right to left (Arabic, Hebrew) or top to bottom; interfaces need Unicode character sets for all languages (file 18); designs must be accessible (colour-blind users, screen readers, contrast, font size).

## Legislation

*Refs: OCR 1.5.1(a), 1.5.1(b), 1.5.1(c), 1.5.1(d) · Eduqas C1.9: Legislation.*

### Data protection (Data Protection Act 1998, now Data Protection Act 2018 and UK GDPR)

- Applies to organisations (data controllers) that process personal data about living people (data subjects).
- Principles: personal data must be processed lawfully, fairly and transparently; collected for specified, explicit purposes and not used for others; adequate, relevant and limited to what is needed; accurate and kept up to date; kept no longer than necessary; processed securely (protected against loss, theft and unauthorised access); and the controller must be accountable (able to show it complies). (The 1998 Act had eight similar principles, including data subjects' rights and not transferring data outside the EEA without adequate protection.)
- Rights of data subjects: to be informed; to access their data (subject access request); to correct it; to have it erased in some cases; to object to processing such as direct marketing; rights over automated decision making.
- Enforced by the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO), which can fine organisations.

### Computer Misuse Act 1990

- Three main offences (with later additions):
  1. Unauthorised access to computer material (for example guessing someone's password to look at their files).
  2. Unauthorised access with intent to commit or facilitate further offences (for example accessing a bank system to steal money).
  3. Unauthorised acts with intent to impair the operation of a computer (or recklessly doing so): spreading malware, deleting files, denial of service attacks.
  - Later amendments added making, supplying or obtaining tools for these offences.
- Penalties range from fines to long prison sentences for serious cases.

### Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988

- Protects the creators of original works (software, music, films, images, books) from having their work copied, distributed, adapted or performed without permission. Copyright is automatic: no need to register.
- Illegal: copying software without a licence, sharing music or films online, using images from the web without permission.
- Software licences set what users may do. Patents protect inventions (in the UK, software alone is rarely patentable).

### Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000 (RIPA)

- Sets out when public bodies (police, security services, some councils) may carry out surveillance, intercept communications and access communications data, and the authorisation needed.
- Can require internet service providers to provide access to customers' communications and to install equipment for interception, and can require people to hand over encryption keys or decrypt data.
- Aims to balance national security and crime prevention against privacy. Much of the interception regime is now in the Investigatory Powers Act 2016.

### Freedom of Information Act 2000 (Eduqas)

- Gives the public the right to request information held by public authorities (government departments, councils, schools, the NHS, police). The authority must usually reply within 20 working days, with some exemptions (national security, personal data, commercial confidentiality).
- Promotes openness and accountability. It does not cover private companies.

## Professional behaviour

*Refs: Eduqas C1.9: Professional behaviour.*

### What you need to know

- Professional bodies such as BCS (the Chartered Institute for IT) and the ACM publish codes of conduct that members must follow.
- Typical duties: act in the public interest (health, safety, privacy, the environment, no discrimination); only take on work you are competent to do and keep skills up to date; act with integrity towards employers and clients (honesty, avoiding conflicts of interest, respecting confidentiality); uphold the reputation of the profession and report wrongdoing.
- How codes promote professional behaviour: they set clear expected standards; breaking them can lead to expulsion, which affects reputation and employment; they give engineers grounds to refuse unethical instructions; they build public trust.

### Worked example

Discussion question: "A local council plans to use an algorithm to decide which families get extra social care visits. Discuss the issues." A strong answer covers:

- Benefits: consistent and fast; may spot risks humans miss; uses limited staff efficiently.
- Risks: biased training data could unfairly target or overlook groups; decisions may be hard to explain to families; errors repeated across many cases; data protection: lawful basis, minimising data, security, and rights over automated decisions.
- Safeguards: human review of decisions, testing for bias, transparency about how it works, an appeals process, DPA compliance.
- Justified conclusion: for example, acceptable only as a tool that supports, not replaces, a social worker's judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Quoting the Computer Misuse Act for copying music (that is copyright) or for selling data (that is data protection).
- Writing "the DPA stops companies storing data". It regulates how they store and use it.
- Writing one-sided answers in discussion questions, or making general points with no specific example.

## Mark scheme

2. The choices designers make about data, goals and rules reflect their values/assumptions (1); these affect people's lives at scale (1); example such as a hiring algorithm trained on biased past decisions, or engagement-maximising feeds (1).
3. Level-based (6). Points: technology changes faster than laws; international nature/jurisdiction; anonymity and enforcement; balancing privacy, security and free speech; lack of technical understanding; power of global companies; new issues such as AI liability. Top band: several well-developed points with examples and a conclusion.
4. Computer Misuse Act 1990 (1); unauthorised access to computer material (1); unauthorised access with intent to commit further offences/unauthorised modification (1).
5. Any four: lawful, fair and transparent; specific purposes; adequate, relevant and limited; accurate and up to date; not kept longer than needed; secure; accountability (4).
6. Data must not be kept longer than necessary (1); data must be limited to what is needed for the stated purpose (1).
7. Allows authorised public bodies to carry out surveillance/intercept communications/access communications data (1); can require ISPs to provide access or people to hand over encryption keys (1); concern: invasion of privacy/possible misuse/chilling effect (1).
8. Level-based (6). Benefits: speed, consistency, cost, can process vast data. Drawbacks: bias, lack of transparency/explanation, errors at scale, accountability, job losses, rights under data protection law. Top band: balanced with examples and a justified conclusion.
9. Any two issues: energy use of data centres; e-waste; mining of rare materials; short replacement cycles (2). Help: smart energy management/modelling climate/remote working/route optimisation (1).

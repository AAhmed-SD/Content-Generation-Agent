# 3.5.2 Aquatic food production systems

Fish and shellfish are a major source of protein. Wild fisheries are a renewable resource only if catches stay within what stocks can replace. This file covers marine productivity, fishing methods and their impacts, how stocks are assessed and managed, and aquaculture.

## 3.5.2.1 Marine productivity

### What you need to know

Nutrient availability; the photic and aphotic zones; productivity of the open ocean, coastal waters and upwelling areas.

### Explained

- **Photic zone**: the top layer (down to about 200 m in clear water, much less in turbid water) where enough light reaches for photosynthesis. Below is the **aphotic zone**, with no photosynthesis; life there depends on organic matter sinking from above.
- Nutrients (nitrate, phosphate, iron) sink out of the photic zone in dead organisms and faeces. In the open ocean a warm surface layer sits over cold deep water and they hardly mix, so surface waters are nutrient-poor. The open ocean has low productivity per square metre despite its huge area ("ocean desert").
- **Coastal waters** receive nutrients from rivers and mixing by tides and waves in shallow water, so productivity is higher.
- **Upwelling areas** (for example off Peru and north-west Africa): winds push surface water offshore and cold, nutrient-rich deep water rises to replace it, giving very high productivity and huge fisheries. When El Niño weakens upwelling off Peru, anchovy catches collapse.

## 3.5.2.2 Fishing

### What you need to know

- Methods: pelagic and demersal trawling, purse seining, drift netting, long lining, traps for shellfish.
- Impacts: overfishing, changes in population age structure, by-catch, ghost fishing, damage to the seabed and reefs, food web effects.
- Estimating fish populations and maximum sustainable yield (MSY): biomass, recruitment, growth, natural mortality and catch; catch size, catch per unit effort, mean size and age; breeding success from egg and larva surveys.
- Ways to reduce impacts: quotas, net design, effort restrictions, no-take zones and closed seasons, minimum and maximum landing sizes, captive rearing and release, biodegradable or tracked gear.

### Explained

| Method | Target | Description | Problems |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pelagic trawl | Mid-water shoals (herring, mackerel) | Cone-shaped net towed through the water | By-catch of dolphins |
| Demersal (bottom) trawl | Seabed fish (cod, plaice) and shrimp | Net dragged along the seabed | Destroys seabed habitats and reefs; high by-catch |
| Purse seine | Surface shoals (tuna, sardines) | Net encircles a shoal then is closed at the bottom | Can catch dolphins and juvenile tuna |
| Drift net | Surface fish | Long curtain of net drifting freely | Huge by-catch of seabirds, turtles, mammals; large drift nets banned in many waters |
| Long line | Tuna, swordfish, cod | Line with thousands of baited hooks | Seabirds (albatrosses) and turtles hooked |
| Traps (pots) | Crabs, lobsters | Baited cages on the seabed | Low impact, though lost pots ghost fish |

**Impacts**:

- Overfishing: catch exceeds the rate of replacement, so stocks decline (for example the collapse of the north-west Atlantic cod fishery in the early 1990s).
- Age structure: large old fish are removed first, so populations become dominated by young fish; fewer, smaller breeding fish produce fewer eggs.
- By-catch: unwanted species and undersized fish caught and usually discarded dead.
- Ghost fishing: lost or abandoned nets and pots keep catching animals for years.
- Seabed damage from trawls and dredges.
- Food web effects: removing predators or prey changes whole ecosystems (fewer forage fish means less food for seabirds and seals).

**Stock assessment**: a stock grows by recruitment (young fish joining) and growth of individuals, and shrinks by natural mortality and catch. Scientists estimate:

- total catch and catch per unit effort (CPUE): catch divided by fishing effort (for example tonnes per day at sea). Falling CPUE suggests a falling stock.
- mean size and age of fish caught (from scales or ear bones, otoliths): falling values suggest overfishing.
- breeding success from surveys of eggs and larvae in plankton samples.

**MSY**: the largest catch that can be taken year after year without reducing the stock. Population growth is fastest at about half the carrying capacity, so harvesting to keep the stock near that level gives the MSY. Fishing harder than this lowers future catches.

**Management**:

| Measure | How it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Quotas (total allowable catch) | Limit the mass landed |
| Net design (larger mesh, square-mesh panels, escape hatches, turtle excluder devices) | Lets young fish and non-target species escape |
| Effort restriction | Limits days at sea, boat numbers or engine power |
| No-take zones and closed seasons | Protect breeding areas and spawning times; adults spill over into fished areas |
| Minimum landing size | Fish can breed at least once before capture |
| Maximum landing size | Protects large, highly fertile females |
| Captive rearing and release | Restocks depleted populations |
| Biodegradable gear, tracked gear | Lost gear stops fishing or can be recovered |

### Worked example

A fleet caught 12 000 t in 3000 boat-days in one year and 8000 t in 4000 boat-days five years later. CPUE fell from 4.0 to 2.0 t per boat-day: the stock has probably halved even though catches only fell by a third.

## 3.5.2.3 Aquaculture

### What you need to know

- Extensive and intensive aquaculture.
- Principles: species and stock selection, breeding, disease control, competition, nutrition, temperature, dissolved oxygen, light, water flow.
- How far aquaculture can replace fishing (food needs, trophic efficiency).
- Reducing impacts: site selection, organic waste control, lower stocking density, preventing escapes, controlling feed, reducing pesticide and antibiotic use.

### Explained

- **Extensive**: low stocking density in ponds or lagoons, with natural food; low input and output (for example carp in ponds, mussels on ropes).
- **Intensive**: high stocking density in tanks or sea cages with manufactured feed; high output (for example salmon farms).

Farmers choose fast-growing, disease-resistant species with low FCRs; control breeding to produce young; prevent disease (vaccination, low density); remove competitors and predators; feed balanced diets; and keep temperature, oxygen (aeration), light and water flow at the best levels.

**Can it replace fishing?** Herbivores and filter feeders (carp, tilapia, mussels, oysters) can be farmed efficiently. Carnivores (salmon, prawns) are often fed fishmeal and fish oil from wild fish, so they are at a high trophic level and may use more wild fish than they produce, though plant-based feeds are reducing this.

**Impacts and solutions**:

| Impact | Solution |
| --- | --- |
| Uneaten food and faeces cause eutrophication and deoxygenate the seabed | Site in areas with strong currents; lower density; control feeding; rotate sites |
| Disease and parasites (sea lice) spread to wild fish | Lower density; cleaner fish that eat lice; vaccines |
| Pesticides and antibiotics pollute water and select for resistance | Reduce use; biological controls |
| Escaped farmed fish compete and interbreed with wild fish | Stronger cages; sterile stock |
| Mangroves cleared for shrimp ponds | Careful site selection |

### Common mistakes

- Saying MSY is reached by keeping the stock at its maximum size. Growth is fastest at intermediate size.
- Saying aquaculture always takes pressure off wild stocks.

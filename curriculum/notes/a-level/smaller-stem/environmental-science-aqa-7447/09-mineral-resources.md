# 3.2.3 Mineral resources

Minerals from the lithosphere supply metals, industrial raw materials and building materials. They form over geological time, so they are non-renewable on human timescales. The geology of ore formation and exploration is covered in more depth in [Geology 18 Mining geology](../geology-ocr-h414/18-mining-geology.md).

## 3.2.3.1 Minerals are non-renewable

### What you need to know

Categories: metals and their ores; industrial minerals; construction materials.

### Explained

- **Metal ores**: rocks containing enough metal to extract at a profit, for example haematite (iron), bauxite (aluminium), chalcopyrite (copper), galena (lead), cassiterite (tin).
- **Industrial minerals**: used for their chemical or physical properties, for example china clay (paper, ceramics), salt, potash (fertiliser), gypsum (plaster), fluorite.
- **Construction materials**: sand, gravel, crushed rock (aggregate), limestone (cement), clay (bricks), building stone. Used in huge tonnages, so transport cost matters and they are dug close to where they are used.

## 3.2.3.2 Processes that concentrate deposits

### What you need to know

Hydrothermal deposition; metamorphic processes; Proterozoic marine sediments; physical sedimentary processes; biological sedimentary processes.

### Explained

| Process | How it concentrates | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrothermal | Hot water circulating near a magma body dissolves metals and deposits them as it cools, in veins and fractures | Cornish tin and copper veins; lead-zinc veins |
| Metamorphic | Heat and pressure recrystallise rock, forming new useful minerals | Marble, slate, garnet, graphite |
| Proterozoic marine sediments | Iron dissolved in oxygen-poor oceans precipitated when oxygen rose | Banded iron formations, the main world iron ore |
| Physical sedimentary (placers) | Dense, hard, chemically resistant minerals are concentrated by flowing water where it slows | Gold, tin (cassiterite) and diamonds in river and beach sands |
| Biological sedimentary | Organisms accumulate elements | Limestone from shells; phosphate deposits; coal from plants |

Evaporation of sea water also concentrates salts (evaporites: halite, gypsum, potash).

## 3.2.3.3 Reserves and resources; Lasky's principle

### What you need to know

- Definitions of resource and reserve.
- Lasky's principle: as ore grade falls, the amount of ore available rises roughly logarithmically.

### Explained

- **Resource**: the total amount of a mineral in the crust that could possibly be extracted, now or in future, including deposits not yet found or not yet economic.
- **Reserve**: the part of the resource that is known and can be extracted profitably with current technology and prices.

Reserves change: a higher price, better technology or new discoveries turn resources into reserves; falling prices or new regulations turn reserves back into resources.

**Lasky's principle**: for many metals, the total tonnage of ore increases geometrically as the minimum grade decreases. In practice, halving the cut-off grade might multiply available ore several times. So reserves can grow when lower grades become economic. But lower grade means more rock moved, more energy and more waste per tonne of metal.

### Worked example

An ore contains 0.5% copper. To produce 1 tonne of copper, 1 ÷ 0.005 = 200 tonnes of ore must be mined and processed (ignoring losses). At 0.25%, 400 tonnes are needed: double the waste and energy.

## 3.2.3.4 Exploration techniques

### What you need to know

Satellite imagery; seismic surveys; gravimetry; magnetometry; resistivity; trial drilling.

### Explained

| Technique | Principle | Detects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Satellite imagery | Different rocks and minerals reflect different wavelengths; lineaments show faults | Rock types, altered zones, fractures |
| Seismic surveys | Sound waves reflect from boundaries between layers of different density and velocity | Structure, layering, traps |
| Gravimetry | Dense ore bodies give a stronger gravitational field (positive anomaly); low-density rocks (salt) give a negative one | Dense metal ores, salt domes |
| Magnetometry | Magnetic minerals distort Earth's magnetic field | Magnetite, iron ores, some igneous bodies |
| Resistivity | Current passed into the ground; metal sulfides conduct well (low resistivity) | Sulfide ores, groundwater |
| Trial drilling | Cores taken and analysed | Confirms grade, depth and size |

Remote and geophysical methods are cheap per square kilometre and narrow down targets; drilling is expensive but is the only way to prove a reserve.

## 3.2.3.5 Mine viability

### What you need to know

Ore purity and cut-off grade; chemical form; overburden and hydrology; economics and mining costs.

### Explained

- **Grade and cut-off grade**: the cut-off grade is the minimum grade worth processing. It depends on metal price and costs.
- **Chemical form**: some compounds are easier to process than others (oxides are often easier to smelt than sulfides; some forms need expensive chemical treatment).
- **Overburden**: the rock above the ore. A high overburden-to-ore ratio raises the cost of open-pit mining.
- **Hydrology**: water flooding a mine must be pumped out, adding cost; it may also become polluted.
- **Economics**: metal price, transport distance, energy and labour costs, political stability, taxes and the cost of restoration.

## 3.2.3.6 Environmental impacts of mining and their control

### What you need to know

Turbid drainage water; spoil; leachate (and neutralisation); site management; restoration.

### Explained

| Impact | Control |
| --- | --- |
| Turbid (muddy) drainage water reduces light in rivers and smothers riverbed habitats | Settlement lagoons before discharge |
| Spoil heaps: unstable, ugly, may slide | Compacting, landscaping, vegetating; stable slopes |
| Leachate: acidic water carrying dissolved metals from spoil | Collection; neutralising with lime; wetlands |
| Noise, dust, traffic | Screening banks, damping roads, working hours |
| Habitat loss | Restoration: backfilling, topsoil replacement, planting, creating lakes and nature reserves |

Acid mine drainage is covered in [17](17-oil-pesticides-nutrients-and-metals.md).

## 3.2.3.7 Securing future supplies

### What you need to know

- Improved exploration including remote sensing; bioleaching with acidophilic bacteria; phytomining; Cradle to Cradle design.
- Advantages and difficulties of recycling.

### Explained

- **Bioleaching**: acid-loving bacteria oxidise sulfide minerals, releasing metal ions (for example copper) into a solution from which the metal is recovered. Works on low-grade ore and waste with little energy.
- **Phytomining**: plants (hyperaccumulators) grown on low-grade ore or contaminated land take up metals into their tissue; the plants are harvested and burned, and metal is extracted from the ash.
- **Cradle to Cradle design**: products are designed from the start so that at end of life all materials can be recovered and reused, not thrown away ("cradle to grave").

**Recycling**:

| Advantages | Difficulties |
| --- | --- |
| Conserves finite reserves | Identifying and separating materials in mixed products |
| Less energy than extracting from ore (aluminium recycling uses about 5% of the energy) | Quality loss (contamination of steel with copper; downcycling) |
| Less mining damage and waste | Collection and transport cost |
| Less landfill | Needs consumer cooperation |

### Common mistakes

- Using "reserve" and "resource" as if they mean the same thing.
- Saying recycling has no environmental cost. Collection and processing use energy.

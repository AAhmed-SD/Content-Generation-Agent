# Solid waste, noise, ionising radiation and new control technologies

Radioactive decay and half-life are covered in more physics depth in [A-level Physics 18](../../physics/18-radioactivity-decay-and-nuclear-radius.md).

## 3.4.3.2.12 Solid wastes

### What you need to know

- Domestic waste: landfill, incineration, recycling, composting; evaluate each.
- Specialist wastes: high-, intermediate- and low-level radioactive waste (sealed storage, monitoring, encapsulation, absorbers, cooling); asbestos (sealed storage); cyanide (incineration).

### Explained

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Landfill | Cheap; takes mixed waste; landfill gas (methane) can be captured for energy | Uses land; leachate can pollute groundwater; methane leaks; smell, pests; sites running out |
| Incineration | Reduces volume by about 90%; energy from waste; kills pathogens | Air pollutants (dioxins, particulates, CO₂); toxic ash; discourages recycling; high capital cost |
| Recycling | Saves resources and energy; less landfill | Needs sorting and collection; quality loss; markets fluctuate |
| Composting | Turns organic waste into soil improver; less methane than landfill | Needs separation; only organic waste; odours if badly managed |

The waste hierarchy (reduce, reuse, recycle, recover, dispose) ranks options; see [22](22-sustainability.md).

**Modern landfill design**: clay and plastic liners, leachate collection pipes and treatment, gas collection, daily cover with soil, capping and monitoring after closure.

**Radioactive waste**:

| Category | Source | Management |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High-level (HLW) | Spent fuel, reprocessing liquids; hot and highly radioactive | Cooled in water ponds for years; vitrified (made into glass); stored in steel containers; long-term plan is a deep geological repository |
| Intermediate-level (ILW) | Fuel cladding, reactor parts, resins | Encapsulated in cement in steel drums; stored; future deep disposal |
| Low-level (LLW) | Lightly contaminated clothing, tools, paper | Compacted and placed in near-surface engineered facilities |

Principles: sealed storage, absorbers (shielding with concrete, lead, water), cooling (for heat-producing waste), and continued monitoring.

**Asbestos**: fibres cause lung disease and cancer; wetted, double-bagged and sealed, buried in special landfill cells.

**Cyanide**: highly toxic; destroyed by high-temperature incineration (or chemical oxidation).

## 3.4.3.2.13 Noise

### What you need to know

- Principles of sound: hearing range, the logarithmic decibel scale, loudness, threshold of hearing.
- Effects on objects (acoustic fatigue, shock waves) and on organisms (hearing damage, stress, behaviour, wildlife disturbance and feeding).
- Sources and controls for aircraft, railways, road traffic, industry and domestic noise.

### Explained

Humans hear roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Sound intensity level is measured in decibels (dB), a logarithmic scale: dB = 10 log₁₀(I ÷ I₀), where I₀ is the threshold of hearing. An increase of 10 dB is a tenfold increase in intensity (and is heard as roughly twice as loud); +3 dB is a doubling of intensity. The dB(A) scale weights frequencies to match human hearing.

**Effects**: acoustic fatigue (repeated vibration cracks materials) and sonic booms (shock waves) can damage structures. In humans, prolonged noise above about 85 dB(A) damages hearing; noise also causes stress, sleep loss and poorer concentration. Wildlife: birds sing louder or at different times; noise masks predator or prey sounds; marine mammals are disturbed by ship noise and sonar; animals avoid noisy areas, losing feeding grounds.

| Source | Controls |
| --- | --- |
| Aircraft | Siting airports away from homes; insulating nearby homes; flight paths avoiding towns; minimum altitudes; night flight bans; quieter engine design (high-bypass turbofans) |
| Railways | Polishing track smooth; damping rails and wheels; fairings (shields) over wheels; lubricating curves; composite brake blocks that keep wheels smooth |
| Road traffic | Low-noise (porous) road surfaces; acoustic fences and earth banks; insulation of buildings; aerodynamic vehicle shapes |
| Industry | Ear protection; anti-vibration mats; limiting working times; quieter alternative processes |
| Domestic | Insulation between homes; volume limiters |

### Worked example

Two identical machines each produce 80 dB. Together the intensity doubles, so the level = 80 + 10 log₁₀2 = 80 + 3 = 83 dB, not 160 dB.

## 3.4.3.2.14 Ionising radiation

### What you need to know

- Uses: weapons, nuclear electricity, ship propulsion, manufacturing, healthcare, agriculture.
- Principles: half-life, Relative Biological Effectiveness (RBE), exposure versus contamination, activation products, becquerel, gray, sievert.
- Effects: free radicals, DNA damage, acute and chronic effects, somatic and genetic (gonadic) effects.
- Control: ALARA, BATNEEC, risk-benefit; closed sources, absorbers, inverse square law, reducing exposure time, monitoring workers.
- Environmental monitoring: CPA, sampling (dust, soil, water, seaweed, molluscs, fish, milk, vegetables, meat), CGM.

### Explained

**Uses**: nuclear weapons; nuclear power; nuclear-powered submarines and ships; manufacturing (thickness gauges, checking welds, sterilising equipment); healthcare (X-rays, radiotherapy, tracers, sterilising); agriculture (irradiating food to kill bacteria, sterile-male insect release, tracers in fertiliser studies).

**Units and terms**:

- Half-life: the time for half the radioactive nuclei (or activity) in a sample to decay.
- Becquerel (Bq): activity, one decay per second.
- Gray (Gy): absorbed dose, one joule absorbed per kilogram.
- Sievert (Sv): equivalent or effective dose = absorbed dose × RBE (radiation weighting). Alpha has a high RBE (about 20) because it ionises densely; beta and gamma have an RBE of 1.
- Exposure: radiation from a source outside the body. Contamination: radioactive material on or inside the body (inhaled, swallowed); alpha emitters are most dangerous inside the body.
- Activation products: substances made radioactive by absorbing neutrons, for example in reactor steel.

**Effects**: radiation ionises water in cells, forming free radicals that damage DNA. Acute effects follow a large dose quickly (radiation sickness, burns, death). Chronic effects follow lower doses over time (cancer, cataracts). Somatic effects harm the exposed person; genetic (gonadic) effects damage sperm or egg cells and may affect offspring.

**Control**:

- ALARA: keep doses As Low As Reasonably Achievable.
- BATNEEC: Best Available Techniques Not Entailing Excessive Cost.
- Risk-benefit: a use is justified only if benefits outweigh risks (medical scans).
- Practical protection: closed (sealed) sources; absorbers (lead, concrete, water shielding); distance (gamma intensity falls with the inverse square of distance); short exposure time; film badges or dosimeters to monitor workers.

**Environmental monitoring**: CPA identifies pathways from a site to people (for example discharges to sea concentrating in seaweed and shellfish; deposition onto grass, then into milk); samples of dust, soil, water, seaweed, molluscs, fish, milk, vegetables and meat are measured regularly; CGM focuses on the most exposed people.

### Worked example

A worker receives 2.0 mGy absorbed dose from gamma rays (RBE 1) and 0.1 mGy from alpha particles (RBE 20). Equivalent dose = 2.0 × 1 + 0.1 × 20 = 4.0 mSv.

Inverse square law: at 1 m a gamma dose rate is 40 µSv h⁻¹. At 4 m it is 40 ÷ 4² = 2.5 µSv h⁻¹.

## 3.4.3.3 New pollution control technologies

### What you need to know

Prevention; monitoring impacts; leachate collection; satellite monitoring of oil spills; GPS tracking of ships; heavy metal adsorption by polymers; phytoremediation; bioremediation of hydrocarbons.

### Explained

- Prevention: redesigning processes so pollutants are not made at all.
- Monitoring: continuous sensors and data loggers detect releases early.
- Leachate collection at landfill sites stops groundwater pollution.
- Satellites detect oil slicks (radar sees the calmer water surface) and identify illegal discharges; GPS (and automatic identification systems) tracks ships so polluters can be found.
- Polymer adsorbents bind heavy metal ions from water.
- Phytoremediation: plants take up metals or break down organic pollutants in soil.
- Bioremediation: bacteria break down hydrocarbons; adding nutrients and oxygen speeds it.

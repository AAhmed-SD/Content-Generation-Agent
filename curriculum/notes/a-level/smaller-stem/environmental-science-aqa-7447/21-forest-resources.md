# 3.5.3 Forest resources

Forests supply materials and services. Managing them for one product (usually timber) can reduce their other values. This file covers what forests provide, how plantation design affects productivity and biodiversity, and the causes, effects and sustainable alternatives to deforestation. The rainforest ecosystem is also in [04](04-habitat-conservation-and-key-ecosystems.md).

## 3.5.3.1 Resources and ecosystem services

### What you need to know

- Resources: timber, fuel, food, fibres, medicines.
- Services: atmospheric regulation, habitats, hydrological and climate regulation, soil conservation, recreation.

### Explained

| Resource | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Timber | Construction, furniture, paper pulp |
| Fuel | Firewood and charcoal (the main fuel for cooking for many people in low-income countries) |
| Food | Fruits, nuts, honey, bushmeat |
| Fibres | Rattan, bamboo, latex (rubber) |
| Medicines | Quinine (cinchona bark), many traditional and modern drugs |

Services:

- Atmospheric regulation: forests take in CO₂ and release O₂; they store large amounts of carbon.
- Habitat: forests hold most terrestrial species.
- Hydrological regulation: canopies intercept rain; roots and litter increase infiltration; this slows run-off, reducing floods and keeping rivers flowing in dry periods.
- Climate regulation: transpiration adds water vapour, producing rain further inland; evaporative cooling; low albedo.
- Soil conservation: roots bind soil; litter protects the surface and adds organic matter.
- Recreation and tourism.

## 3.5.3.2 Productivity and biodiversity of managed forests

### What you need to know

How productivity and biodiversity are affected by non-indigenous species, single-species plantations, close planting and simple age structure.

### Explained

Commercial plantations are designed for maximum timber output:

| Feature | Effect on productivity | Effect on biodiversity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Non-indigenous species (for example Sitka spruce in the UK, eucalyptus in the tropics) | Fast growth, high yield | Few native species are adapted to use them |
| Single species (monoculture) | Uniform, efficient harvesting | Few niches; pests and disease spread easily |
| Close planting | Trees grow tall and straight competing for light; more trees per hectare | Dark understorey with few plants; little ground flora |
| Simple age structure (all trees planted at once) | Whole stand harvested together (clear-felling) | No old trees, dead wood or variety of habitats |

So plantations have high timber productivity but low biodiversity compared with natural forest.

## 3.5.3.3 Deforestation and sustainable forestry

### What you need to know

- Causes: harvesting faster than the maximum sustainable yield; clearance for other land uses.
- Effects on resources, biodiversity, hydrology (less interception and transpiration, more run-off), soil and climate (albedo, carbon, rainfall downwind).
- Sustainable management: mixed species, indigenous species, mixed ages, selective logging.

### Explained

**Causes**: logging faster than trees regrow; clearance for cattle ranching, soya, oil palm, mining, roads, settlements and reservoirs; fuelwood collection around towns.

**Effects**:

- Resources: loss of future timber, fuel, food and medicines.
- Biodiversity: habitat loss and fragmentation; extinction of endemic species.
- Hydrology: less interception and transpiration means more rain reaches the ground quickly and runs off, causing floods, erosion and lower dry-season flow.
- Soil: erosion; nutrient loss (especially in rainforests, where most nutrients are in the biomass); laterisation (hard crusts) in some tropical soils.
- Climate: burning and decay release stored carbon as CO₂; higher albedo of cleared land slightly changes local energy balance; less transpiration means less rain downwind (much rain in the western Amazon comes from moisture recycled by forests further east).

**Sustainable forestry**:

- Mixed species: more habitats and less vulnerable to pests.
- Indigenous species: support native wildlife.
- Mixed ages (continuous cover): some trees always standing, varied habitat, no bare soil.
- Selective logging: only some mature trees removed, leaving the forest structure and canopy. Damage is reduced by directional felling, planned extraction routes and removing climbers first.
- Harvesting at or below the maximum sustainable yield (annual growth increment).
- Certification schemes let buyers choose timber from well-managed forests.

### Worked example

A forest has a standing volume of 240 m³ ha⁻¹ and grows by 6 m³ ha⁻¹ per year. Sustainable annual harvest ≈ 6 m³ ha⁻¹. With a 30-year cycle, selective logging could remove 30 × 6 = 180 m³ ha⁻¹ per cycle at most, about 75% of the standing volume, but leaving more improves biodiversity and regrowth.

### Common mistakes

- Saying deforestation increases rainfall locally. It reduces transpiration and so rainfall downwind.
- Saying plantations are as good as natural forest because tree cover is the same.

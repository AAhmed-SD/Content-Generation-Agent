# Monitoring, ecological principles, succession and populations

Good conservation planning needs data on what is present and how populations are changing, and an understanding of how organisms relate to their habitat. Background on succession and populations is in [A-level Biology 23](../../biology/23-populations-in-ecosystems.md). Field sampling methods are in [23](23-research-methods-and-fieldwork.md).

## 3.1.2.3.5 Ecological monitoring in conservation planning

### What you need to know

Monitoring finds out: which species are present; population size; distribution; survival rates; age structure.

### Explained

- **Species present**: a baseline survey lists species so changes can be detected and priorities chosen.
- **Population size**: shows whether a species is increasing or decreasing; methods include counts, quadrats and mark-release-recapture.
- **Distribution**: maps where a species occurs, to locate key sites and corridors.
- **Survival rates**: the proportion of individuals surviving a year, or of released animals surviving, shows whether a population can sustain itself.
- **Age structure**: a population with many young is growing; one with mostly old individuals and few young is not recruiting and will decline.

Repeating surveys with the same method at the same time of year makes results comparable.

## 3.1.2.3.6 New monitoring technologies

### What you need to know

Satellite and radio tracking; DNA databases and environmental DNA (eDNA); image recognition; acoustic monitoring and sonograms.

### Explained

| Technology | How it works | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Radio tracking | Animal wears a transmitter; receiver locates it by signal direction | Home range, survival of released animals |
| Satellite tracking | Tag sends GPS positions via satellite | Long-distance migration (turtles, albatrosses, whales) |
| DNA databases | Reference DNA sequences for species and individuals | Identify species from fragments, track illegal trade (for example ivory source) |
| eDNA | Organisms shed DNA into water or soil; a sample is filtered and sequenced | Detect rare or hidden species (great crested newts in ponds) without catching them |
| Image recognition | Software identifies species or individuals from camera-trap photos (stripe, spot or fin patterns) | Count large numbers of images quickly |
| Acoustic monitoring and sonograms | Recorders log sounds; a sonogram plots frequency against time | Identify bats, birds, whales, frogs by call |

Advantages: less disturbance, larger areas and longer periods covered, fewer skilled field workers needed. Limits: cost, battery life, tag effects on animals, eDNA cannot give population size or show whether the animal is alive nearby now.

## 3.1.3.1 Adaptation and habitat requirements

### What you need to know

Organisms are adapted to abiotic factors (light, water, nutrients, pH, physical habitat) and biotic factors (food, control by predators, pollination, seed dispersal, biotic habitat such as trees for nesting, and other interspecies relationships).

### Explained

A conservation plan must provide everything a species needs. For example, a bat needs insects (food), roosts (often in old trees or buildings), hedgerows to navigate along, and unpolluted water bodies for insects. Losing any one factor can cause decline. Interspecies relationships include mutualism (pollinator and flower), parasitism, predation and competition.

## 3.1.3.2 Ecological terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Species | A group of organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring |
| Taxon | Any named group in classification (species, genus, family ...) |
| Ecological niche | The role of a species: how it uses resources and interacts with abiotic and biotic factors |
| Population | All individuals of one species in an area at one time |
| Community | All populations of all species in an area |
| Ecosystem | A community and its abiotic environment, interacting |
| Biome | A large-scale ecosystem type defined by climate and vegetation (tundra, desert, tropical rainforest) |

Common mistake: saying a niche is where an organism lives. That is its habitat.

## 3.1.3.3 Ecological succession and plagioclimax

### What you need to know

- Succession: colonisation by pioneer species, a series of seral stages (seres), modification of abiotic conditions, ending in a climax community.
- Deflected succession and plagioclimax, maintained by grazing, mowing, burning, coppicing or pollarding.
- Secondary succession.

### Explained

**Primary succession** starts on bare ground with no soil (new volcanic rock, sand dunes, retreating glaciers).

1. Pioneer species (lichens, mosses, marram grass on dunes) tolerate harsh conditions.
2. They change abiotic conditions: weathering rock, adding organic matter when they die, so soil forms and holds water and nutrients.
3. New species colonise and outcompete the pioneers. Each stage is a sere.
4. Biodiversity, biomass and soil depth generally increase.
5. A climax community forms, stable under the local climate (in lowland Britain, usually deciduous woodland).

**Secondary succession** happens where vegetation is removed but soil remains (after fire or ploughing). It is faster because soil and seeds are present.

**Deflected succession** happens when a human activity stops succession reaching climax, holding it at a **plagioclimax**. Many valued British habitats are plagioclimaxes:

- grazing keeps chalk grassland free of scrub
- mowing for hay keeps meadows
- burning keeps heather moorland
- coppicing (cutting trees to ground level on a cycle) and pollarding (cutting above browsing height) keep a mosaic of light and shade in woodland.

Conservation of these habitats needs the management to continue.

## 3.1.3.4 Population control

### What you need to know

- Carrying capacity; density-dependent and density-independent factors.
- Conservation uses release programmes, habitat management, culling and eradication.
- r-selected and K-selected species.

### Explained

**Carrying capacity (K)** is the largest population an environment can support long-term. A population grows quickly at first, then slows as resources become limiting, and levels off near K (a sigmoid curve).

- **Density-dependent factors** have a stronger effect as density increases: competition for food, predation, disease and parasitism. They regulate populations around K.
- **Density-independent factors** affect a population regardless of its density: floods, drought, frost, fire, pollution events.

**Management**: release programmes raise numbers of rare species; habitat management raises K (for example more nest sites); culling reduces overabundant species (deer that prevent woodland regeneration); eradication removes invasive species (rats on islands).

| r-selected species | K-selected species |
| --- | --- |
| Many small offspring, little parental care | Few large offspring, much parental care |
| Short life, early maturity | Long life, late maturity |
| Populations fluctuate; colonise disturbed habitats | Populations stable near K |
| Examples: many insects, weeds | Examples: elephants, great apes, whales |

K-selected species are slower to recover from loss, so they are more vulnerable to exploitation.

### Worked example

A deer population of 400 lives in a wood with a carrying capacity of 250. To bring the population to K, 150 must be removed (37.5%). If the population then grows by 20% per year without culling, the next year it would be 300, so about 50 must be culled each year to hold it at 250.

### Common mistakes

- Calling weather a density-dependent factor.
- Saying climax communities never change. They change slowly with climate.

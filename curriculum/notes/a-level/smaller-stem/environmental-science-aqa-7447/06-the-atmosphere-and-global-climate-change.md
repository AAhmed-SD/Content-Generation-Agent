# The atmosphere and global climate change

The atmosphere controls how solar energy reaches and leaves the Earth. Human activity is changing its composition and so the climate. Background biology of climate change is in [A-level Biology 42](../../biology/42-climate-change-and-human-impact.md); feedback loops are revisited in [22 Sustainability](22-sustainability.md).

## 3.2.1.1 UV, visible light and IR in the atmosphere

### What you need to know

- The origins and roles of UV and IR: insolation from the Sun, IR emitted by the Earth, thermal stratification of the atmosphere, chemical processes.
- How each wavelength is transmitted, absorbed and converted to heat or chemical energy, or reflected.

### Explained

The Sun is very hot, so it emits mainly short wavelengths: UV, visible and near IR. The Earth is much cooler, so it emits long-wave IR.

| Radiation | Source | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UV | Sun | UV-C and most UV-B absorbed by oxygen and ozone in the stratosphere; the energy breaks bonds (chemical energy) and heats the stratosphere |
| Visible | Sun | Mostly transmitted through a clear atmosphere; some reflected by cloud, ice and pale surfaces; the rest absorbed by the surface and converted to heat; used in photosynthesis (chemical energy) |
| IR (short-wave) | Sun | Partly absorbed by water vapour and CO₂; rest reaches the surface |
| IR (long-wave) | Earth's surface | Absorbed by greenhouse gases in the troposphere, which re-emit it in all directions, some back down: the greenhouse effect |

**Thermal stratification**: the troposphere is heated from below by the warm surface, so it cools with height. The stratosphere is heated from within by ozone absorbing UV, so it warms with height. The warm layer over a cooler one is stable, so the stratosphere hardly mixes with the troposphere. This is why pollutants that reach it (like CFCs) stay for decades.

### Common mistakes

- Saying greenhouse gases trap incoming sunlight. They absorb outgoing long-wave IR.
- Saying the hole in the ozone layer causes global warming. They are different problems with some links.

## 3.2.1.2 Global climate change

### What you need to know

- Greenhouse gases: CO₂, CH₄, oxides of nitrogen (mainly N₂O), tropospheric ozone and CFCs; their sources.
- Effects on interconnected systems: thermohaline circulation, El Niño, sea-level rise; the cryosphere (snow, glaciers, ice sheets, ice shelves, sea ice); precipitation and wind.
- Why monitoring and predicting is difficult: data gaps, reliability of proxies, remote sensing, climate models, natural fluctuations, time lags.
- Uncertainty about ecological impacts.
- Negative feedback (low cloud, photosynthesis) and positive feedback (permafrost melt, acidification, albedo, methane hydrates, fires, cirrus cloud, soil decomposition); tipping points.
- Carbon footprints and sustainable development.

### Explained

**Greenhouse gases**:

| Gas | Main human sources | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CO₂ | Burning fossil fuels, deforestation, cement making | Largest total effect |
| CH₄ | Rice paddies, cattle, landfill, gas leaks, coal mines | Much stronger per molecule than CO₂ but shorter-lived |
| N₂O | Fertilised soils, combustion | Long-lived |
| Tropospheric ozone | Formed from NOx and hydrocarbons in sunlight | Also a pollutant (smog) |
| CFCs | Old refrigerants, aerosols, foam | Very strong per molecule; also destroy stratospheric ozone |

**Ocean effects**:

- Thermohaline circulation is driven by density differences: cold, salty water sinks in the North Atlantic. Melting ice adds fresh water, lowering density, which could slow the circulation and cool north-west Europe (see [08](08-the-hydrosphere.md)).
- El Niño: a periodic weakening of Pacific trade winds that lets warm water spread east, changing rainfall worldwide. Warming may change its frequency or strength.
- Sea-level rise: thermal expansion of warming water, plus melting land ice (glaciers and ice sheets). Melting sea ice and floating ice shelves do not raise sea level directly, but their loss lets land ice flow faster into the sea.

**Cryosphere**: less snow cover and sea ice lower albedo; glaciers retreat, threatening dry-season water supply; ice sheets in Greenland and Antarctica lose mass; ice shelves break up.

**Precipitation and wind**: warmer air holds more water vapour, so heavier rainfall events; patterns shift, with some regions wetter and others drier; storm tracks may move.

**Why monitoring and prediction are difficult**:

- Data gaps: few long records from oceans, poles and poorer countries.
- Proxy reliability: tree rings, ice cores and sediments need calibration and have uncertainty.
- Remote sensing: satellites only since the 1970s; instruments drift and change.
- Models: the climate system is complex; clouds and feedbacks are hard to represent; models depend on future emissions, which depend on human choices.
- Natural fluctuations: volcanic eruptions, solar cycles, El Niño hide the trend in short records.
- Time lags: oceans warm slowly, so effects continue long after emissions.

**Ecological uncertainty**: species may adapt, migrate or decline; interactions (for example a predator and prey responding at different rates) are hard to predict.

**Feedback**:

- Negative (damps change): warming increases evaporation and low cloud, which reflects sunlight; higher CO₂ can increase photosynthesis, removing CO₂.
- Positive (amplifies change): permafrost melting releases CH₄ and CO₂; warmer, more acidic oceans absorb less CO₂; melting ice lowers albedo so more heat is absorbed; methane hydrates on the sea floor may release CH₄; more forest and peat fires release CO₂; more high cirrus cloud traps IR; faster soil decomposition releases CO₂.
- Tipping point: a threshold beyond which a positive feedback becomes self-sustaining and the system moves to a new state even if the original cause stops.

**Carbon footprint**: the total greenhouse gas emissions caused by a person, product or organisation, expressed as CO₂ equivalent. Sustainable development means meeting present needs without stopping future generations meeting theirs; reducing footprints (energy efficiency, renewables, less meat, less flying) is part of it.

### Worked example

An albedo of 0.8 for fresh sea ice and 0.06 for open ocean: with 200 W m⁻² of insolation, ice absorbs 200 × 0.2 = 40 W m⁻² and ocean absorbs 200 × 0.94 = 188 W m⁻². Replacing ice with ocean increases absorption by 148 W m⁻², warming further: a positive feedback.

### Common mistakes

- Saying melting sea ice raises sea level.
- Describing a feedback without saying whether it amplifies or reduces the original change.

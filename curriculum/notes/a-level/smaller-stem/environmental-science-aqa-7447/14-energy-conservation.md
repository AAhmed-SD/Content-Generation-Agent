# 3.3.4.2 Energy conservation

The cheapest and cleanest energy is energy not used. Conservation means doing the same job with less energy (efficiency) or avoiding waste. This file covers transport, buildings and industry, including the electricity grid.

## 3.3.4.2.1 Transport

### What you need to know

- Vehicle design: aerodynamic and hydrodynamic shape, low mass, tyre design, kinetic energy recovery (KERS, regenerative braking), low embodied-energy materials, bulk transport.
- Infrastructure and management: integrated road, rail and cycle networks; Active Traffic Management and smart motorways.
- End-of-life design: recyclable materials, easy identification, easy dismantling.

### Explained

**Vehicle design**:

- Aerodynamics: smooth shapes reduce air resistance. Drag force rises with the square of speed, so the effect is greatest at high speed. Ships use hull shapes (hydrodynamics) and coatings to reduce water drag.
- Low mass: aluminium, composites and high-strength steel reduce the energy needed to accelerate and climb.
- Tyres: low rolling-resistance tyres and correct inflation reduce energy lost as heat.
- KERS and regenerative braking: instead of turning kinetic energy into heat in the brakes, a motor acts as a generator and charges a battery (or spins a flywheel); the energy is reused for acceleration.
- Low embodied-energy materials reduce energy used before the vehicle is driven.
- Bulk transport: one train, bus or ship carries many passengers or tonnes, so energy per passenger-km or tonne-km is much lower than for cars or lorries.

**Infrastructure**: integrated networks let people combine cycling, buses and trains (park-and-ride, cycle lanes, through ticketing). Active Traffic Management on smart motorways uses variable speed limits and opens the hard shoulder at busy times, smoothing flow so less stop-start driving and fuel waste.

**End of life**: designing vehicles so parts are easy to take apart, labelled by material, and made of recyclable materials, so materials (and their embodied energy) are recovered.

## 3.3.4.2.2 Buildings

### What you need to know

- Design: passive solar orientation, low surface-area-to-volume ratio, high thermal mass.
- Low embodied-energy materials: rammed earth, limecrete, straw, earth-sheltered buildings.
- Low thermal transmittance (U-value): double and triple glazing, low-emissivity glass, vacuum or inert-gas glazing, wall, floor and roof insulation.
- Energy management: occupancy sensors, insulation, automatic or solar-powered ventilation, heat exchangers.
- Low-energy appliances: CFL and LED lighting, low-energy white goods.

### Explained

**Design**:

- Passive solar: large windows facing the Sun (south in the UK) let in solar heat in winter; overhangs shade them from the high summer Sun.
- Low surface-area-to-volume ratio: compact shapes (and terraced houses or flats sharing walls) lose less heat.
- High thermal mass: dense materials (stone, concrete, earth) absorb heat by day and release it at night, smoothing temperature swings.

**Low embodied-energy materials**: rammed earth (compacted soil); limecrete (lime-based concrete instead of cement concrete, which needs high-temperature kilns); straw bales (good insulation, renewable); earth-sheltered buildings (partly underground, so soil insulates and adds thermal mass).

**Thermal transmittance**: the U-value is the rate of heat loss through 1 m² of a structure for a 1 °C temperature difference (W m⁻² K⁻¹). Lower is better.

- Double and triple glazing trap layers of gas, which conduct heat poorly.
- Low-emissivity (low-e) glass has a thin metal-oxide coating that reflects long-wave IR back into the room.
- Vacuum glazing removes the gas completely; inert-gas glazing uses argon or krypton, which conduct heat less than air.
- Cavity wall, loft and floor insulation trap air in fibres or foams.

Heat loss rate = U × A × ΔT.

### Worked example

A window of area 2.0 m² has a U-value of 4.8 W m⁻² K⁻¹ (single glazed). Inside is 20 °C and outside 5 °C. Heat loss = 4.8 × 2.0 × 15 = 144 W. Replacing it with triple glazing (U = 0.8) gives 0.8 × 2.0 × 15 = 24 W, a saving of 120 W (83%).

**Energy management**: occupancy sensors turn off lights and heating in empty rooms; automatic ventilation opens only as needed; solar-powered vents; heat exchangers (mechanical ventilation with heat recovery) warm incoming fresh air using outgoing stale air.

**Appliances**: LEDs use far less electricity than filament lamps for the same light; compact fluorescent lamps (CFLs) were an earlier step; A-rated fridges, washing machines and dishwashers use less energy.

## 3.3.4.2.3 Industry and electricity supply

### What you need to know

- Heat management: storing bulk hot fluids, heat exchangers, combined heat and power (CHP).
- Electricity infrastructure: high-voltage grid, peak shaving and pumped storage, ICT to coordinate supply and demand, choosing locations for new generation.

### Explained

**Heat management**: waste heat from one process is captured by heat exchangers and used to preheat inputs or warm buildings; hot fluids are stored in insulated tanks for later use. **CHP**: a power station's waste heat (which in a conventional station is about half the fuel energy, lost in cooling towers) is piped to nearby buildings or industry. Overall efficiency can rise from about 35 to 40% to 75 to 80%.

**Electricity infrastructure**:

- The grid transmits at very high voltage (for example 400 kV), so current is low and resistive losses (P = I²R) are small.
- Peak shaving and pumped storage meet short peaks without running inefficient extra stations.
- Smart grids use ICT to balance supply and demand: smart meters, time-of-use pricing, and switching flexible loads (fridges, car charging) to times of surplus.
- New generation sited near demand reduces transmission losses; CHP needs heat users nearby.

### Common mistakes

- Saying insulation stops heat loss completely. It reduces the rate.
- Confusing U-value (heat loss rate, lower is better) with R-value (resistance, higher is better).

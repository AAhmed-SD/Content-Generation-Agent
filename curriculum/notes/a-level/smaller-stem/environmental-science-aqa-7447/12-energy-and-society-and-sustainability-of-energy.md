# Energy and society, features of energy resources and sustainability

Cheap, plentiful energy shaped modern society. This file covers why energy matters, the features that decide how useful a resource is, and the environmental costs of the way we use energy now. AQA expects quantitative comparison between resources.

## 3.3.1 Energy and the development of society

### What you need to know

How energy supplies affected agriculture (mechanisation), fishing, industry, service industries, water supply and treatment, transport and domestic living standards.

### Explained

- **Agriculture**: tractors, combine harvesters and irrigation pumps replaced human and animal labour, so a small workforce feeds many. Energy also makes fertiliser (Haber process) and pesticides.
- **Fishing**: powered boats travel further and haul larger nets; refrigeration keeps catches fresh, increasing catches (and overfishing).
- **Industry**: steam, then electricity, allowed factories, mass production and smelting of metals.
- **Service industries**: offices, computing, hospitals and communications all need reliable electricity.
- **Water supply and treatment**: pumping, treatment and sewage disposal improved public health dramatically.
- **Transport**: railways, cars, ships and aircraft let people and goods move cheaply, enabling trade and cities.
- **Domestic living**: heating, lighting, refrigeration, washing machines and cooking appliances raised living standards and freed time.

## 3.3.2 Features of energy resources

### What you need to know

Abundance; energy density; locational constraints; intermittency; the need to convert to a form usable at the point of use.

### Explained

| Feature | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Abundance | How much is available | Solar input vastly exceeds human use; fossil fuels finite |
| Energy density | Energy per unit mass or volume | Uranium very high; oil and coal high; wood lower; wind and solar low per unit area |
| Locational constraints | Where it can be exploited | Tidal power needs a large tidal range; geothermal needs hot rocks; HEP needs height and rainfall |
| Intermittency | Whether supply varies uncontrollably | Wind and solar vary with weather and time; fossil and nuclear are available on demand |
| Conversion | Whether it must be changed into another form for use | Most primary sources are converted to electricity or fuels; each conversion loses energy |

High energy density makes fuels easy to store and transport, which is why oil dominates transport. Low-density, intermittent sources need large areas and storage.

### Worked example

Petrol has an energy density of about 46 MJ kg⁻¹; a lithium-ion battery about 0.6 MJ kg⁻¹. Mass of battery storing the same energy as 40 kg of petrol: 40 × 46 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 3070 kg. In practice electric motors are about three times more efficient than petrol engines, so the useful energy gap is smaller (about 1000 kg), but batteries remain much heavier.

## 3.3.3 Sustainability of current energy use

### What you need to know

- Impacts before use: extracting fuel, processing it, making equipment, developing and operating sites, transport, embodied energy.
- Impacts from use: air pollution from fossil fuels, oil pollution, radioactive waste, noise from wind turbines, thermal pollution from steam turbines.
- Habitat damage: from extraction, siting of stations and equipment, tidal and HEP schemes, pipelines and cables.
- Depletion of non-renewable reserves.

### Explained

**Before use**:

- Extraction: open-cast coal mines, oil wells, uranium mines disturb land and produce waste.
- Processing: refining oil, enriching uranium and making fuel rods use energy and create pollutants.
- Equipment manufacture: wind turbines need steel, concrete and rare-earth magnets; solar panels need silicon purified at high temperature.
- Site development and operation: roads, clearing land, construction traffic.
- Transport of fuel: tankers, pipelines and trains use energy and risk spills.
- **Embodied energy**: all the energy used to make, transport and build something. A renewable device must generate more than its embodied energy to give a net gain. Energy payback time = embodied energy ÷ annual output.

**From use**:

- Fossil fuel combustion releases CO₂, SO₂, NOx, particulates and CO.
- Oil spills harm marine life.
- Nuclear power produces radioactive waste that must be isolated for thousands of years.
- Wind turbines make noise and can kill birds and bats.
- Thermal power stations release warm cooling water (thermal pollution), lowering dissolved oxygen in rivers.

**Habitat damage**: from mines and wells; from building large stations; tidal barrages change estuary mudflats and block fish movement; HEP dams flood valleys and block fish migration; pipelines and cables cut through habitats.

**Depletion**: fossil fuels and uranium are finite. Reserves-to-production ratio (years left at current use) = reserves ÷ annual production; it changes as new reserves are found and use changes.

### Worked example

A wind turbine has embodied energy of 3.0 TJ and produces 6.0 GWh per year. 6.0 GWh = 6.0 × 10⁹ × 3600 J = 2.16 × 10¹³ J = 21.6 TJ. Payback time = 3.0 ÷ 21.6 ≈ 0.14 years, about 7 weeks.

### Common mistakes

- Saying renewable energy has no environmental impact. Manufacture, siting and habitat effects still matter.
- Mixing up energy (J, kWh) and power (W).

## Check questions

1. Explain how mechanisation changed agricultural productivity and employment. (2)
2. Define energy density and explain why it matters for transport fuels. (3)
3. Explain why intermittency is a problem for a national grid relying on solar power. (2)
4. A solar farm has embodied energy of 45 TJ and an annual output of 25 GWh. Calculate its energy payback time in years. (3)
5. Describe two environmental impacts that occur before an oil-fired power station burns any fuel. (2)
6. Explain how thermal pollution from a power station can affect a river ecosystem. (3)
7. Compare the sustainability of coal-fired and wind-powered electricity. (6)

## Mark scheme

1. Machines replace human / animal labour so more output per worker (1); fewer people employed in farming / larger farms possible (1).
2. Energy stored per unit mass or volume (1); high-density fuels mean less mass / volume carried (1); longer range / less energy used moving the fuel (1).
3. Output varies with weather / time of day / none at night (1); supply may not match demand so backup or storage needed (1).
4. 25 GWh = 25 × 10⁹ × 3600 = 9.0 × 10¹³ J = 90 TJ (1); 45 ÷ 90 (1) = 0.5 years (1).
5. Any two: extraction disturbs habitat / spills; refining emissions; tanker transport / spill risk; manufacture of equipment; site construction.
6. Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen (1); increases respiration rate of organisms so more oxygen demand (1); sensitive species die / change in community / algal growth (1).
7. Level-marked, up to 6: coal finite, CO₂, SO₂, particulates, mining damage (1–2); coal reliable / dispatchable (1); wind renewable, low operating emissions, short payback (1); wind intermittent, needs storage, visual / noise / bird impacts (1); land use, embodied energy (1); judgement (1).

# Air pollutants and thermal pollution

For each pollutant, know its sources, its impacts and the technologies that control it. Controls work in three ways: reducing production, reducing release, or reducing the damage done.

## 3.4.3.2.1 Smoke and PM10

### What you need to know

- Sources; impacts on breathing, albedo and smog formation with temperature inversions.
- Controls: Clean Air Act 1956, treating coal, electrostatic precipitators, cyclones, bag filters.

### Explained

PM10 means particles smaller than 10 micrometres; they reach deep into the lungs. Sources: burning coal and wood, diesel engines, industry, construction dust.

Impacts: respiratory and heart disease; soot darkens buildings; dark particles on snow and ice lower albedo, increasing melting; particles act as nuclei for fog droplets, and during inversions form dense smog (the London smog of December 1952 killed thousands).

| Control | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Clean Air Act 1956 | Smoke control zones; only smokeless fuels allowed; taller chimneys for industry |
| Coal treatment | Smokeless fuel made by heating coal to remove volatile tar-forming compounds |
| Electrostatic precipitator | Particles in flue gas are given a negative charge and attracted to positive plates; plates are shaken to remove dust |
| Cyclone | Gas spun in a cone; heavier particles thrown to the wall and fall out |
| Bag filter | Gas passes through fabric bags that trap particles |

## 3.4.3.2.2 Acid precipitation

### What you need to know

- Sources of SOx, NOx and ozone.
- Impacts on buildings, organisms (lakes, forests) and humans.
- Controls: fuel desulfurisation, wet and dry flue-gas desulfurisation, catalytic converters, urea sprays.

### Explained

Burning fossil fuels containing sulfur makes SO₂. High-temperature combustion makes NOx. In the atmosphere, oxidation (helped by ozone) forms sulfuric and nitric acids, which fall as acid rain or are deposited dry.

**Impacts**:

- Buildings: limestone and marble (calcium carbonate) dissolve; metals corrode.
- Lakes: low pH kills fish eggs and invertebrates; aluminium is released from soils and damages fish gills.
- Forests: leaching of calcium and magnesium from soil; aluminium toxicity to roots; damage to needles.
- Humans: SO₂ and acid aerosols irritate airways.

**Controls**:

- Fuel desulfurisation: removing sulfur from oil and gas before burning.
- Wet flue-gas desulfurisation: flue gas sprayed with a slurry of crushed limestone; SO₂ reacts to form calcium sulfate (gypsum), which can be sold for plasterboard.
- Dry flue-gas desulfurisation: powdered lime injected into the gas.
- Catalytic converters on vehicles reduce NOx to N₂.
- Urea (or ammonia) sprays into flue gases convert NOx to N₂ and water.
- Liming lakes reduces damage, but treats the symptom.

## 3.4.3.2.3 Oxides of nitrogen

### What you need to know

Sources (high-temperature combustion, fertilisers); photochemical smog and climate change; controls (catalytic converters, urea sprays, fertiliser management).

### Explained

NOx forms when N₂ and O₂ combine at high temperature in engines and power stations; fertilised soils release N₂O. NO₂ splits in sunlight, releasing oxygen atoms that form tropospheric ozone; with hydrocarbons this gives photochemical smog (brown haze, eye and lung irritation, crop damage), worst on sunny, still days in cities. N₂O is a long-lived greenhouse gas.

Controls: catalytic converters; urea sprays; lower combustion temperatures; precise fertiliser use.

## 3.4.3.2.4 Hydrocarbons

### What you need to know

Sources (unburnt fuel, fossil fuel extraction, solvents, aerosols); greenhouse effect (methane) and photochemical smog; controls (catalytic converters, efficient combustion, vapour collection and incineration, activated carbon).

### Explained

Volatile hydrocarbons evaporate from fuel tanks and filling stations, escape from oil and gas wells and pipelines, and come from paints, solvents and aerosols. Unburnt fuel leaves exhausts. Methane is a greenhouse gas; other hydrocarbons react with NOx in sunlight to make ozone and smog.

Controls: catalytic converters oxidise hydrocarbons to CO₂ and water; efficient combustion; vapour recovery nozzles at filling stations; collecting and burning (flaring or incinerating) vapours at industrial sites; activated carbon filters adsorb vapours.

## 3.4.3.2.5 Carbon monoxide

### What you need to know

Source (incomplete combustion); effect (binds to haemoglobin); controls (catalytic converters, efficient combustion).

### Explained

CO forms when carbon fuels burn with too little oxygen, for example in cold engines, faulty boilers and stoves. CO binds to haemoglobin far more strongly than oxygen (forming carboxyhaemoglobin), so blood carries less oxygen, causing headaches, unconsciousness and death. Controls: catalytic converters oxidise CO to CO₂; good air supply and maintenance of boilers; CO alarms indoors.

**Catalytic converter**: a honeycomb coated with platinum, palladium and rhodium. A three-way converter does three jobs at once: oxidises CO to CO₂, oxidises hydrocarbons to CO₂ and water, and reduces NOx to N₂. It works only once hot, and does not reduce CO₂.

## 3.4.3.2.6 Thermal pollution

### What you need to know

Effect of temperature on dissolved oxygen; cooling towers as a control.

### Explained

Thermal power stations and some industries use river or sea water for cooling and return it warmer. Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen, while warmer animals respire faster and need more. Fish and invertebrates that need cool, oxygen-rich water (trout, mayfly larvae) decline; algae may increase. Sudden temperature changes when stations start or stop can kill organisms.

**Cooling towers** transfer waste heat to the air (mainly by evaporation) before water is returned, so the river temperature rises much less.

### Common mistakes

- Saying catalytic converters reduce CO₂. They produce CO₂ from CO and hydrocarbons.
- Saying acid rain is caused by CO₂. The main causes are SO₂ and NOx.

# 3.3.4.1 Securing future energy supplies

Future supply depends on getting more from existing resources, developing new technologies, and matching supply to demand with storage. For each resource, know how the technology works, its advantages and its problems.

## Improved technologies by resource

### What you need to know

- Fossil fuels: secondary and tertiary oil recovery, directional drilling, oil shale and tar sands, CCS, hydraulic fracturing (fracking), coal gasification and liquefaction, methane hydrates.
- Nuclear: fission (better uranium extraction, plutonium and thorium reactors); fusion (toroidal and laser).
- Solar: photothermal, heat pumps, photovoltaic, multi-junction cells, anti-reflective surfaces, concentrating solar power.
- HEP: low-head and helical turbines.
- Wind: new horizontal-axis and vertical-axis turbines; wind-assisted ships.
- Wave power.
- Biofuels: crops, hydrogen from algae, microorganisms.
- Geothermal: low-temperature fluids, district heating.
- Tidal: barrages, lagoons, in-stream turbines.

### Explained

**Fossil fuels**

- Primary recovery uses the natural pressure in the reservoir, getting about 10 to 20% of the oil. Secondary recovery pumps water or gas in to push more oil out. Tertiary (enhanced) recovery lowers viscosity, for example by injecting steam, CO₂ or detergents. Together these can raise recovery towards 50% or more.
- Directional drilling: the well is steered at an angle or horizontally, so one platform reaches many parts of a field, or a well runs along a thin reservoir layer.
- Oil shale and tar sands: rocks with solid or very viscous hydrocarbons. They are mined or heated in place; this needs a lot of energy and water and causes major habitat damage.
- CCS: capture CO₂ from flue gases and store it in porous rocks (see [10](10-biogeochemical-cycles.md)).
- Fracking: water with sand and chemicals is pumped at high pressure into shale to open fractures; sand keeps them open so gas flows to a horizontal well. Concerns: water use, contamination, small earthquakes, methane leaks.
- Coal gasification turns coal into a gas fuel (carbon monoxide and hydrogen), cleaner to burn and easier to capture CO₂ from; liquefaction makes liquid fuels from coal.
- Methane hydrates: methane trapped in ice crystals on continental slopes and in permafrost; a huge resource but hard and risky to extract.

**Nuclear**

- Fission: uranium-235 nuclei split, releasing heat. Improved extraction (including from seawater) increases resources. Fast breeder reactors make plutonium-239 from the common uranium-238, extending fuel many times. Thorium reactors use thorium-232, which is more abundant and produces less long-lived waste.
- Fusion: light nuclei (deuterium and tritium) join, as in the Sun. Toroidal reactors (tokamaks) hold a hot plasma in a doughnut-shaped magnetic field; laser fusion compresses fuel pellets with powerful lasers. Fuel is abundant and waste is less long-lived, but a power station that gives more energy than it uses has not yet been built.

**Solar**

- Photothermal (solar thermal): panels heat water directly.
- Heat pumps move heat from the air, ground or water into a building, using electricity; they give out about three to four times more heat than electricity used.
- Photovoltaic (PV): semiconductor cells convert light to electricity. Multi-junction cells stack layers that absorb different wavelengths, so efficiency is higher. Anti-reflective coatings reduce light lost by reflection.
- Concentrating solar power: mirrors focus sunlight to heat a fluid that drives a turbine; molten-salt storage lets it run after dark.

**HEP**: low-head turbines generate from small height differences (weirs, rivers) without large dams; helical turbines work in flowing water at low speed.

**Wind**: larger horizontal-axis turbines (HAWT) with longer blades capture more energy (power is proportional to swept area and to wind speed cubed); floating offshore turbines reach deep water. Vertical-axis turbines (VAWT) work with wind from any direction and suit turbulent urban sites. Wind-assisted ships use sails or rotors to cut fuel use.

**Wave power**: devices convert the up-and-down or back-and-forth motion of waves into electricity. Problems: storm damage, corrosion, maintenance offshore.

**Biofuels**: ethanol from fermenting sugar or starch crops, and biodiesel from oil crops; hydrogen produced by some algae; microorganisms that make biogas (methane) from waste by anaerobic digestion. Problems: land competing with food, habitat clearance.

**Geothermal**: hot rocks heat water underground. Low-temperature fluids, unsuitable for electricity, can still supply district heating (hot water piped to many buildings). Sites are limited; some release H₂S or CO₂.

**Tidal**:

- Barrage: a dam across an estuary; water passes through turbines on the ebb (and flood) tide. Predictable but very costly and changes estuary habitats.
- Lagoon: a wall encloses part of the coast, with less effect on the whole estuary.
- In-stream turbines: underwater turbines in fast tidal currents, like underwater wind turbines.

### Worked example

Wind power ∝ v³. If wind speed rises from 8 m s⁻¹ to 10 m s⁻¹, power changes by (10 ÷ 8)³ = 1.95, nearly double.

## Supply, demand and storage

### What you need to know

- Causes of fluctuation: weather, season, weekday and weekend, 24-hour cycle, short-term peaks (for example mealtimes, television breaks).
- Storage: pumped-storage HEP (peak shaving), rechargeable batteries, fuel cells, compressed gas, thermal storage, vehicle-to-grid (V2G), power-to-gas (P2G), the hydrogen economy.

### Explained

Electricity must be generated at the same moment it is used. Demand peaks on winter evenings, falls at night and at weekends, and has sudden short peaks (many kettles switched on at the end of a popular programme). Wind and solar output varies with weather and season.

| Storage | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Pumped storage | Cheap off-peak electricity pumps water uphill; at peak times it flows down through turbines. Starts in seconds; "peak shaving" |
| Rechargeable batteries | Large lithium-ion arrays store surplus and release it quickly |
| Fuel cells | Hydrogen and oxygen react to give electricity, with water as waste |
| Compressed gas | Air compressed into caverns, released through turbines |
| Thermal storage | Heat stored in molten salt, water or rock |
| Vehicle-to-grid (V2G) | Parked electric cars return power to the grid at peak times |
| Power-to-gas (P2G) | Surplus electricity electrolyses water to make hydrogen (or methane) for storage |
| Hydrogen economy | Hydrogen used widely as a fuel for transport, heating and industry, made from renewable electricity |

### Common mistakes

- Saying pumped storage generates energy. It stores it and loses about 20 to 30% in the round trip.
- Saying fusion is used in power stations today.

## Mark scheme

1. Secondary: water / gas injected to maintain pressure and push oil out (1); tertiary: lowers oil viscosity using steam / CO₂ / detergents (1).
2. Fluid pumped at high pressure into shale through a horizontal well (1); fractures rock; sand holds cracks open so gas flows (1); any two concerns: water use; groundwater contamination; small earthquakes; methane leakage; traffic / noise (2).
3. Converts non-fissile uranium-238 into fissile plutonium-239 (1); so most of the uranium (not just the 0.7% U-235) can be used (1).
4. Layers absorb different wavelengths (1); more of the spectrum converted to electricity (1).
5. (9 ÷ 12)³ = 0.42 (1); 1.2 × 0.42 ≈ 0.51 MW (1).
6. Barrage: dam across estuary, large predictable output (1) but high cost, floods / changes mudflats, blocks fish (1); in-stream: smaller, cheaper, less habitat change (1) but lower output, maintenance underwater, possible collision with marine animals (1).
7. Off-peak cheap electricity pumps water to upper reservoir (1); at peak demand water released through turbines (1); responds within seconds to sudden demand (1).
8. Up to 5: stores large amounts for long periods (1); versatile fuel for transport / industry / heat (1); only water released when used (1); round-trip efficiency low (1); storage and transport difficult, leaks, embrittlement (1); infrastructure cost (1); judgement (1).

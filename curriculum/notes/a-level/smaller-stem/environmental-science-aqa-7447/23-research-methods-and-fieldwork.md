# 3.7 Research methods and fieldwork

Both papers test research methods. AQA does not set a list of experiments; instead every student must have first-hand experience of six methodologies (Me1 to Me6) and six groups of sampling technique (ST1 to ST6), with at least four days of fieldwork (or two field days plus twelve lab activities). For each technique, know its purpose, method and limitations. The section also gathers the maths skills listed in every "opportunities for skills development" sub-section.

## 3.7.1 Scientific methodologies (Me1 to Me6)

### What you need to know

Random sampling; systematic sampling; transects (line, belt, continuous, interrupted); timing of samples; sample size; number of samples; standardised techniques; designs that allow statistical significance to be tested.

### Explained

| Code | Methodology | When and why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Me1 | Random sampling | Area with no obvious gradient. Grid the area and use random number tables or a generator for coordinates, so every point has an equal chance and personal bias is avoided |
| Me2 | Systematic sampling | Where there is a gradient (shore, sand dune, distance from a road) or to sample at fixed intervals. Transects: a line transect records species touching a line; a belt transect uses quadrats along the line. Continuous transects sample all along; interrupted transects sample at intervals |
| Me3 | Number of samples | Enough samples to represent the variability. Plot a running mean against number of samples; when the mean stops changing much, enough samples have been taken |
| Me4 | Sample size | For example quadrat size: bigger for larger organisms or sparse vegetation; in a uniform (homogeneous) area small samples suffice. Plot species found against quadrat area (species-area curve) and choose the size where the curve levels off |
| Me5 | Sample timing | Conditions vary with weather, season and time of day (diurnal), for example light and temperature, insect activity. Sample at the same time or record conditions, and repeat over time |
| Me6 | Standard deviation and statistical significance | Use enough replicates to calculate the spread and apply a suitable statistical test |

**Standardised techniques**: same equipment, method, person (where possible) and time, so results are comparable between sites.

## 3.7.2.1 Standard sampling techniques (ST1 to ST6)

### Explained

**Biotic sampling**:

| Code | Technique | Purpose and method | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ST2 | Quadrats: open frame, gridded, point | Frame placed on the ground; record species, percentage cover, frequency or density. Point quadrat: pins lowered through a frame; species touched recorded | Hard to estimate cover consistently; overlapping layers; point quadrats slow |
| ST4 | Pitfall traps | Container sunk level with the ground, covered against rain, catches ground-surface invertebrates | Catches active species more; predators eat others in the trap; check often |
| ST4 | Tullgren funnel | Soil sample on a mesh under a lamp; animals move away from heat and light and fall into alcohol | Only active animals; slow-moving ones may be missed or die |
| ST4 | Earthworm extraction | Dilute mustard or irritant solution poured on soil; worms surface; or hand-sort a dug soil block | Not all worms respond; irritant can harm |
| ST5 | Sweep nets | Swept through long vegetation to catch insects | Catches only those on the upper vegetation; varies with operator |
| ST5 | Beating trays | Branches tapped over a tray to dislodge invertebrates | Flying insects escape |
| ST5 | Light traps | Lamp attracts night-flying insects (moths) into a box | Attracts from an unknown area; weather affects catch |
| ST5 | Bat detectors | Convert ultrasound calls to audible sound or record sonograms | Species identification needs skill or software |
| ST6 | Pond net and kick sampling | Kick the stream bed for a fixed time upstream of a net held in the flow | Hard to standardise effort |
| ST6 | Surber sampler | Frame of fixed area on the stream bed with a net; disturb inside the frame | Only shallow riffles |
| ST6 | Colonisation media | Artificial substrate (stones in a mesh bag) left in water, colonised then lifted | Takes weeks; may not match natural habitat |

**Measures of abundance and diversity**:

- DAFOR scale: Dominant, Abundant, Frequent, Occasional, Rare. Quick but subjective.
- Species richness: number of species. Species frequency: percentage of quadrats in which a species occurs. Density: number per unit area. Percentage cover: proportion of ground covered.
- **Lincoln Index** (mark-release-recapture): N = (n₁ × n₂) ÷ m₂, where n₁ = number marked in first sample, n₂ = number in second sample, m₂ = number marked in second sample. Assumes no births, deaths or migration, marks are not lost and do not affect survival, and marked animals mix fully.
- **Simpson's Index of Diversity**: D = 1 − Σ(n ÷ N)², where n is the number of individuals of each species and N the total. Values near 1 mean high diversity. (Some versions use n(n − 1) ÷ N(N − 1).)

**Abiotic measurements (ST1)**:

| Factor | Equipment |
| --- | --- |
| Light | Light meter (lux) |
| Temperature | Thermometer or temperature probe; data logger |
| Wind speed | Anemometer |
| Humidity | Hygrometer (wet and dry bulb) |
| Turbidity | Secchi disc (depth at which it disappears) or turbidity meter |
| pH | pH meter or indicator |
| Ion concentration (nitrate) | Test kits, colorimeter or ion-selective probe |

**Edaphic (soil) factors (ST3)**:

- Texture: sedimentation (shake soil with water in a measuring cylinder; sand settles first, then silt, then clay) or sieving; plot percentages on a soil texture triangle to name the soil.
- Water content: weigh, dry at about 105 °C to constant mass, reweigh. Water content (%) = (fresh mass − dry mass) ÷ dry mass × 100 (or ÷ fresh mass, state which).
- Organic matter: heat dry soil in a furnace (about 500 °C) to burn off organic matter; loss in mass ÷ dry mass × 100.
- pH: mix soil with distilled water (and barium sulfate to settle clay) and test.
- Bulk density: dry mass ÷ volume of a core (see [11](11-soils.md)).

## 3.7.2.2 Field and laboratory activities

Suitable investigations include: population size, density, frequency, distribution and biodiversity studies; soil analysis; climate variability for renewable energy (insolation, wind speed); heat loss from containers of different insulation or volume; biotic indices (lichens for air quality, aquatic invertebrates for water quality); effect of pH on seed germination; turbidity and light penetration; nutrient concentration and algal growth; noise against distance and insulation; slope and vegetation against rain-splash erosion; effect of trees on microclimate.

**Biotic indices**: some species tolerate pollution and others do not. Lichens: shrubby (fruticose) lichens need clean air; crusty (crustose) ones tolerate more SO₂. Freshwater invertebrates: stonefly and mayfly nymphs need high oxygen; bloodworms and rat-tailed maggots survive low oxygen. A score based on which groups are present indicates pollution.

## 3.7.2.3 Specialist techniques (knowledge only)

Camera traps (motion-sensitive) and photographic databases of features (stripe or fin patterns); marking (tags, rings, collars); acoustic recording and sonograms; radio, GPS and satellite tracking; satellite sensor data (land cover, sea temperature, chlorophyll); DNA and tissue databases; indirect evidence (nests, burrows, droppings, feeding signs, owl pellets, tracks, territorial marks). See also [05](05-monitoring-ecology-succession-and-populations.md).

## Maths skills (Appendix B) and statistical tests

### What you need to know

Units, standard form, ratios, percentages, estimation, logs; significant figures; mean, median, mode; frequency tables and histograms; probability; sampling; scatter graphs and correlation; order of magnitude; measures of spread (range, standard deviation); uncertainty; rearranging and solving equations; logarithms; graphs including kite, Sankey and radar diagrams; y = mx + c; intercepts and gradients; tangents; areas and volumes. Statistical tests: chi-squared, Student's t-test, Spearman's rank, Mann-Whitney U.

### Explained

**Choosing a test**:

| Question | Test |
| --- | --- |
| Is there a correlation between two variables (paired measurements)? | Spearman's rank correlation |
| Is there a difference between two sets of data that are normally distributed (means)? | Student's t-test |
| Is there a difference between two sets of data that are not normally distributed (medians, ordinal data)? | Mann-Whitney U |
| Do observed frequencies differ from expected, or is there an association between categories? | Chi-squared |

**Null hypothesis**: there is no difference (or no correlation). Compare the calculated value with the critical value at p = 0.05 (5% probability that the result is due to chance). For Spearman's, t and chi-squared, a calculated value greater than the critical value means reject the null hypothesis. For Mann-Whitney U, a calculated U smaller than or equal to the critical value means the difference is significant.

**Spearman's rank**: rₛ = 1 − (6Σd²) ÷ (n(n² − 1)), where d is the difference in ranks. Range −1 to +1.

**Chi-squared**: χ² = Σ (O − E)² ÷ E. Degrees of freedom = number of categories − 1 (or (rows − 1)(columns − 1) for a contingency table).

**Standard deviation** measures spread around the mean; about 68% of normally distributed values lie within one standard deviation of the mean.

**Uncertainty**: percentage uncertainty = (uncertainty ÷ reading) × 100.

### Worked example

Lincoln Index: 40 beetles caught and marked; next day 50 caught, 8 marked. N = 40 × 50 ÷ 8 = 250.

Simpson's: a quadrat has species A 10, B 6, C 4 (N = 20). Σ(n/N)² = 0.25 + 0.09 + 0.04 = 0.38. D = 1 − 0.38 = 0.62.

Spearman's: 6 sites, Σd² = 4. rₛ = 1 − (6 × 4) ÷ (6 × 35) = 1 − 24 ÷ 210 = 0.886. The critical value for n = 6 at p = 0.05 is 0.886, so the result is just significant; a strong positive correlation.

### Common mistakes

- Saying a significant correlation proves cause.
- Using a t-test on skewed or ranked data.
- Forgetting to state the null hypothesis and the probability level.

## Check questions

1. Explain when a belt transect should be used instead of random quadrats. (2)
2. Describe how to decide the number of quadrats needed to sample a meadow. (3)
3. 60 woodlice are marked and released. Later 45 are caught, of which 12 are marked. Estimate the population and give two assumptions. (3)
4. Calculate Simpson's Index of Diversity for a sample of: species P 15, Q 3, R 2. (2)
5. Describe how to measure the percentage organic matter in a soil sample. (4)
6. A student compared the number of invertebrate species above and below a sewage outfall at 10 sites each. The data were not normally distributed. Name the statistical test to use and state a null hypothesis. (2)
7. Explain one limitation of pitfall traps and suggest how to reduce it. (2)
8. Explain why invertebrate biotic indices give a better picture of river pollution over time than a single chemical test. (3)

## Mark scheme

1. Where there is an environmental gradient / change across the area (1); to show how species distribution changes along it (1).
2. Take quadrats one at a time and calculate a running mean (or species found) (1); plot against number of quadrats (1); stop when the mean / curve levels off (1).
3. 60 × 45 ÷ 12 = 225 (1); any two assumptions: no births / deaths / migration; marks not lost; marks do not affect survival; marked animals mix randomly (2).
4. N = 20; Σ(n/N)² = 0.5625 + 0.0225 + 0.01 = 0.595 (1); D = 0.405 (1).
5. Dry soil to constant mass and weigh (1); heat strongly in a furnace / crucible to burn organic matter (1); cool in a desiccator and reweigh, repeat to constant mass (1); loss in mass ÷ dry mass × 100 (1).
6. Mann-Whitney U (1); there is no difference in the number of invertebrate species above and below the outfall (1).
7. One limitation: predators eat other catches / flooded by rain / catches only active species (1); matching fix: check frequently / add cover / use with other methods (1).
8. Organisms live in the river continuously (1); integrate conditions over time, including pollution events missed by a single test (1); show the biological effect, not just a concentration (1).

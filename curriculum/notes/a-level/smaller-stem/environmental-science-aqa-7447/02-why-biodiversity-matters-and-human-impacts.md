# Why biodiversity matters and how humans affect it

Biodiversity is the variety of life: genes, species and ecosystems. This file covers the reasons to conserve it (resources we use now, value we have not yet found, and services ecosystems provide) and the ways human activity reduces it. Biodiversity indices are in [A-level Biology 14](../../biology/14-species-taxonomy-and-biodiversity.md) and in file [23](23-research-methods-and-fieldwork.md).

## 3.1.2.1.1 Resources and sustainable habitat management

### What you need to know

- Wild species and habitats supply resources: timber, fibres, vegetable oils, biofuels and new food crops.
- These can be harvested sustainably if the habitat is managed so that use does not exceed renewal.

### Explained

| Resource | Examples | Sustainable management |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timber | Hardwoods, softwoods | Harvest no more than annual growth; selective logging; replanting |
| Fibres | Cotton, rattan, sisal | Harvest a fraction of wild stems; protect the plants that regrow |
| Vegetable oils | Oil palm, coconut, shea | Grow on already-cleared land; certification schemes |
| Biofuels | Wood, ethanol from sugar cane | Avoid clearing forest; use residues |
| New food crops | Wild relatives domesticated (quinoa, many fruits) | Keep wild populations as a gene source |

The key idea is that a habitat is a renewable resource only while harvest stays below its maximum sustainable yield. Once a forest or wetland is cleared, its resources stop entirely.

### Common mistakes

- Treating all biofuels as sustainable. Clearing forest to grow them releases more carbon than they save.

## 3.1.2.1.2 Value of species not yet investigated

### What you need to know

Habitats should be conserved even where species have not been studied, because they may be useful for:

- biomimetics: copying designs from nature
- new medicines
- physiological research
- pest control by wildlife
- genetic resources, including wild crop relatives found in Vavilov centres.

### Explained

Most species have never been studied in detail, so we do not know what we lose when they go extinct.

- **Biomimetics**: engineers copy solutions evolved over millions of years, for example burrs that inspired hook-and-loop fasteners, termite mounds that inspired passive ventilation, and the shape of a kingfisher's beak used on high-speed train noses to reduce noise.
- **Medicines**: many drugs came first from organisms, for example aspirin from willow bark compounds, and the anticancer drugs vincristine and vinblastine from the Madagascar periwinkle. Untested species may hold more.
- **Physiological research**: unusual species help us understand body processes, for example animals that hibernate (bone and muscle loss) or that tolerate low oxygen.
- **Pest control**: predators and parasites control pests for free. Ladybirds eat aphids; some wasps parasitise caterpillars.
- **Genetic resources**: wild relatives of crops carry genes for disease resistance, drought tolerance and salt tolerance that can be bred into crops. **Vavilov centres** are regions where crops were first domesticated (for example the Fertile Crescent for wheat, the Andes for potato, Mesoamerica for maize). They hold the greatest genetic variety of those crops and their wild relatives, so they are priorities for conservation.

### Common mistakes

- Saying Vavilov centres are seed banks. They are geographic regions of origin; seed banks are ex-situ stores.

## 3.1.2.1.3 Ecosystem services

### What you need to know

Ecosystems provide services that support life and human society:

- control of atmospheric composition
- biogeochemical cycles
- services between species: pollination and seed dispersal
- soil formation and erosion control.

### Explained

- **Atmospheric composition**: photosynthesis removes CO₂ and releases O₂; forests and oceans are carbon sinks; wetlands and soils store carbon.
- **Biogeochemical cycles**: decomposers recycle nutrients; nitrogen-fixing bacteria make nitrogen available.
- **Pollination**: about three quarters of leading food crops benefit from animal pollination. Losing pollinators cuts yield and quality.
- **Seed dispersal**: birds, bats and mammals carry seeds, letting plants colonise new ground and recover after disturbance.
- **Soil**: roots bind soil; leaf litter adds organic matter; earthworms mix and aerate it. Vegetation cover reduces erosion by wind and rain.

Ecosystem services are often unpriced, so they are lost when land is cleared for a priced product. Assigning a value (for example, the cost of hand-pollinating a crop) helps decision-makers.

## 3.1.2.2 Human influence on biodiversity

### What you need to know

Humans reduce biodiversity through:

- direct exploitation (hunting, fishing, collecting)
- deliberate eradication (of pests, predators, disease vectors)
- changing abiotic factors: water, light, oxygen, nutrients, pH, temperature
- changing biotic factors: introduced species, loss of relationships between species
- habitat destruction: deforestation, agriculture, urbanisation, mineral extraction, reservoirs.

### Explained

**Direct exploitation**: species taken faster than they reproduce. Large, slow-breeding (K-selected) species are most at risk, for example great whales, rhinos for horn, and many fish stocks.

**Deliberate eradication**: wolves, bears and birds of prey were killed in Britain to protect livestock and game. Pest control can remove species with useful roles.

**Abiotic changes** (each with an example):

| Factor | Human cause | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water | Wetland drainage, river abstraction | Wetland species lost |
| Light | Turbid water from soil erosion; artificial light at night | Less aquatic photosynthesis; disrupted insect and bird behaviour |
| Oxygen | Organic pollution, eutrophication | Fish kills |
| Nutrients | Fertiliser run-off | Nutrient-loving plants outcompete specialists of poor soils |
| pH | Acid rain, acid mine drainage | Acid-sensitive species lost from lakes |
| Temperature | Climate change, thermal pollution | Range shifts; coral bleaching |

**Biotic changes**:

- Introduced (non-native, invasive) species compete with, prey on or spread disease to natives. Examples: grey squirrels carry squirrelpox, which kills red squirrels; rats on islands eat seabird eggs; Japanese knotweed shades out native plants.
- Loss of relationships: if a pollinator or seed disperser dies out, the plant that depends on it declines too.

**Habitat destruction** is the single largest cause of biodiversity loss: clearing forest for farmland, building towns and roads, quarrying and mining, and flooding valleys for reservoirs. Fragmentation into small patches makes populations small and isolated.

### Common mistakes

- Writing "pollution" without naming which abiotic factor changes and how.
- Treating every non-native species as harmful. Only invasive ones cause damage.

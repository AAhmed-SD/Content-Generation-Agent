# 3.1.1 Conditions for life on Earth

Life on Earth depends on a narrow set of physical conditions. This topic covers how those conditions came about, how early life then changed the planet, and how scientists work out what past conditions were like.

## 3.1.1.1 How the conditions for life came about

### What you need to know

- An atmosphere was kept because of the Earth's gravity. Its gases mattered: carbon dioxide and methane kept the surface warm (greenhouse effect); nitrogen was the bulk gas.
- Liquid water existed because surface temperatures sat between 0 °C and 100 °C at the pressures found on Earth.
- Insolation (incoming solar radiation) and albedo (the fraction reflected) set how much energy the surface absorbed.
- Distance from the Sun put Earth in the "habitable zone".
- The Earth's orbit (nearly circular) and axial tilt and rotation kept temperature changes moderate.
- The magnetic field (magnetosphere) deflects charged particles from the Sun (the solar wind and cosmic rays), protecting the atmosphere and surface.

### Explained

Think of each condition as a dial that had to be in the right range.

| Condition | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Gravity | A planet with too little mass (like Mars or the Moon) loses light gases to space. Earth's gravity held an atmosphere. |
| Greenhouse gases | Early Sun was about 25 to 30% dimmer. CO₂ and CH₄ trapped outgoing infrared, keeping water liquid instead of frozen. |
| Liquid water | A solvent for biochemistry, a medium for transport, and a store of heat (high specific heat capacity) that steadies temperatures. |
| Insolation and albedo | Energy absorbed = insolation × (1 − albedo). Ice and cloud raise albedo and cool; dark oceans and forests absorb more. |
| Distance from the Sun | Closer (Venus) leads to runaway greenhouse heating; further (Mars) is too cold for liquid water at the surface. |
| Orbit and axial behaviour | A near-circular orbit means small yearly changes in insolation. Rotation every 24 hours stops one side baking and the other freezing. Tilt gives seasons but spreads heat. |
| Magnetosphere | Generated by the liquid iron outer core. It shields the atmosphere from being stripped by the solar wind, and shields life from damaging particles. |

### Common mistakes

- Saying the greenhouse effect is harmful in itself. Without it the average surface temperature would be well below freezing; the problem today is the enhanced effect.
- Saying the magnetosphere blocks ultraviolet. It deflects charged particles. Ultraviolet is absorbed by ozone.
- Confusing albedo (reflection) with absorption: high albedo means less energy absorbed.

## 3.1.1.2 How life changed the environment

### What you need to know

- Photosynthetic bacteria (cyanobacteria), and later algae and plants, released oxygen into an atmosphere that had almost none.
- Oxygen in the upper atmosphere formed ozone (O₃), which absorbs UV and allowed life to move onto land.
- Organisms removed carbon from the atmosphere and locked it away (sequestration) in limestone, fossil fuels and organic matter.
- Living things link the biogeochemical cycles (carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus) together.

### Explained

The early atmosphere was mainly nitrogen, carbon dioxide and water vapour, with no free oxygen. From about 2.4 billion years ago, oxygen from cyanobacteria began to build up (the Great Oxidation Event). At first it reacted with dissolved iron in the oceans and with rocks; only when those "sinks" were used up did oxygen accumulate in the air.

Oxygen allowed:

- aerobic respiration, which releases far more energy per glucose than anaerobic respiration, so larger and more complex organisms became possible
- the ozone layer: O₂ split by UV forms O atoms, which join O₂ to make O₃. Ozone absorbs UV-B and UV-C, so the land surface became safe.

Carbon sequestration lowered CO₂. Marine organisms made calcium carbonate shells and skeletons, which became limestone. Plants buried in swamps became coal; plankton buried in mud became oil and gas. This cooled the planet as the Sun brightened, which kept temperatures suitable.

Organisms link cycles: photosynthesis takes in carbon and releases oxygen; nitrogen-fixing bacteria need energy from organic carbon; decomposers release carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus together when they break down dead matter.

### Common mistakes

- Saying plants made the first oxygen. Photosynthetic bacteria did, long before land plants.
- Saying ozone formed from carbon dioxide. It forms from oxygen.

## 3.1.1.3 Monitoring past conditions

### What you need to know

- Early methods had limits: they relied on proxy data (indirect evidence), such as dendrochronology (tree rings) and pollen analysis, which cover limited time spans and places and need calibration.
- Improved methods include long-term data sets, electronic monitoring, ice-core analysis of trapped gas and isotopes, and better platforms: balloons, aircraft and satellites.

### Explained

A proxy is something measurable today that depends on a past condition.

- Tree rings: wide rings mean good growing seasons (warm, wet); narrow rings mean poor ones. Counting rings gives the year. Limits: trees only live a few thousand years (longer with overlapping timber samples), only grow on land, and ring width depends on more than one factor.
- Pollen: each plant species has distinctive pollen that survives in lake and peat sediments. The mix of pollen shows the vegetation, and so the climate. Limits: some plants make far more pollen than others, and pollen can blow long distances.

Improved methods:

- Ice cores: air bubbles in Antarctic and Greenland ice are samples of past atmosphere, so CO₂ and CH₄ can be measured directly back about 800 000 years. The ratio of oxygen isotopes (¹⁸O to ¹⁶O) in the ice shows past temperature.
- Long-term data sets: measurement at one site for decades (for example continuous atmospheric CO₂ records since the 1950s) shows trends that short studies miss.
- Electronic monitoring: data loggers record continuously and unattended, reducing human error.
- Platforms: weather balloons sample the vertical profile; aircraft sample the troposphere and lower stratosphere; satellites give global coverage repeatedly.

### Worked example

An ice core shows CO₂ of 280 ppm before industrialisation. A modern measurement is 420 ppm. Percentage increase = (420 − 280) ÷ 280 × 100 = 50%.

### Common mistakes

- Saying ice cores show temperature directly. Temperature comes from isotope ratios (a proxy); gas concentrations are measured directly.
- Forgetting that proxies need calibrating against modern instrument records.

# Conservation priorities, legislation and captive breeding

Conservation money and effort are limited, so they have to be targeted. This file covers how priorities are set, the laws and agreements that protect species and sites, and how captive breeding and release (CBR) works.

## 3.1.2.3.1 Setting priorities

### What you need to know

- The IUCN (International Union for Conservation of Nature) assesses species and publishes the Red List.
- Criteria for priority: Red List category, evolutionary uniqueness (EDGE species), endemic species, keystone species, flagship species, and how dispersed a population is.

### Explained

**Red List categories** run from Least Concern, Near Threatened, Vulnerable, Endangered and Critically Endangered to Extinct in the Wild and Extinct. Species with too little information are Data Deficient. The threatened categories are decided by measurable criteria: how fast the population is falling, how small it is, how small its geographic range is, and the modelled probability of extinction.

**EDGE** means Evolutionarily Distinct and Globally Endangered. A species with no close relatives (for example the aye-aye or a tuatara) holds a large amount of unique evolutionary history. Losing it loses more "branch length" from the tree of life than losing one of many similar species.

**Endemic species** live in only one place, often an island or isolated mountain. If they are lost there, they are lost everywhere.

**Keystone species** have an effect on their ecosystem far larger than their numbers suggest. Examples: sea otters eat sea urchins, which would otherwise destroy kelp forests; beavers create wetlands; fig trees feed many animals when other fruit is scarce. Protecting a keystone species protects many others.

**Flagship species** are charismatic animals (giant panda, tiger, orangutan) used to raise public interest and money. Protecting their habitat protects many less popular species too.

**Population dispersal**: a species split into a few small, isolated populations is at higher risk (inbreeding, local catastrophes) than one with many connected populations.

### Common mistakes

- Confusing keystone (ecological importance) with flagship (public appeal). Some species are both, but the reasons differ.

## 3.1.2.3.2 Legislation and protocols

### What you need to know

- The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 (UK).
- UK site designations: SSSI, NNR, SAC, SPA, Natura 2000 sites, Ramsar sites, MNR, LNR, MPA, MCZ.
- CITES (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species), Appendices I and II.
- Regulation of exploitation by the IWC (International Whaling Commission), the EU Common Fisheries Policy (CFP) and the ITTO (International Tropical Timber Organisation).

### Explained

**Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981**: makes it an offence to kill, injure or take listed wild animals and to pick or uproot listed plants; protects nesting birds; controls release of non-native species; and is the basis for designating SSSIs.

**Site designations**:

| Designation | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| SSSI (Site of Special Scientific Interest) | UK sites of special wildlife or geological value; owners need consent for damaging operations |
| NNR (National Nature Reserve) | Key sites managed mainly for conservation and research |
| LNR (Local Nature Reserve) | Declared by local councils for wildlife and for people to enjoy |
| SAC (Special Area of Conservation) | Protects listed habitats and non-bird species (originally under the EU Habitats Directive) |
| SPA (Special Protection Area) | Protects rare and migratory birds (originally under the EU Birds Directive) |
| Natura 2000 | The network of SACs and SPAs across Europe |
| Ramsar site | Wetland of international importance (Ramsar Convention, 1971) |
| MNR (Marine Nature Reserve) | Early UK marine protected sites |
| MPA (Marine Protected Area) | General term for protected sea areas |
| MCZ (Marine Conservation Zone) | UK marine areas protecting nationally important habitats and species |

**CITES** controls international trade:

- Appendix I: species threatened with extinction. Commercial trade is banned (for example tigers, most great apes).
- Appendix II: species that could become threatened if trade is not controlled. Trade needs an export permit showing it is not harmful to the species' survival.

**Regulating exploitation**:

- IWC: set a moratorium (pause) on commercial whaling from 1986.
- EU Common Fisheries Policy: sets catch limits (quotas), technical rules on nets, and closed areas for EU waters.
- ITTO: promotes trade in tropical timber from sustainably managed forests.

### Common mistakes

- Mixing up SAC (habitats and non-bird species) and SPA (birds).
- Saying CITES bans all trade in Appendix II species. It controls it with permits.

## 3.1.2.3.3 Captive breeding and release

### What you need to know

- Ex-situ conservation keeps organisms away from their natural habitat (zoos, seed banks, botanic gardens).
- Criteria for choosing CBR, breeding methods (artificial insemination, embryo transfer, managing group dynamics), soft and hard release, and post-release monitoring.

### Explained

**When CBR is appropriate**: the wild population is too small to recover alone; the species breeds well in captivity; suitable habitat exists (or will exist) for release; the original threat has been removed. Without the last two, released animals simply die.

**Breeding methods**:

- Artificial insemination (AI): sperm from a male is placed in a female. Sperm can be frozen and moved between zoos, so genes are shared without moving animals, and males that will not mate can still father young.
- Embryo transfer: embryos are placed into a surrogate mother, possibly of a common related species, so a rare female can produce more young.
- Group dynamics: social species need the right group size and structure to breed (for example flamingos need a large flock; some primates need stable family groups).
- Studbooks record the ancestry of every animal so that matings avoid inbreeding and keep genetic diversity.

**Release methods**:

- Hard release: animals are released straight into the wild with no support.
- Soft release: animals are kept in an enclosure at the release site to get used to it, with food and shelter provided at first and gradually withdrawn. Survival is usually higher.

**Problems**: captive animals may lose wild behaviours (finding food, avoiding predators); small founder populations lose genetic variation; disease may spread to wild populations; cost is high.

**Post-release monitoring**: radio collars or tags, sightings and counts track survival, breeding and movement, so methods can be improved. Without monitoring, success is unknown.

### Worked example

A captive population was founded by 8 animals. The fraction of heterozygosity kept per generation is 1 − 1/(2N), where N is the effective population size. With N = 8: 1 − 1/16 = 0.9375. After 5 generations: 0.9375⁵ ≈ 0.72, so about 28% of the original heterozygosity is lost. This shows why studbooks and gene exchange between zoos matter.

### Common mistakes

- Saying CBR saves a species on its own. It only works with habitat protection and removal of the original threat.

# 3.6 Sustainability

This section pulls the course together. Natural systems have run for billions of years by keeping dynamic equilibria, using renewable energy and recycling materials. Human systems are often linear and unstable. The circular economy applies natural principles to human activity. Expect synoptic questions that draw on every other topic.

## 3.6.1 Dynamic equilibria

### What you need to know

- 3.6.1.1 Negative feedback resists change: climate (cloud and albedo; photosynthesis and CO₂), the hydrological cycle, density-dependent control of populations.
- 3.6.1.2 Positive feedback amplifies change: permafrost melting, ocean acidification, falling albedo, methane hydrate release, forest and peat fires, cirrus cloud formation, soil decomposition.
- 3.6.1.3 Tipping points: natural processes that human activity may make self-sustaining (fires, methane hydrates, permafrost).
- 3.6.1.4 Diverse systems (coral reefs, rainforests) resist change better than low-diversity human systems and agroecosystems.

### Explained

A dynamic equilibrium has continuous flows in and out but stays roughly constant overall, because negative feedback corrects any change.

**Negative feedback examples**:

- Warming → more evaporation → more low cloud → more sunlight reflected → cooling.
- More CO₂ → faster photosynthesis → CO₂ removed.
- Hydrological cycle: more evaporation → more rain, balancing stores over time.
- Population above carrying capacity → more competition and disease → more deaths → population falls (density-dependent control; see [05](05-monitoring-ecology-succession-and-populations.md)).

**Positive feedback examples** (warming leading to more warming): permafrost melting releases CH₄ and CO₂; warmer, more acidic oceans absorb less CO₂; ice melt lowers albedo; methane hydrates destabilise; drier forests and peat burn, releasing CO₂; more cirrus cloud traps IR; faster soil decomposition releases CO₂ (see [06](06-the-atmosphere-and-global-climate-change.md)).

**Tipping points**: once a threshold is crossed, a positive feedback keeps going without further human input. Examples: permafrost carbon release once thawing is widespread; widespread drought and fire turning rainforest into savanna; methane hydrate release.

**Diversity and stability**: in a species-rich ecosystem, many species fill similar roles, and food webs have many links, so if one species declines, others take over (functional redundancy). Coral reefs and rainforests have kept going through many past changes. Monoculture farms and towns depend on few species and on constant human inputs; a single pest or supply failure can collapse them.

## 3.6.2 Energy

### What you need to know

- 3.6.2.1 Natural processes run on renewable energy, mainly solar: the hydrological, carbon and nitrogen cycles, atmospheric and ocean circulation.
- 3.6.2.2 Natural processes use low energy-density sources, which can be concentrated to higher densities.
- 3.6.2.3 Most natural processes work at low temperatures (photosynthesis, enzymes, nitrogen fixation, compared with the Haber process).
- 3.6.2.4 Carbon footprints and sustainable development reconsidered in the light of copying natural energy systems.

### Explained

- Solar energy drives evaporation (water cycle), photosynthesis (carbon cycle), winds and ocean currents. It is effectively unlimited and causes no pollution.
- Sunlight is spread thinly (low energy density), but natural systems concentrate it: photosynthesis stores it in sugars, food chains concentrate it in animals, and over geological time it was concentrated into fossil fuels. Human systems could do the same: solar panels and wind turbines over large areas, storage in batteries or hydrogen.
- Biological processes use enzymes to work at about 0 to 40 °C and normal pressure. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert N₂ to ammonia at soil temperature; the Haber process needs about 450 °C and 200 atmospheres, using about 1 to 2% of world energy. Copying low-temperature processes (biotechnology, enzyme catalysts) saves energy.
- A sustainable society would run on renewable energy, concentrate it efficiently, and use low-temperature processes, cutting carbon footprints.

## 3.6.3 Material cycles

### What you need to know

- 3.6.3.1 Many human systems are linear: fossil fuels are used up; minerals are dispersed and mixed, so they are hard to recover.
- 3.6.3.2 Natural systems use a small set of elements to build monomers and polymers (carbohydrates, proteins) and reuse simple raw materials.
- 3.6.3.3 Natural wastes are non-toxic or do not build up: molecules are biodegradable.

### Explained

A linear economy is "take, make, dispose": resources are extracted, made into products and thrown away. Metals end up spread through landfill in tiny amounts; complex mixtures (alloys, composites, electronics) are hard to separate.

Natural systems build everything from a few elements (C, H, O, N, P, S and a few others), joined into a few types of monomer (sugars, amino acids, nucleotides) and then into polymers. When organisms die, decomposers break polymers back to simple molecules that are reused. No natural waste accumulates indefinitely, because something can always break it down. In contrast, many synthetic materials (plastics, persistent pesticides) are not biodegradable.

## 3.6.4 The circular economy

### What you need to know

- 3.6.4.1 Principles: cycling materials; renewable energy; supporting natural ecosystems; separating technical and biological materials; diversity and connected systems; designing for reuse at end of life; optimum rather than maximum production (pollinators and decomposers versus agroecosystems); design technologies. Applied to land use, water, minerals, waste, pollution control, energy and food.
- 3.6.4.2 Biocapacity and ecological footprints; regional comparisons using the WWF Living Planet Report, the Living Planet Index and footprint calculations.

### Explained

**Circular economy principles**:

- Materials are kept in use for as long as possible, then recovered.
- **Biological materials** (food, wood, natural fibres) are designed to return safely to the soil (compost, anaerobic digestion).
- **Technical materials** (metals, plastics) are designed to be recovered and remanufactured without loss of quality, kept separate from biological materials.
- Energy comes from renewable sources.
- Systems are diverse and connected, so they are resilient.
- Products are designed for disassembly and reuse (see Cradle to Cradle in [09](09-mineral-resources.md)).
- Aim for optimum production that keeps the system healthy, not maximum short-term output (for example keeping pollinators and soil decomposers rather than maximising yield with chemicals that destroy them).

**Applications**:

| Area | Circular approach |
| --- | --- |
| Land use | Multiple uses, such as agroforestry; protecting ecosystem services |
| Water | Conservation, reuse of grey water, catchment management |
| Minerals | Recovering metals from low-grade ores and waste (bioleaching, phytomining) |
| Waste | Waste hierarchy: reduce, reuse, repurpose, recycle |
| Pollution control | Moving from end-of-pipe treatment to not producing pollutants at all (for example fuel cells that emit only water) |
| Energy | Renewables; low-temperature manufacturing |
| Food | Natural nutrient supply, biological pest control, soil protection (see [19](19-agriculture.md)) |

**Biocapacity and ecological footprint**:

- Biocapacity: the area of productive land and sea available to produce resources and absorb wastes, measured in global hectares (gha).
- Ecological footprint: the area needed to supply a population's consumption and absorb its CO₂ (also in gha).
- If footprint exceeds biocapacity, the population is in ecological deficit, relying on imports or using up natural capital. The world as a whole currently uses resources faster than the Earth regenerates them.
- The WWF Living Planet Report tracks footprints and the **Living Planet Index**, an average of trends in monitored vertebrate populations since 1970, which shows large declines, especially in tropical regions and fresh water.

### Worked example

A country has a population of 60 million, a footprint of 4.5 gha per person and a biocapacity of 1.2 gha per person. Deficit per person = 3.3 gha. Total deficit = 3.3 × 60 × 10⁶ = 1.98 × 10⁸ gha. The country would need 4.5 ÷ 1.2 = 3.75 times its own biocapacity to be self-sufficient.

### Common mistakes

- Calling any change in a system "positive feedback" because the outcome is good. Positive means the change is amplified.
- Saying recycling alone is a circular economy. Design for reuse, renewable energy and reducing consumption are also needed.

# 3.2.4 Biogeochemical cycles

Elements that living things need are recycled between the atmosphere, oceans, rocks, soil and organisms. Human activities speed up some flows and create new ones. The biology of the nitrogen and phosphorus cycles is also in [A-level Biology 17](../../biology/17-energy-in-ecosystems-and-nutrient-cycles.md).

## 3.2.4.1 Why cycles matter

### What you need to know

Many elements are scarce in available forms, so natural systems recycle and reuse them.

### Explained

Only a small part of each element is in a form organisms can use (for example, nitrogen gas is 78% of the air, but most organisms cannot use N₂). Natural systems therefore reuse the same atoms repeatedly: decomposers return them from dead matter to soil and water. Human systems often take elements from a store and release them as waste in the wrong place, causing both depletion and pollution.

## 3.2.4.2 The carbon cycle

### What you need to know

- Processes affected by humans: photosynthesis, aerobic and anaerobic respiration, combustion, exchange of CO₂ between air and sea, movement of biomass, changes in stores, dissolved CO₂ and carbonic acid, methane in the atmosphere.
- Sustainable management: alternatives to fossil fuels, carbon sequestration, carbon capture and storage (CCS), matching afforestation to deforestation, raising soil organic matter, conserving peat bogs.

### Explained

**Stores**: rocks (limestone, fossil fuels) hold by far the most carbon; then the deep ocean; then soils, vegetation and the atmosphere.

**Human effects**:

- Combustion of fossil fuels moves carbon from a long-term rock store to the atmosphere in decades rather than millions of years.
- Deforestation reduces photosynthesis and releases carbon from trees and soil.
- Draining peat bogs lets oxygen in, so aerobic decomposition releases CO₂.
- Rice paddies, landfill and livestock increase anaerobic respiration, releasing methane.
- The oceans absorb about a quarter of emitted CO₂. CO₂ dissolves and forms carbonic acid (CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃), lowering ocean pH (ocean acidification), which harms shell-building organisms.

**Management**:

| Method | How it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Renewable and nuclear energy | Less fossil carbon released |
| Afforestation (at least matching deforestation) | Trees store carbon as they grow |
| Carbon capture and storage | CO₂ captured from power stations and pumped into porous rocks underground (old gas fields, saline aquifers) |
| Raising soil organic matter | Less ploughing, cover crops, adding compost store carbon in soil |
| Conserving and re-wetting peat bogs | Waterlogging stops decomposition so carbon stays stored |

## 3.2.4.3 The nitrogen cycle

### What you need to know

- Human effects: the Haber process; land drainage; growing legumes; sewage and eutrophication; NOx from combustion; decomposition and ammonification.
- Consequences: eutrophication, climate change (N₂O), toxicity of NOx, photochemical smog.
- Management: less combustion, using natural nitrogen fixation instead of Haber fertilisers, biological waste management, reducing nitrate leaching.

### Explained

Natural cycle in brief: nitrogen-fixing bacteria (free-living and in legume root nodules) and lightning convert N₂ to ammonium or nitrate; plants absorb nitrate and ammonium; decomposers turn dead matter into ammonium (ammonification); nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonium to nitrite then nitrate; denitrifying bacteria in waterlogged soils turn nitrate back to N₂.

**Human effects**:

- The Haber process fixes about as much nitrogen as all natural land fixation, as fertiliser.
- Land drainage aerates soil, reducing denitrification and increasing nitrification, so more nitrate.
- Growing legumes adds fixed nitrogen.
- Sewage and manure add ammonium and organic nitrogen to rivers.
- High-temperature combustion joins N₂ and O₂ to make NOx.

**Consequences**:

- Eutrophication: nitrate (and phosphate) washed into water cause algal blooms; when the algae die, decomposing bacteria use up oxygen; fish and invertebrates die.
- N₂O from fertilised soils is a greenhouse gas and also depletes stratospheric ozone.
- NOx irritates lungs and forms acid rain and photochemical smog with hydrocarbons in sunlight.

**Management**: lower combustion temperatures and catalytic converters; legumes and crop rotation rather than synthetic fertiliser; composting and anaerobic digestion of wastes; applying fertiliser in the right amount at the right time; buffer strips beside rivers; keeping soil covered in winter to stop nitrate leaching.

## 3.2.4.4 The phosphorus cycle

### What you need to know

- Mobilisation of phosphate rock as fertiliser; eutrophication (with nitrates); phosphate as a limiting factor.
- Management: biological waste as fertiliser, breeding crops that use phosphate efficiently, mycorrhizal fungi.

### Explained

The phosphorus cycle has no significant gas phase. Phosphate is released very slowly by weathering of rocks, taken up by plants, passed along food chains, and returned by decomposition. Much ends up in ocean sediments, which become rock again only over millions of years.

Phosphate is often the **limiting factor** for plant and algal growth in fresh water, because natural supplies are so low. So even small additions (from fertiliser run-off, sewage and detergents) cause eutrophication.

Humans mine phosphate rock, which is finite and concentrated in a few countries, to make fertiliser.

**Management**: recycling phosphate from manure, sewage sludge and food waste; breeding crops that take up phosphate more efficiently; encouraging mycorrhizal fungi, which extend the root system and absorb phosphate for the plant; removing phosphate at sewage works (by precipitation with iron salts).

### Worked example

A river carries 2.5 mg dm⁻³ of nitrate with a flow of 3.0 m³ s⁻¹. Mass per second = 2.5 mg dm⁻³ × 3000 dm³ s⁻¹ = 7500 mg s⁻¹ = 7.5 g s⁻¹. Per day: 7.5 × 86 400 = 648 000 g ≈ 650 kg of nitrate.

### Common mistakes

- Saying algae use up the oxygen in eutrophication. Bacteria decomposing dead algae do.
- Giving phosphorus an atmospheric stage.

# 3.2.1.3 Ozone depletion

Stratospheric ozone depletion is studied as a case of how environmental science works: a hypothesis, evidence, explanation of the pattern, and an international response. The chemistry is also in [A-level Chemistry 35](../../chemistry/35-atmosphere-ozone-and-green-chemistry.md).

## The hypothesis

### What you need to know

- The Rowland-Molina hypothesis (1974): CFCs are persistent, reach the stratosphere, are split by UV, and release chlorine that destroys ozone.

### Explained

CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) were used as refrigerants, aerosol propellants and in foams because they are unreactive, non-toxic and non-flammable. Being unreactive, they are not broken down in the troposphere and slowly mix up into the stratosphere over several years.

In the stratosphere, strong UV breaks a C–Cl bond, releasing a chlorine atom (a free radical):

- Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂
- ClO + O → Cl + O₂

The chlorine atom is regenerated, so it acts as a catalyst: one atom can destroy many thousands of ozone molecules before it is locked up in a stable compound. Overall: O₃ + O → 2O₂.

## Effects of increased UV-B

### What you need to know

Effects on human health, plants and marine life.

### Explained

- **Human health**: more skin cancer (including melanoma), cataracts, sunburn and suppressed immune response.
- **Plants**: DNA damage, reduced photosynthesis and growth, lower crop yields.
- **Marine life**: phytoplankton in surface water are damaged, reducing productivity at the base of food webs; larvae of fish and other animals near the surface are harmed.

## Collecting the evidence

### What you need to know

- Ground-based and satellite data.
- Variation in ozone with place (spatial), time (temporal, seasonal) and altitude.

### Explained

- **Ground stations** (for example spectrophotometers in Antarctica) measured total column ozone from the 1950s. British Antarctic Survey scientists published a large springtime fall in 1985.
- **Satellites** give global maps. Early satellite software had flagged very low values as errors, so the hole was at first missed; reprocessing confirmed it. This shows the need to question automatic data filtering.
- **Balloons (ozonesondes)** measure ozone at different altitudes, showing loss concentrated in the lower stratosphere (about 15 to 20 km).

Variability: ozone varies naturally with latitude, season, the solar cycle and weather systems, so long records are needed to see a trend.

## Why Antarctica is worst

### What you need to know

Very low temperature, ice crystals, polar stratospheric clouds, the polar vortex.

### Explained

1. In the Antarctic winter a strong circular wind (the polar vortex) isolates the air above the continent, so it cannot mix with warmer, ozone-rich air.
2. Temperatures in the vortex fall below about −78 °C, so polar stratospheric clouds (PSCs) of ice and nitric acid crystals form.
3. Reactions on the crystal surfaces convert stable chlorine "reservoir" compounds (HCl, ClONO₂) into forms (Cl₂) that are easily split by light.
4. In spring (September to November) sunlight returns, splits the chlorine, and catalytic destruction is very fast.
5. When the vortex breaks up in late spring, ozone-rich air flows back in.

The Arctic vortex is weaker and warmer, so loss there is smaller and less regular.

## Restoring the ozone layer

### What you need to know

- The Montreal Protocol (1987): phasing out CFCs, use of alternatives, collection and safe disposal of existing CFCs.
- Comparing the response with the response to climate change.

### Explained

The Montreal Protocol set dates to stop making CFCs and other ozone-depleting substances, with later amendments tightening controls and adding more chemicals. It worked because:

- the cause was clear and the science agreed
- there were few producers, so control was simple
- alternatives existed (HCFCs as a temporary step, then HFCs, hydrocarbons, ammonia)
- poorer countries got time and money to change
- old fridges and equipment were collected and CFCs recovered and destroyed.

Atmospheric chlorine is now falling and the ozone layer is slowly recovering, expected to return to 1980 levels around the middle of this century (later over Antarctica), because CFCs last decades.

**Comparison with climate change**:

| Ozone | Climate change |
| --- | --- |
| A few gases from a few industries | Emissions from almost every activity (energy, transport, farming) |
| Cheap alternatives available | Alternatives often need big investment |
| Effects clear and quick to show | Effects spread over decades, harder to attribute |
| Strong international agreement | Agreements (Kyoto, Paris) harder to enforce |

A complication: HFCs, the CFC replacements, are strong greenhouse gases; a later amendment (Kigali, 2016) phases them down.

### Common mistakes

- Saying CFCs destroy ozone directly. Chlorine atoms released from CFCs do, as catalysts.
- Saying the ozone hole is a hole with no ozone. It is a region of greatly reduced ozone.
- Saying the hole lets heat in and causes global warming.

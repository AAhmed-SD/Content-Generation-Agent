# 3.1.2.3.4 Habitat conservation and key ecosystems

Protecting habitats (in-situ conservation) protects whole communities at once and keeps the relationships between species working. This file covers how habitats are conserved and created, what makes a reserve effective, and seven ecosystems the spec names: for each, its features, importance, threats and the conservation methods used.

## Habitat conservation methods

### What you need to know

- In-situ conservation protects species in their natural habitats.
- Habitat creation and rewilding.
- Reserve design: area, shape, age of habitat and wildlife corridors.

### Explained

**In-situ conservation** is usually cheaper per species than captive breeding, keeps natural behaviour and keeps evolution going. It needs control of threats (hunting, clearance, pollution).

**Habitat creation**: making new habitat, for example digging ponds, planting woodland, creating reedbeds on old gravel pits, or managed realignment of coasts to form saltmarsh.

**Rewilding**: restoring natural processes and letting the ecosystem largely manage itself, often by reintroducing missing species (for example beavers, whose dams create wetland) and reducing human management.

**Reserve design**:

| Feature | Better | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Area | Larger | Supports bigger populations, more species and large-range animals |
| Shape | Compact (near circular) | Less edge relative to area, so fewer edge effects (drying, light, disturbance, predators from outside) |
| Age | Older habitat | Older woodland has more niches (dead wood, old trees) and species that are slow to colonise |
| Corridors | Present | Hedgerows, river banks and green bridges link fragments so animals can move, find mates and recolonise |

### Worked example

Compare edge-to-area for a 1 km by 1 km square reserve and a 4 km by 0.25 km strip of the same area. Both have area 1 km². Perimeters are 4 km and 8.5 km. The strip has more than twice as much edge per unit area, so more of it is affected by edge effects.

## The seven ecosystems

### Temperate broadleaf woodland

- **Features**: deciduous trees (oak, ash, beech) that drop leaves in winter; layered structure (canopy, shrub layer, field layer, ground layer); rich leaf litter and soil fauna; seasonal light on the woodland floor in spring (bluebells flower before the canopy closes).
- **Importance**: biodiversity (many invertebrates on oak), timber, carbon storage, soil protection, recreation.
- **Threats**: clearance for farming and building; fragmentation; overgrazing by deer, which stops regeneration; invasive species (rhododendron); tree diseases (ash dieback); loss of traditional management.
- **Conservation**: protection as SSSI or NNR; coppicing to restore varied structure; deer control; planting native species; linking fragments with hedgerows.

### Tropical rainforest

- **Features**: high rainfall and temperature all year; very high biodiversity and productivity; most nutrients held in living biomass, with thin, nutrient-poor soils; tall layered canopy; many epiphytes.
- **Importance**: biodiversity, medicines, timber and non-timber products, carbon store, regional rainfall (transpiration recycles moisture), indigenous peoples' homes.
- **Threats**: clearance for cattle ranching, soya and oil palm; logging; mining; roads opening up the forest; fire.
- **Conservation**: protected areas; sustainable forestry certification; extractive reserves where local people harvest products sustainably; ecotourism; **Debt for Nature Swaps**, in which part of a country's foreign debt is cancelled or bought by a conservation body in return for the country funding forest protection.

### Tropical coral reefs

- **Features**: built by colonial corals with symbiotic algae (zooxanthellae) in their tissues; need warm (about 20 to 29 °C), clear, shallow, nutrient-poor, well-lit water; very high biodiversity.
- **Importance**: fisheries, coastal protection from waves, tourism, medicines, biodiversity.
- **Threats**: rising sea temperature causes bleaching (corals expel their algae); ocean acidification makes calcium carbonate harder to build; sediment from land clearance blocks light; nutrient pollution favours algae; overfishing of grazing fish; destructive fishing (explosives, cyanide); crown-of-thorns starfish outbreaks; physical damage by anchors and divers.
- **Conservation**: marine protected areas with no-take zones; controlling land run-off; banning destructive fishing; reducing CO₂ emissions; coral gardening (growing fragments to replant).

### Deep-water coral reefs

- **Features**: cold-water corals (for example *Lophelia*) at hundreds of metres depth with no light and no symbiotic algae; they feed on plankton; grow very slowly (millimetres per year); form reefs that shelter fish.
- **Importance**: biodiversity, fish nursery areas.
- **Threats**: bottom trawling smashes reefs, which take centuries to regrow; oil and gas drilling; acidification.
- **Conservation**: banning bottom trawling in reef areas (marine protected areas); mapping reefs so they can be avoided.

### Oceanic islands

- **Features**: isolated, so few species arrive; many endemic species evolve; often no native mammal predators, so birds may be flightless and ground-nesting; small populations.
- **Importance**: endemic species, evolutionary research (adaptive radiation).
- **Threats**: introduced predators (rats, cats, goats eating vegetation); habitat clearance; small ranges, so a single event can cause extinction.
- **Conservation**: eradicating invasive mammals (for example rat removal from seabird islands); biosecurity to stop new arrivals; captive breeding of endemics.

### Mangroves

- **Features**: salt-tolerant trees on tropical coasts in the intertidal zone; aerial roots (pneumatophores) for gas exchange in waterlogged mud; prop roots trap sediment.
- **Importance**: coastal protection from storms and tsunamis; nursery areas for fish; carbon store in mud ("blue carbon"); timber and fuel; filter sediment, protecting nearby coral reefs.
- **Threats**: clearance for shrimp farms, tourism and building; pollution; changes to freshwater and sediment supply.
- **Conservation**: protection; replanting; sustainable harvesting by local communities.

### Antarctica

- **Features**: coldest continent; mostly ice-covered; very low terrestrial biodiversity but productive surrounding ocean (krill support whales, seals and penguins).
- **Importance**: climate records in ice; global climate regulation; wildlife; science.
- **Threats**: climate change (ice shelf loss, changing sea ice affecting krill and penguins); overfishing of krill and toothfish; tourism; pollution; past whaling and sealing.
- **Conservation**: the **Antarctic Treaty 1959** sets the continent aside for peace and science; later agreements ban mineral extraction and protect the environment; regulation of fishing (catch limits for krill and toothfish); controls on tourism.

### Common mistakes

- Saying deep-water corals bleach. They have no symbiotic algae; bleaching is a tropical coral problem.
- Saying rainforest soils are fertile. Most nutrients are in the vegetation, so cleared land loses fertility quickly.

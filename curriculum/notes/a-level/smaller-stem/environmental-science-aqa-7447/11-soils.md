# 3.2.5 Soils

Soil forms from weathered rock, organic matter, water and air, over hundreds to thousands of years per centimetre. It supports almost all food production, so damaging it is effectively using up a non-renewable resource. Soil sampling methods (texture, water, organic matter, pH, bulk density) are in [23](23-research-methods-and-fieldwork.md) (ST3).

## 3.2.5.1 Human effects on soil fertility

### What you need to know

How ploughing and drainage (aeration), nutrient addition, irrigation, compaction (bulk density) and pH control change fertility.

### Explained

| Practice | Effect on fertility |
| --- | --- |
| Ploughing | Aerates soil, kills weeds, buries residues. But speeds decomposition of organic matter, damages soil structure and soil organisms, and exposes soil to erosion |
| Drainage | Removes waterlogging, so roots get oxygen and aerobic decomposers work; soils warm faster in spring. Can dry wetlands and release carbon |
| Nutrient addition | Fertilisers and manure replace nutrients removed by crops. Excess leaches and pollutes water; organic manures also improve structure |
| Irrigation | Supplies water in dry areas. In hot climates, evaporation leaves salts at the surface (salinisation), which harms crops |
| Compaction | Heavy machinery and trampling squash pores, increasing bulk density. Less air and water infiltration, roots cannot penetrate, more run-off |
| pH control | Liming (adding calcium carbonate) raises the pH of acid soils, making nutrients available and helping soil organisms |

**Bulk density** = dry mass of soil ÷ total volume (including pores). A higher value means more compaction and fewer pores.

### Worked example

A soil core of volume 250 cm³ has a dry mass of 400 g. Bulk density = 400 ÷ 250 = 1.6 g cm⁻³. If the mineral particle density is 2.65 g cm⁻³, porosity = 1 − (1.6 ÷ 2.65) = 0.40, so 40% pore space.

## 3.2.5.2 Soil degradation and erosion

### What you need to know

- Erosion by rain splash, wind blow and surface run-off.
- Features that protect soil: vegetation cover, organic matter, infiltration rate.
- The Universal Soil Loss Equation (USLE).
- Human causes: ploughing vulnerable soils, removing vegetation, overgrazing, compaction, cultivating steep slopes.
- Impacts: loss of productivity, sedimentation, downstream flooding, coastal sedimentation, airborne particulates, desertification, landslides.

### Explained

**Erosion processes**:

- Rain splash: raindrops hit bare soil, detaching particles and throwing them downslope.
- Surface run-off: water that cannot infiltrate flows over the surface, carrying soil in sheets, rills and gullies.
- Wind blow: strong wind lifts dry, fine, loose particles, especially from bare, flat fields.

**Protective features**: vegetation intercepts rain and binds soil with roots; organic matter binds particles into stable crumbs (aggregates) and holds water; high infiltration rates reduce run-off.

**USLE**: A = R × K × LS × C × P

| Factor | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| A | Predicted average annual soil loss |
| R | Rainfall erosivity (intensity and amount of rain) |
| K | Soil erodibility (texture, structure, organic matter) |
| LS | Slope length and steepness |
| C | Cover and management (vegetation, crop type) |
| P | Support practices (contour ploughing, terracing) |

Each factor is a number from tables; lowering any factor (more cover, contour ploughing) reduces predicted loss. It is an empirical model for average loss, not single storms.

**Impacts**:

- On site: loss of fertile topsoil, lower yields, more fertiliser needed.
- Off site: sediment fills reservoirs and rivers (sedimentation), raising flood risk; muddy water harms aquatic life; coastal sediment smothers coral reefs; dust storms carry particulates that harm health; in dry areas, degradation leads to desertification; on slopes, saturated soils may slide (landslides).

## 3.2.5.3 Reducing soil erosion

### What you need to know

Long-term crops; contour ploughing; tied ridging; terracing; windbreaks; multicropping; strip cropping; mulching; increasing organic matter.

### Explained

| Method | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Long-term crops (trees, perennials) | Soil covered all year; roots bind soil |
| Contour ploughing | Furrows run across the slope, so they hold water instead of channelling it downhill |
| Tied ridging | Ridges along the contour with cross ties, making small basins that trap water |
| Terracing | Steep slopes cut into flat steps, slowing run-off |
| Windbreaks (hedges, tree lines) | Reduce wind speed near the ground |
| Multicropping | Growing more than one crop at once, so the ground is better covered |
| Strip cropping | Bands of different crops (for example a row crop between strips of grass) across the slope; the dense strips catch soil |
| Mulching | Covering soil with straw or residues to absorb raindrop impact, reduce evaporation and add organic matter |
| Increasing organic matter | Compost and manure build stable aggregates and improve infiltration |

### Common mistakes

- Saying ploughing always improves soil. It also speeds organic matter loss and erosion.
- Confusing the USLE factors; remember C and P are the ones farmers control directly.

### Practical

Investigating the effect of slope and vegetation on rain-splash erosion: place trays of soil (bare and with plant cover) at set angles; drip water from a fixed height; collect splashed soil on paper around the tray and weigh it after drying. Repeat for several angles; control drop height, soil type, moisture and volume of water.

# 3.5.1 Agriculture

Agriculture replaces natural ecosystems with managed agroecosystems designed to channel as much energy as possible into a few useful species. This file covers how farmers control the agroecosystem, the energy and productivity of farming, its environmental impacts, the social and economic forces on it, and more sustainable methods. Energy transfer background is in [A-level Biology 17](../../biology/17-energy-in-ecosystems-and-nutrient-cycles.md).

## 3.5.1.1 Agroecosystems

### What you need to know

- Choosing species to suit the environment and technology available.
- Controlling biotic factors: cultural pest control, predators, biological control, sterile males, pheromone traps, genetic resistance.
- Pesticide properties (toxicity, specificity, persistence, solubility, contact or systemic action); insecticide groups (organochlorines, organophosphates, pyrethroids, neonicotinoids).
- Antibiotics in livestock (growth promotion, prevention, treatment).
- Protecting pollinators; soil biota (detritivores, decomposers).
- Food chain energy losses and food conversion ratios (FCRs).
- Controlling abiotic factors: temperature, light, water, nutrients, topography and relief, pH, CO₂, soil fertility, salinity.

### Explained

**Species selection**: crops and livestock are chosen for the local climate and soil and the machinery and inputs available (rice in flooded paddies; drought-tolerant sorghum in dry areas; sheep on steep upland pasture).

**Biotic control** (pests, weeds and diseases):

| Method | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Cultural control | Farming practice that disrupts pests: crop rotation, timing of sowing, removing crop residues |
| Natural predators | Encourage ladybirds, hoverflies, ground beetles with field margins and beetle banks |
| Biological control | Release a natural enemy (for example parasitic wasps against whitefly in glasshouses) |
| Sterile male technique | Release many males sterilised by radiation; females mate but lay infertile eggs |
| Pheromone traps | Sex attractants lure males to traps, or confuse mating |
| Genetic resistance | Breeding or GM crops that resist pests or disease |

**Pesticides**: properties as in [17](17-oil-pesticides-nutrients-and-metals.md). Groups:

- Organochlorines (DDT): persistent, lipid-soluble, biomagnify; now banned in most countries.
- Organophosphates: less persistent but highly toxic to nervous systems, including in humans.
- Pyrethroids: synthetic versions of a natural plant insecticide; low toxicity to mammals but very toxic to fish and aquatic insects.
- Neonicotinoids: systemic; taken into all tissues including pollen and nectar; linked to harm to bees, so outdoor use is restricted in the UK and EU.

**Antibiotics**: given to livestock to treat disease, to prevent it in crowded conditions, and in the past to speed growth. Overuse selects for resistant bacteria that can spread to humans. Growth promotion is banned in the UK and EU.

**Pollinators**: maintained by flower-rich margins and hedges, nest sites, and reducing harmful pesticides.

**Soil biota**: earthworms (detritivores) mix and aerate soil; bacteria and fungi (decomposers) recycle nutrients. Ploughing and pesticides reduce them.

**Energy losses and FCR**: only about 10% of energy passes from one trophic level to the next; the rest is lost in respiration, heat, excretion and uneaten parts. Eating plants directly feeds more people per hectare than eating animals. FCR = mass of feed ÷ mass gain of animal (lower is more efficient). Poultry and fish have lower FCRs than beef cattle because they are smaller-boned, and fish are ectotherms (no energy used keeping warm).

**Abiotic control**: glasshouses raise temperature and CO₂ and add light; irrigation and drainage control water; fertilisers supply nutrients; terracing alters topography; liming controls pH; leaching or drainage controls salinity.

### Worked example

A pig gains 90 kg after eating 270 kg of feed. FCR = 270 ÷ 90 = 3.0. A chicken with FCR 1.7 needs 1.7 × 90 = 153 kg of feed for the same gain, 117 kg less.

## 3.5.1.2 Genetic manipulation and agricultural energetics

### What you need to know

- Selective breeding, cloning, genetic modification (GM).
- Productivity, efficiency, intensive versus extensive farming, energy subsidies, energy ratios.
- Stocking density and monocultures.

### Explained

- **Selective breeding**: choosing parents with desirable traits over many generations (high-yield wheat, dairy cattle). Slow, limited to traits within the species, can reduce genetic diversity.
- **Cloning**: making genetically identical copies (cuttings, tissue culture, embryo splitting). Keeps desirable traits exactly but gives no variation, so a disease can wipe out the whole crop.
- **GM**: transferring genes between species (pest-resistant Bt crops, herbicide-tolerant crops, vitamin-enriched rice). Fast and precise; concerns include gene flow to wild relatives, effects on non-target insects, and corporate control of seed.

**Energetics**:

- Productivity: output per hectare (or per animal) per year.
- Intensive farming uses high inputs (fertiliser, pesticides, machinery, housing) to get high output per hectare. Extensive farming uses low inputs over large areas with lower output per hectare.
- Energy subsidy: energy added by humans (fuel, fertiliser manufacture, machinery, transport) on top of solar energy.
- Energy ratio = energy in food produced ÷ energy subsidy input. Traditional systems often have ratios well above 1; intensive systems (especially intensive livestock and glasshouse crops) may have ratios below 1, using more fossil energy than they produce as food.

**Stocking density**: more animals per area gives more output but increases disease, stress, pollution from manure and overgrazing.

**Monocultures**: one crop over large areas allows efficient machinery but provides ideal conditions for specialist pests and diseases, reduces wildlife, and needs more pesticides and fertiliser.

### Worked example

A wheat farm uses 18 GJ ha⁻¹ of fuel, fertiliser and machinery energy and produces 7 tonnes ha⁻¹ of grain containing 15 GJ t⁻¹. Energy ratio = (7 × 15) ÷ 18 = 105 ÷ 18 ≈ 5.8.

## 3.5.1.3 Environmental impacts of agriculture

### What you need to know

Habitat clearance, draining wetlands, ploughing grassland, genetic contamination, soil erosion; pesticides, nutrients, greenhouse gases (CH₄, CO₂, N₂O); unsustainable irrigation and changes to evapotranspiration.

### Explained

- Habitat loss: hedgerow removal, drained wetlands, ploughed old grassland, cleared forest.
- Genetic contamination: genes from GM or highly bred crops spread to wild relatives.
- Soil erosion and compaction (see [11](11-soils.md)).
- Pesticides kill non-target species; nutrients cause eutrophication.
- Greenhouse gases: CH₄ from ruminants and rice paddies; CO₂ from fuel, ploughing and clearance; N₂O from fertilised soils.
- Irrigation can lower water tables, empty rivers and lakes, and cause salinisation. Replacing forest with crops changes evapotranspiration and so local rainfall.

## 3.5.1.4 Social, economic and political factors

### What you need to know

Consumer choice (social, cultural, religious, ethical); subsidies, guaranteed prices and quotas; trade and economic controls.

### Explained

- Consumer choices change demand: dietary rules of different faiths, vegetarianism, demand for organic, free-range or locally grown food, and concern about food miles.
- Subsidies (payments to farmers), guaranteed minimum prices and quotas shape what is grown. Past production subsidies encouraged overproduction and intensification; newer schemes pay for environmental management (hedgerows, margins, wetlands).
- Trade rules, tariffs and world prices affect whether farming is profitable; cheap imports can undercut sustainable local farms.

## 3.5.1.5 Sustainable agriculture

### What you need to know

Less pesticide and antibiotic use; cultural pest control (weeding, mulching, rotation, barrier crops, companion crops, habitats for predators, polyculture); integrated pest management; natural nutrient supply (nitrogen fixers, decomposition, rotation, recycling organic matter, permaculture, legumes, protecting soil biota); low tillage; water management; social impacts in rural areas.

### Explained

- Integrated pest management (IPM): monitor pest numbers and act only above a threshold, using cultural and biological methods first and selective pesticides as a last resort.
- Crop rotation breaks pest and disease cycles and, with legumes, adds nitrogen.
- Barrier crops block pest movement; companion crops repel pests or attract predators (for example strong-smelling plants beside vegetables).
- Polyculture (several crops together) reduces pest spread and uses resources more fully.
- Permaculture designs farms as self-sustaining systems copying natural ecosystems (perennials, layered planting, recycling all wastes).
- Low (or zero) tillage leaves residues on the surface, protecting soil, saving fuel and keeping soil biota and organic matter.
- Water management: drip irrigation, rainwater harvesting, mulches to cut evaporation.
- Rural social impacts: sustainable methods are often more labour-intensive, which can support rural jobs, though yields may be lower.

### Common mistakes

- Saying an energy ratio below 1 means a farm makes a loss. It means more energy (mostly fossil) is put in than is harvested as food energy.
- Saying GM and selective breeding are the same.

# Oil, pesticides, nutrients, acid mine drainage and heavy metals

These pollutants mainly affect water, soil and food chains. Use the property terms from [15](15-pollutant-properties-and-control-principles.md) when explaining their effects.

## 3.4.3.2.7 Oil pollution

### What you need to know

- Sources: disposal of waste oil, tanker tank washing, tanker and other shipping accidents, refinery spills, pipeline leaks, drilling.
- Effects: toxicity, asphyxiation, loss of insulation, food chain effects.
- Controls: recycling, maintenance, bund walls, double hulls, twin engines, inert-gas systems in tanks, recirculating washing water, GPS navigation, offshore routes, oil interceptors.
- Clean-up: booms, skimmers, absorbents, polymerising materials, dispersants, steam cleaning, bioremediation.

### Explained

Most oil reaching the sea comes from many small routine releases (waste oil poured down drains, washing tanks at sea, run-off) rather than famous tanker disasters.

**Effects**: oil floats, forming a slick. Toxic light fractions kill plankton and larvae. Oil smothers shore organisms and coats gills (asphyxiation). It mats feathers and fur so birds and sea otters lose insulation and buoyancy, and die of cold; birds swallow oil while preening. Toxins pass along food chains.

**Prevention**:

| Control | How it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Recycling waste oil | Less dumped |
| Maintenance | Fewer leaks from pipelines and engines |
| Bund walls | Walls around storage tanks contain spills |
| Double hulls | An inner hull stays intact if the outer hull is holed |
| Twin engines | A ship can still steer if one fails |
| Inert-gas systems | Tanks filled with non-flammable gas, reducing explosion risk |
| Recirculating washing water | Oily water from tank washing kept aboard, not discharged |
| GPS navigation and offshore routes | Fewer groundings; accidents away from coasts |
| Oil interceptors | Tanks in drains where oil floats and is skimmed off before water leaves |

**Clean-up**:

- Booms: floating barriers that contain a slick.
- Skimmers: collect oil from the surface.
- Absorbents (straw, peat, synthetic pads) soak up oil.
- Polymerising materials turn oil into a rubbery solid that is easy to remove.
- Dispersants break oil into small droplets that mix into the water and are degraded faster, but they can be toxic themselves and spread oil through the water column.
- Steam cleaning of rocks removes oil but also kills shore organisms.
- Bioremediation: adding nutrients (or bacteria) to speed natural breakdown by oil-eating bacteria.

## 3.4.3.2.8 Pesticides

### What you need to know

- Properties: toxicity, systemic or contact action, specificity, persistence, lipid solubility, bioaccumulation, biomagnification, mobility, synergism.
- Direct effects, effects of concentration through food chains, and indirect effects.
- Restrictions on organochlorines and organophosphates.

### Explained

- Contact pesticides kill organisms they touch; systemic pesticides are absorbed and carried in the plant, so they kill insects feeding on it (and may reach pollen and nectar).
- Broad-spectrum (low specificity) pesticides kill non-target species, including predators of pests and pollinators.
- Persistent, lipid-soluble pesticides (organochlorines such as DDT) bioaccumulate and biomagnify. DDT in birds of prey (peregrine falcons, sparrowhawks) caused thin eggshells, breeding failure and population crashes.
- Mobile, water-soluble pesticides reach rivers and groundwater.
- Indirect effects: herbicides remove weeds that insects and birds feed on, so populations fall without being poisoned.
- Organochlorines are banned or severely restricted in most countries; organophosphates (toxic to the nervous system of humans and wildlife) are increasingly restricted. Neonicotinoids are discussed in [19](19-agriculture.md).

## 3.4.3.2.9 Nutrients

### What you need to know

- Inorganic nutrients (nitrates, phosphates): eutrophication; methaemoglobinaemia ("blue baby syndrome") and possible carcinogenic effects of nitrate; controls (low-solubility fertilisers, Nitrate Vulnerable Zones, removing phosphate by precipitation with iron).
- Organic nutrients: sources (sewage, manure, silage effluent, wood and paper industry waste); deoxygenation; sewage treatment stages.

### Explained

**Inorganic**: eutrophication is described in [10](10-biogeochemical-cycles.md). Nitrate in drinking water is converted in infants' guts to nitrite, which reduces blood's oxygen-carrying ability; nitrates may form carcinogenic nitrosamines. Controls: slow-release, low-solubility fertilisers; Nitrate Vulnerable Zones where farmers must follow rules on timing and amount of fertiliser and manure; precipitating phosphate at sewage works by adding iron salts.

**Organic**: organic matter is food for aerobic bacteria, which use oxygen as they decompose it. The **Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD)** measures this. High-BOD effluent (silage effluent is extremely high) deoxygenates rivers, killing fish and invertebrates. Downstream of a discharge there is an oxygen "sag" that recovers as the river reaerates.

**Sewage treatment**:

1. Preliminary treatment: screens remove large objects; grit traps let sand and grit settle.
2. Primary sedimentation: slow flow lets suspended solids settle as sludge.
3. Secondary (biological) treatment: aerobic microorganisms break down dissolved organic matter, either in activated sludge tanks (air bubbled through) or trickling filter beds (sewage sprayed over stones coated with microbes). Then secondary sedimentation removes the microbial sludge.
4. Tertiary treatment (where needed): phosphate removal by iron salts; microfilters; UV disinfection.
5. Sludge treatment: anaerobic digestion produces methane (biogas) for energy and a stable residue used as fertiliser.

## 3.4.3.2.10 Acid mine drainage

### What you need to know

Oxidation of sulfides; low pH and mobilisation of metals; collection and neutralisation with lime.

### Explained

Mining exposes sulfide minerals (especially pyrite, FeS₂) to air and water. They oxidise (helped by bacteria) to form sulfuric acid. The acid water dissolves toxic metals (iron, copper, zinc, cadmium, aluminium). When it reaches rivers, iron precipitates as orange ochre that smothers the bed, and acidity and metals kill life. It can continue for centuries after a mine closes, especially when pumping stops and old workings flood. Control: collect drainage, neutralise with lime (raising pH so metals precipitate), and settle the precipitate in lagoons; or pass water through constructed wetlands.

## 3.4.3.2.11 Heavy metals

### What you need to know

- Lead: neurotoxicity, lipid solubility, bioaccumulation and biomagnification; uses that have been stopped; storing waste at high pH.
- Mercury: the same properties; its chemical form (elemental, inorganic or organic) affects its toxicity.

### Explained

**Lead** damages the nervous system, especially in children (lower IQ, behaviour problems). Past sources: leaded petrol, lead paint, lead water pipes, lead shot and fishing weights. These uses have been phased out. Lead waste is stored at high pH, where lead compounds are insoluble and do not leach.

**Mercury**: elemental mercury vapour is toxic when breathed; inorganic mercury salts damage the kidneys; organic methylmercury, formed by bacteria in sediments, is the most dangerous. It is lipid-soluble, crosses the blood-brain barrier and the placenta (teratogenic), bioaccumulates and biomagnifies in fish. Minamata (Japan) in the 1950s is the classic example of poisoning from eating contaminated fish. Sources: coal burning, gold mining, industrial processes.

### Common mistakes

- Saying sewage treatment removes nitrate in the secondary stage. Standard secondary treatment removes organic matter; nutrient removal is tertiary.
- Treating all mercury as equally toxic.

# Geohazards in the British Isles

The British Isles lie far from plate boundaries, yet geohazards cost a lot of money and occasionally lives. The most costly is shrinking and swelling of clay soils. This file covers clays, subsidence, landslides and other mass movements, slope monitoring and stabilisation, and evidence of past tsunamis. Small British earthquakes are covered in principle in [19](19-earthquakes-and-geohazard-risk.md).

## Shrinking and swelling clays (OCR 6.1.3(a); Eduqas T1.1c)

### What you need to know

- Causes of physical and chemical change in clays in excavations: comparing kaolinite and smectite; dewatering, oxidation, compaction; forces from interaction with groundwater.
- Effects on structures and engineering solutions.

### Explained

Clay minerals are sheet silicates. **Smectite** (montmorillonite) has weak bonds between its sheets, so water molecules enter between them: it swells greatly when wet and shrinks when dry. **Kaolinite** has strongly bonded layers that water cannot enter, so it barely swells. Clays rich in smectite (for example the London Clay, which also contains illite and smectite) are high-shrink-swell soils.

**Causes of volume change**:

- Seasonal wetting and drying: clay shrinks in dry summers and swells in wet winters.
- Trees and shrubs extract water by their roots, drying clay near foundations; removing trees lets the clay swell (heave).
- Dewatering for excavations or leaking drains changes moisture.
- Oxidation of pyrite in some clays forms sulfuric acid and gypsum, which can expand and attack concrete (sulfate attack).
- Compaction of loose fills.

**Effects**: shallow foundations move unevenly (differential movement), causing cracked walls, sticking doors and broken pipes. Swelling clays exert large uplift pressures on floor slabs.

**Engineering responses**: deeper foundations (piles below the zone of seasonal moisture change); reinforced raft foundations; compressible void formers under slabs to absorb heave; controlling vegetation near buildings (or not removing old trees suddenly); sulfate-resisting concrete; good drainage.

## Subsidence (OCR 6.1.3(b); Eduqas T1.1c)

### What you need to know

Causes and effects of subsidence: shrinking clays, limestone (solution sinkholes) and mining subsidence; engineering to mitigate effects.

### Explained

| Cause | Process | Mitigation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shrinking clays | Drying clay contracts | See above |
| Solution of limestone, chalk, gypsum or salt | Groundwater dissolves soluble rock, forming cavities; the roof or overlying soil collapses (sinkholes, dolines). Leaking water mains and altered drainage speed it up. Gypsum (Ripon) and salt (Cheshire, where brine pumping caused subsidence) dissolve fastest | Site investigation (boreholes, geophysics), grouting cavities, rafts, controlling drainage |
| Mining | Collapse of old shallow workings (bell pits, pillar-and-stall coal mines) produces crown holes; longwall mining causes a broad, predictable trough of subsidence that travels with the face | Mine plans and shaft records; drilling and grouting old workings; flexible structures; compensation schemes |

## Landslides and mass movements (OCR 6.1.3(c); Eduqas T1.1b, T1.2d, T1.2e)

### What you need to know

- Mechanisms and triggers of rock avalanches, landslides and debris flows: slope angle, lithology, weathering, load, groundwater, rainfall, ground vibration, vegetation cover.
- Characteristics and controls of slope failure in competent and incompetent rocks.
- Monitoring potential failures: ground levelling and surveying; microseismic events; borehole distortion (inclinometers); ground deformation (creep, strain, tilt); groundwater pressures (piezometers); electronic distance measurement (EDM), satellite and GPS.
- Stabilisation: slope stabilisation, drainage control, retaining structures.

### Explained

A slope fails when the downslope force (shear stress, from the weight of material) exceeds the resisting shear strength (friction and cohesion). Factor of safety = resisting forces ÷ driving forces; failure when it falls below 1.

**Factors**:

- Slope angle: steeper means more driving force.
- Lithology and structure: in competent rocks (sandstone, limestone, granite), failure is controlled by discontinuities: rockfalls, planar slides along bedding or joints that dip out of the slope, and wedge failures where two joint sets intersect. In incompetent rocks (clay, shale), failure is through the material: rotational slumps on curved slip surfaces, and flows.
- Weathering weakens rock and clay.
- Load: building on the top of a slope or dumping spoil adds weight.
- Groundwater and rainfall: water adds weight and raises pore-water pressure, which pushes grains apart and reduces friction (effective stress falls). Most landslides follow heavy or prolonged rain.
- Ground vibration: earthquakes, blasting, traffic.
- Vegetation: roots bind soil and plants take up water; clearing them reduces stability.
- Undercutting by rivers or the sea removes support at the toe.

**Types**: rockfalls (free-falling blocks), rock avalanches, translational slides, rotational slumps, debris flows and mudflows (water-rich, fast), and slow creep.

British examples: coastal landslides on clay and soft rock cliffs (for example the Isle of Wight, Dorset and Yorkshire coasts), mine spoil heap failure (Aberfan 1966: a colliery waste tip on a spring became saturated and flowed onto a school).

**Monitoring**: survey markers and levelling; GPS and EDM measure movement to millimetres; satellite radar (InSAR) maps slow movement over large areas; tiltmeters, strain meters and creep meters; inclinometers in boreholes show the depth of the slip surface; piezometers measure groundwater pressure; microseismic sensors detect cracking. Rising pore pressure and accelerating movement warn of failure.

**Stabilisation**:

- Drainage: surface drains, deep drains and pumping to lower pore pressure (often the most effective).
- Regrading: reducing slope angle or removing weight from the top; adding weight (a berm) at the toe.
- Retaining structures: retaining walls, gabions, piles.
- Rock bolts and anchors pinning blocks to stable rock behind; wire mesh, shotcrete on rock faces.
- Vegetation.
- Toe protection from erosion (sea walls, rock armour).

### Worked example

Simple rock slope stability: a block on a bedding plane dipping out of the face at 35°. If the friction angle of the surface is 30°, the block will slide when dry (dip > friction angle), unless cohesion holds it. If the bedding dipped at 25°, it would be stable when dry, but raised water pressure could reduce effective friction and cause failure.

### Practical

**OCR PAG9**: investigate slope angle at failure for different materials and water content in a tray model; measure friction angles of rock surfaces with a tilt table.

## Tsunamis in Britain's past (OCR 6.1.3(d); Eduqas T1.1d)

### What you need to know

Geological evidence for significant tsunamis in the recent geological past, their causes and future risk, including the Storegga Slide.

### Explained

About 8150 years ago a huge submarine landslide on the continental slope off Norway (the Storegga Slide) caused a tsunami that struck the coasts of Norway, Shetland and eastern Scotland. Evidence: a layer of marine sand with shells and microfossils inland within peat and estuary muds, tapering inland; run-up heights reached over 20 m in Shetland. The slide was probably triggered by an earthquake on slopes destabilised by rapid sediment build-up after the ice age, possibly with gas hydrate release.

Other events: historical records and sand layers suggest the 1755 Lisbon earthquake tsunami reached south-west England; there is debate over a possible 1607 Bristol Channel flood (storm surge or tsunami).

Future risk: large slides on the Norwegian margin are thought unlikely in the near future since that sediment has gone; other sources include Atlantic earthquakes and volcanic flank collapse in the Canary Islands (debated). Risk to Britain is low probability but high impact.

### Common mistakes

- Saying kaolinite is a swelling clay.
- Saying water "lubricates" slopes as the main cause of failure. The main effect is raised pore pressure reducing effective friction, plus added weight.

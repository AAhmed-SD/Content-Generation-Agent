# Earthquakes and geohazard risk

Earthquakes are a complex geohazard. Their impact depends on the energy released, how seismic waves travel and are modified by local ground, and how buildings respond. This file also covers how risk is analysed, forecast and communicated. How earthquakes are generated (elastic rebound) is in [11](11-rock-deformation-and-structures.md); mass movements and UK hazards in [20](20-geohazards-in-the-british-isles.md).

## Factors affecting earthquake impact (OCR 6.1.1(a); Eduqas T1.1a)

### What you need to know

- Relationship between earthquakes and active fault zones.
- Absorption and attenuation of energy as waves travel.
- Moment magnitude (Mw), using given equations such as Mw = (2/3) log₁₀E − 6.1 (OCR wording; use whatever equation is given). Logarithmic scales.
- Modified Mercalli intensity scale: depends on size, depth, distance, ground conditions and building standards.
- Seismic hazards: ground shaking, liquefaction, tsunamis (after undersea earthquakes, landslides or eruptions).

### Explained

**Magnitude** measures the energy released at the source. Moment magnitude is based on the seismic moment (fault area × average slip × rock rigidity), so it works for very large earthquakes. It is logarithmic: each whole step is about 32 times more energy (and 10 times more ground motion amplitude on a seismogram).

**Intensity** measures the effects at a particular place (Modified Mercalli, I to XII): what people felt and what damage occurred. One earthquake has one magnitude but many intensities.

Intensity is higher when:

- magnitude is larger
- the focus is shallow
- the place is close to the epicentre (energy spreads out and is absorbed with distance: attenuation; high frequencies are lost first)
- local ground amplifies shaking (soft sediments)
- buildings are poorly constructed
- the earthquake strikes when buildings are occupied.

**Tsunamis**: when an undersea fault displaces the sea floor vertically (mainly at subduction zones), the whole water column is lifted. Waves travel across oceans at 500 to 800 km h⁻¹ with low height; near the coast they slow and grow tall. Landslides and volcanic collapses also cause tsunamis.

### Worked example

Using the given equation Mw = (2/3) log₁₀E − 6.1 (E in the units the question states): for Mw 7.0, log₁₀E = (7.0 + 6.1) × 1.5 = 19.65, so E = 4.5 × 10¹⁹. For Mw 6.0, log₁₀E = 18.15. Ratio = 10^(19.65 − 18.15) = 10^1.5 ≈ 32. So one magnitude step is about 32 times more energy.

## Ground conditions and liquefaction (OCR 6.1.1(b))

### What you need to know

- Interaction of seismic energy with the competence of bedrock and soil; effect of velocity change on amplitude.
- Interaction of groundwater with seismic waves: liquefaction.

### Explained

When seismic waves pass from hard bedrock (high velocity) into soft sediment (low velocity), they slow down. To carry the same energy, their amplitude increases, so shaking is stronger. Soft basins can also trap and resonate waves, prolonging shaking. Mexico City (1985) was badly damaged hundreds of kilometres from the epicentre because it stands on soft lake sediments.

**Liquefaction**: loose, saturated, fine sand or silt is shaken; grains try to pack more tightly, but water cannot escape quickly, so pore-water pressure rises until grains are no longer pressed together. The soil loses strength and behaves like a liquid: buildings tilt and sink, sand boils erupt, buried tanks float up, and slopes flow.

### Practical

**OCR PAG2**: model liquefaction by shaking a container of saturated sand with a weight on top; investigate wave speed in different materials with a hammer source and a phone geophone app.

## Reducing impact through engineering (OCR 6.1.1(c); Eduqas T1.2e)

### What you need to know

Civil engineering reduces impact; natural frequency of structures; aseismic building design; reducing ground accelerations; tsunami defences.

### Explained

Every structure has a natural frequency at which it sways most easily. Tall buildings have low natural frequencies; short buildings high ones. If the ground shakes at the building's natural frequency, resonance makes the swaying grow and the building may collapse. Engineers design buildings so their natural frequency differs from the expected ground shaking, and add damping.

Aseismic design:

- base isolation: bearings (rubber and lead layers, sliders) between the building and its foundation reduce the ground acceleration transmitted
- cross-bracing and shear walls to resist sideways forces
- reinforced concrete with steel that can bend without snapping (ductile)
- dampers (large weights or hydraulic dampers) to absorb energy
- symmetrical design without heavy roofs or weak ground floors
- deep foundations into firm ground; avoiding liquefiable sites
- retrofitting older buildings.

Tsunami defences: sea walls, offshore breakwaters, coastal forests and mangroves, vertical evacuation buildings, warning systems and education.

## Seismic hazard analysis and communication (OCR 6.1.1(d); Eduqas T1.2a, T1.2b)

### What you need to know

- Geohazard: the probability of a change in the geological environment of a given magnitude within a specific time period in a given area. Risk: the consequent threat of loss of life or damage to property and infrastructure.
- Risk assessment: nature of the hazard, probability and return period, communication to the vulnerable population.
- Role of geologists in building codes, disaster planning, public education and communication; limitations and usefulness of hazard maps for non-specialists.

### Explained

Risk = hazard × vulnerability (× exposure). A large earthquake in an empty desert is a hazard but a low risk; a moderate earthquake under a crowded city of weak buildings is a high risk. Seismic hazard maps show the probability of exceeding a level of ground shaking (for example, peak ground acceleration with a 10% chance of being exceeded in 50 years). They are used to set building codes and plan emergency services. Limitations: based on the short historical record and known faults; unknown faults and rare large events may be missed; non-specialists may misread probabilities.

## Probabilistic forecasting and return periods (OCR 6.1.2(a); Eduqas T1.2b)

### What you need to know

- Effectiveness and limitations of probabilistic forecasting.
- Calculate probability and return periods from an annual maximum time series: return period T = (n + 1) ÷ m, where n is the number of years of record and m the rank of the event (largest = 1).
- Communicate probability and return periods to non-specialists.

### Explained

A **forecast** gives the probability of an event in a time period (for example a 30% chance of a magnitude 6.7 or larger in 30 years). A **prediction** states a specific time, place and size (deterministic), which is usually not possible for earthquakes.

**Annual maximum series**: list the largest event in each year, rank them from largest (m = 1), and calculate T. Annual probability = 1 ÷ T. The probability of at least one event in N years = 1 − (1 − 1/T)^N.

### Worked example

A 59-year record. The third-largest annual maximum earthquake was Mw 5.4. T = (59 + 1) ÷ 3 = 20 years. Annual probability = 0.05 (5%). Probability of at least one such event in the next 10 years = 1 − 0.95¹⁰ = 0.40 (40%). Communicate as "about a 2 in 5 chance within 10 years", not "it will happen in 20 years".

Limitations: short records; events are not regular; the assumption that the future resembles the past; return period misunderstood as a timetable.

## Deterministic prediction (OCR 6.1.2(b); Eduqas T1.2c)

### What you need to know

- Seismic gap theory; changes in ground conditions near faults; microseismic activity (including as an eruption predictor); tsunami warning systems.
- Eduqas: monitoring seismic activity, groundwater levels and pressure, ground deformation (creep meters, strain meters, tiltmeters), radon gas emissions, electrical resistivity. Evaluate success.

### Explained

- **Seismic gap theory**: sections of an active fault that have not slipped recently while neighbouring sections have are likely to be storing strain and are the next to rupture. Useful for long-term forecasts but timing is uncertain.
- **Precursors**: foreshocks and microseismicity; ground deformation (tilt, strain, creep, GPS); changes in groundwater levels and well water chemistry; increased radon gas from opening cracks; changes in electrical resistivity of rocks; unusual animal behaviour (anecdotal). None is reliable: precursors often do not occur, or occur without an earthquake.
- **Volcanoes**: microseismic swarms and harmonic tremor predict eruptions more reliably than earthquakes are predicted ([10](10-magma-intrusion-and-volcanism.md)).
- **Tsunami warning systems**: seismometers detect a large undersea earthquake; ocean-bottom pressure sensors and buoys confirm a tsunami; warnings are sent to coasts minutes to hours before arrival. Very effective far from the source, less so close to it.

Overall, earthquake prediction has had very limited success; forecasting, building codes and preparedness save most lives.

## GIS in disaster planning (OCR 6.1.2(c))

### What you need to know

Using geographic information systems to combine geological and geographical data for disaster planning and communication to non-specialists.

### Explained

A GIS overlays layers of mapped data: faults, geology, soil type and liquefaction potential, slope, flood zones, population, buildings, hospitals, roads and schools. Overlaying shows where hazards and vulnerable people coincide, where to place emergency services and evacuation routes, and produces clear maps for planners and the public. Limits: data quality and resolution; maps can give false precision.

### Common mistakes

- Saying magnitude 8 is twice as strong as magnitude 4.
- Confusing magnitude (energy at source) and intensity (effects at a place).
- Treating a 100-year return period as meaning "next one in 100 years".

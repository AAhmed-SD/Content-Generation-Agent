# Minerals, silicate structures and mineral identification

Minerals are the building blocks of rocks. This file covers what a mineral is, how silicate minerals are built, how to identify minerals in hand specimen and thin section, how to measure density and hardness, and how rocks are grouped. The crust's eight main elements and the Goldschmidt classification are in [08](08-origin-and-geochemistry-of-the-earth.md).

## What a mineral is (OCR 2.1.1(a); Eduqas F1.1e)

### What you need to know

- A mineral is a naturally occurring, inorganic, crystalline element or compound with a composition that can be written as a chemical formula (within a limited range) and an ordered atomic structure.
- Minerals have distinct compositions, structures and physical properties by which they can be identified.
- OCR examples with formulae: native sulfur (S), native copper (Cu), calcite (CaCO₃), quartz (SiO₂), pyrite (FeS₂), galena (PbS). Other formulae need not be recalled.

### Explained

- Native elements: one element on its own (sulfur, copper, gold, graphite, diamond).
- Compounds: oxides (haematite Fe₂O₃), sulfides (pyrite, galena, chalcopyrite CuFeS₂), carbonates (calcite), sulfates (gypsum CaSO₄·2H₂O, barite BaSO₄), halides (halite NaCl, fluorite CaF₂) and silicates (most rock-forming minerals).
- "Crystalline" means atoms are arranged in a repeating 3D pattern. This pattern controls crystal shape, cleavage and hardness. Glass (obsidian) has no ordered structure, so it is not a mineral.

## Silicate structures (OCR 2.1.1(b); Eduqas F1.1d)

### What you need to know

- Silicates are the most common rock-forming minerals.
- They are built from silicon-oxygen tetrahedra (SiO₄)⁴⁻: one silicon atom surrounded by four oxygen atoms.
- Tetrahedra may be isolated (olivine, garnet), linked in single chains (pyroxene, for example augite), double chains (amphibole, for example hornblende), sheets (micas, clays) or 3D frameworks (quartz, feldspars).
- Compositions can vary (qualitative only): ions of similar size and charge substitute for one another.

### Explained

Tetrahedra link by sharing oxygen atoms. The more oxygens shared, the more silica-rich and the more polymerised the structure.

| Structure | Oxygens shared per tetrahedron | Example | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Isolated | 0 | Olivine (Mg,Fe)₂SiO₄, garnet | Tetrahedra joined by metal ions (Fe, Mg); dense, no cleavage |
| Single chain | 2 | Pyroxene (augite) | Two cleavages at about 90° |
| Double chain | 2 and 3 alternately | Amphibole (hornblende) | Two cleavages at about 60° and 120° |
| Sheet | 3 | Micas (biotite, muscovite), clays | One perfect cleavage; splits into thin flakes |
| Framework | 4 | Quartz, feldspars | Strong in all directions; quartz has no cleavage, feldspars have two |

Cleavage forms along the weakest bonds, which in silicates are the bonds between the tetrahedral units and metal ions, not the strong Si–O bonds.

Substitution: Fe²⁺ and Mg²⁺ are similar in size, so olivine ranges from Mg-rich to Fe-rich. In feldspars, Na⁺ + Si⁴⁺ substitute for Ca²⁺ + Al³⁺ (plagioclase). More in [15](15-igneous-petrology.md).

### Common mistakes

- Saying micas cleave because they are soft. They cleave because of the sheet structure.
- Calling quartz a framework of SiO₂ "molecules". It is a continuous network.

## Diagnostic properties and identification (OCR 2.1.1(c)(i), (ii); Eduqas F1.1e, SP1, SP3)

### What you need to know

- Diagnostic properties: colour, lustre, crystal shape (habit), streak, cleavage or fracture, density (relative density), hardness, reaction with cold dilute (0.5 mol dm⁻³) hydrochloric acid.
- Classify minerals from samples, photographs and thin-section diagrams using their diagnostic properties, and use classification keys and data sheets (Eduqas provides a mineral data sheet in Component 1).
- Eduqas list for recognition: quartz, calcite, feldspars (orthoclase, plagioclase), augite, hornblende, olivine, micas (biotite, muscovite), haematite, galena, pyrite, chalcopyrite, fluorite, barite, halite, gypsum, garnet, chiastolite/andalusite.

### Explained

| Property | How to test | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Colour | Look | Often unreliable: impurities change it (quartz can be clear, milky, pink, purple) |
| Lustre | How it reflects light | Metallic (galena, pyrite) or non-metallic: vitreous (glassy), pearly, silky, earthy, resinous |
| Habit | Crystal shape | Cubic (halite, pyrite, galena, fluorite), hexagonal prisms (quartz), rhombs (calcite) |
| Streak | Rub on unglazed white porcelain | Colour of powder is constant: haematite red-brown, pyrite greenish-black, galena grey |
| Cleavage / fracture | Look for flat, repeated planes; count and measure angles | Fracture is irregular breakage; quartz has conchoidal (curved) fracture |
| Hardness | Scratch test against Mohs scale objects | Fingernail about 2.5, copper coin about 3.5, steel about 5.5, glass about 5.5 |
| Density | Heft (feel) or measure | Galena and barite feel heavy |
| Acid | Drop of cold dilute HCl | Calcite fizzes (CO₂ released) |

**Key minerals** (summary):

| Mineral | Key features |
| --- | --- |
| Quartz | Hardness 7, glassy, no cleavage, conchoidal fracture, hexagonal prisms |
| Calcite | Hardness 3, rhombic cleavage (three directions), fizzes in cold acid |
| Orthoclase feldspar | Hardness 6, pink or white, two cleavages at 90° |
| Plagioclase feldspar | Hardness 6, white or grey, fine parallel striations on cleavage faces |
| Augite (pyroxene) | Dark green-black, two cleavages near 90°, stubby crystals |
| Hornblende (amphibole) | Dark, elongate crystals, cleavages at about 60°/120° |
| Olivine | Olive green, glassy, granular, no cleavage |
| Biotite | Black mica, one perfect cleavage, thin flexible sheets |
| Muscovite | Silvery-colourless mica, one perfect cleavage |
| Haematite | Red-brown streak, metallic or earthy, dense |
| Galena | Grey, metallic, cubic, three cleavages at 90°, very dense (about 7.5) |
| Pyrite | Brassy yellow, cubic, hardness 6 to 6.5, greenish-black streak |
| Chalcopyrite | Brassy yellow (often tarnished), softer than pyrite (3.5 to 4), greenish-black streak |
| Fluorite | Cubic, four cleavages (octahedral), hardness 4, often purple or green |
| Barite | White, very dense for a non-metal (about 4.5), tabular crystals |
| Halite | Cubic, three cleavages at 90°, salty taste, hardness 2.5 |
| Gypsum | Hardness 2 (scratched by fingernail), one perfect cleavage |
| Garnet | Twelve-sided rounded crystals, red-brown, hardness 7 to 7.5, no cleavage |
| Chiastolite / andalusite | Elongate crystals; chiastolite shows a dark cross in cross-section |

**Thin section**: in a polarising microscope, minerals are identified by colour in plane-polarised light (pleochroism: biotite changes colour as the stage turns), relief, cleavage, and interference colours and extinction under crossed polars. Quartz is clear with low relief and grey-white interference colours; olivine shows bright interference colours and cracks; plagioclase shows stripes (twinning).

A classification key asks yes/no questions in order (for example "Does it fizz in acid?", "Is it harder than glass?") to reach a name.

## Measuring density and hardness (OCR 2.1.1(c)(iii), (iv); Eduqas SP2)

### What you need to know

- Practical investigations of density and hardness: the Mohs hardness test and the density test.
- Techniques to measure mass, length and volume.

### Explained

**Mohs scale** (relative scratch hardness): 1 talc, 2 gypsum, 3 calcite, 4 fluorite, 5 apatite, 6 orthoclase, 7 quartz, 8 topaz, 9 corundum, 10 diamond. A mineral scratches anything softer than itself. The scale is ordinal: diamond is far more than ten times harder than talc.

**Density** = mass ÷ volume.

1. Measure mass on a balance (dry sample).
2. Measure volume by displacement: lower the sample into water in a measuring cylinder (or a Eureka can) and record the volume change; or weigh the sample suspended in water (apparent loss of mass = mass of water displaced, numerically equal to the volume in cm³).
3. Relative density = density of mineral ÷ density of water.

Improve accuracy: use a larger sample (smaller percentage uncertainty); remove air bubbles; use a cylinder with fine graduations; repeat and average.

### Worked example

A galena sample has mass 45.0 g. Water level rises from 50.0 cm³ to 56.0 cm³. Density = 45.0 ÷ 6.0 = 7.5 g cm⁻³. If the cylinder reads to ±0.5 cm³, each reading carries ±0.5, so the volume uncertainty is ±1.0 cm³ (17%), a large uncertainty; a balance-in-water method would be better.

### Practical

**PAG1 / SP1, SP2, SP3**: identify unknown minerals using a property table and key, testing streak, hardness (fingernail, coin, steel, glass), acid reaction and density. Safety: wear eye protection with acid; handle sharp specimens carefully.

## Rocks and the three rock classes (OCR 2.1.1(d); Eduqas F1.1f, F1.1g, SP4)

### What you need to know

- Rocks are aggregates of one or more minerals, fragments of earlier rocks, or fossils.
- Igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic rocks differ in composition and texture, which reflect their origin, and can be related to temperature and pressure in the rock cycle.
- Eduqas: interpret composition, colour and texture (crystalline or clastic; crystal or grain size and shape; sorting; foliation; mineral alignment; bedding; banding) to decide the origin. Produce scaled, annotated drawings using a hand lens or microscope (SP4).

### Explained

| Feature | Igneous | Sedimentary | Metamorphic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Texture | Interlocking crystals, random orientation | Usually grains (clastic) with cement; may have fossils | Interlocking crystals, often aligned (foliation) or granular |
| Structure | Massive; may have vesicles | Bedding, laminations, cross-bedding | Foliation, banding |
| Formation conditions | Crystallised from melt at high temperature | Formed at surface temperature and pressure | Recrystallised in the solid state at raised temperature and/or pressure |

Magnification = size of image ÷ size of real object. On a drawing, include a scale bar.

### Worked example

A drawing of a crystal is 36 mm long; the real crystal is 4.5 mm. Magnification = 36 ÷ 4.5 = ×8.

### Common mistakes

- Using colour as the main test.
- Confusing streak (colour of powder) with colour.
- Saying a mineral with hardness 6 cannot be scratched by anything at hardness 5.5 or below; correct, but it can be scratched by quartz (7).

## Mark scheme

1. Naturally occurring, inorganic, crystalline solid (1); definite chemical composition / formula (1).
2. Mica has a sheet structure (1); weak bonds between sheets so splits parallel to them (1); quartz is a 3D framework with strong Si–O bonds in all directions so no plane of weakness (1).
3. Calcite fizzes in dilute HCl, quartz does not (1); calcite scratched by a steel knife / hardness 3, quartz scratches glass / hardness 7 (1); calcite has rhombic cleavage, quartz conchoidal fracture (1).
4. Hardness: pyrite harder (about 6), scratches glass / not scratched by knife; chalcopyrite softer (3.5 to 4) (1); chalcopyrite often tarnished iridescent / pyrite forms cubes (1).
5. Mass of water displaced = 4.7 g so volume 4.7 cm³ (1); density = 18.4 ÷ 4.7 = 3.9 g cm⁻³ (1); closer to barite but lower, so possibly impure barite, not quartz (1).
6. Minerals are ranked only by which scratches which (1); intervals between numbers are not equal (1).
7. Igneous: interlocking crystals (1); sedimentary: separate grains held by cement / pore space (1); sedimentary may show bedding / fossils / rounded grains (1).
8. 54 ÷ 12 = 4.5 mm (1).

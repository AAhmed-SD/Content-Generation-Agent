# The changing Earth: continents, climate and sea level

Over geological time the positions of continents, the climate, the composition of the atmosphere and sea level have all changed greatly. This file covers the patterns of change, the Wilson cycle that links them, how they are read from rocks, fossils and isotopes, and how present-day change compares. Quaternary climate (Eduqas option T3) is in [Eduqas 03](../geology-eduqas-a480qs/03-quaternary-geology.md); British examples in [Eduqas 04](../geology-eduqas-a480qs/04-geological-evolution-of-britain.md).

## Long-term changes (OCR 7.1.1(a); Eduqas G3.2a, G3.2b)

### What you need to know

- Changes in the distribution of continents from the Neoproterozoic: breakup of Pannotia; assembly and breakup of Pangaea.
- Long-term changes in climate and atmospheric composition: icehouse-greenhouse cycles; the role of volcanic activity.
- Long-term changes in global sea level.
- The Wilson cycle as a framework, and links to mass extinctions.
- Eduqas: natural changes in greenhouse gases (CO₂ and CH₄) from volcanic activity, rock weathering and warming of methane hydrates.

### Explained

**Continents**: a supercontinent (Pannotia, about 600 million years ago) broke up in the late Neoproterozoic and early Cambrian. The fragments drifted and collided again (closing oceans such as Iapetus) to form **Pangaea** by about 300 million years ago (late Palaeozoic). Pangaea broke up from the Jurassic onward, opening the Atlantic.

**Icehouse and greenhouse**: the Earth alternates between icehouse states (polar ice caps, low sea level: late Ordovician, Permo-Carboniferous, the last 34 million years) and greenhouse states (no permanent ice, high CO₂, high sea level: most of the Mesozoic, especially the Cretaceous).

**Natural controls on CO₂ and CH₄**:

- Volcanic activity releases CO₂; large igneous provinces release huge amounts quickly (warming), while sulfate aerosols from big eruptions cool for a few years.
- Chemical weathering of silicate rocks removes CO₂ (carbonic acid reacts with silicates; carbon ends up in carbonate rocks). Uplift of mountain belts (Himalaya) speeds weathering and may cool the climate.
- Burial of organic carbon (coal, black shales) removes CO₂.
- Warming of the oceans can destabilise methane hydrates on continental slopes, releasing CH₄ (a strong greenhouse gas) as a positive feedback.

**Sea level**: global (eustatic) sea level changes with:

- the volume of ice on land (glacio-eustatic: up to about 120 m change in ice ages)
- the volume of the ocean basins: fast sea-floor spreading makes broad, hot, shallow ridges that displace water, so sea level is high (as in the Cretaceous); supercontinent assembly leaves old, deep oceans, so sea level is low.

**The Wilson cycle** (J. Tuzo Wilson): the opening and closing of ocean basins:

1. Continental rifting (East African Rift).
2. Young narrow ocean (Red Sea).
3. Mature ocean with passive margins (Atlantic).
4. Subduction begins; ocean shrinks (Pacific).
5. Closing ocean (Mediterranean).
6. Continental collision and mountain building (Himalaya), forming a supercontinent.

Then the cycle repeats over a few hundred million years. It links climate and sea level (supercontinent times: low sea level, continental interiors arid; breakup: high sea level, more volcanic CO₂), and habitats: assembly reduces shallow shelf seas and mixes faunas, which may contribute to extinctions; breakup isolates populations and increases diversity.

## Evidence in the rock record (OCR 7.1.1(b); Eduqas G3.3a, G3.3b, G3.3c, G3.3d)

### What you need to know

- Palaeontological evidence (corals, plants); lithological evidence (coal, desert sandstones, evaporites, tillites, reef limestones).
- Oxygen isotopes (¹⁸O and ¹⁶O) and carbon isotopes (¹³C and ¹²C).
- Eustatic sea level change and Vail sea level curves.
- Eduqas: icehouse deposits (for example the Carboniferous) and tropical greenhouse deposits (for example the Cretaceous); oxygen isotopes in fossil shells as a guide to ocean temperature; analyse present oceanic and atmospheric circulation and its climatic effects.

### Explained

**Climate indicators**:

| Evidence | Climate shown |
| --- | --- |
| Reef corals (warm, shallow, clear sea) and reef limestones | Tropical (within about 30° of the equator) |
| Coal (from swamp forests) | Wet, warm to temperate, high plant productivity |
| Land plants: leaf shape (large leaves with smooth edges and drip tips in warm wet climates; serrated edges in cool climates), absence of growth rings (tropics) | Temperature and rainfall |
| Desert sandstones (red, aeolian) and evaporites | Hot and arid (about 15 to 30° latitude) |
| Tillites (lithified glacial till), dropstones in marine muds, striated pavements | Glacial |

Plotting these on palaeogeographic maps shows climate belts that shift as continents move, and global changes when such deposits appear at unusual latitudes.

**Oxygen isotopes**: water containing the lighter ¹⁶O evaporates more easily. In icehouse times, ice sheets store ¹⁶O-rich water, so seawater becomes enriched in ¹⁸O. Also, shells formed in colder water take up relatively more ¹⁸O. So a higher ¹⁸O/¹⁶O ratio (δ¹⁸O) in fossil marine shells (foraminifera) indicates colder water and/or more ice. In ice cores, the opposite: lower δ¹⁸O in the ice means colder air.

**Carbon isotopes**: photosynthesis takes up ¹²C preferentially, so organic matter is rich in ¹²C. When large amounts of organic carbon are buried, seawater and carbonates become enriched in ¹³C (higher δ¹³C). A sudden drop in δ¹³C in carbonates shows a release of ¹²C-rich carbon (from methane hydrates, burning organic matter, or volcanic release through coal), often at extinction events.

**Sea-level curves**: seismic reflection profiles of continental margins show sequences of sediment that onlap (step landward) during rises and offlap during falls. Vail and colleagues used these to construct global sea-level curves through time. They show long-term highs (Cretaceous) and lows, with many shorter cycles. Limitation: local subsidence and uplift can be mistaken for global changes.

**Present circulation**: ocean currents and atmospheric cells move heat from the equator to the poles; the positions of continents and gateways (open or closed seaways) control this. Studying modern circulation helps interpret past climate patterns ([Eduqas 03](../geology-eduqas-a480qs/03-quaternary-geology.md)).

## Snowball Earth (Eduqas G3.3e)

### Explained

The Snowball Earth hypothesis proposes that in the Neoproterozoic (around 720 to 635 million years ago) ice covered all or nearly all of the Earth's surface at least once.

Evidence:

- glacial deposits (diamictites, dropstones) on every continent, including some with palaeomagnetic evidence of low (tropical) latitudes at the time
- "cap carbonates": thick limestones directly on top of glacial deposits, suggesting a sudden switch to hot conditions
- carbon isotope swings showing collapse of biological productivity
- the reappearance of banded iron formations, suggesting oceans sealed from the atmosphere by ice became oxygen-poor.

Mechanism: once ice reached low latitudes, high albedo produced runaway cooling. Volcanic CO₂ then built up over millions of years (no weathering or photosynthesis to remove it) until a strong greenhouse melted the ice rapidly.

Evaluation: some argue for a "slushball" with open tropical water, because life survived; palaeolatitude data and dating are debated.

## Present-day change and the Anthropocene (OCR 7.1.1(c); Eduqas G3.2c, G3.2d)

### What you need to know

- Current rates and scale of environmental and biological change, compared with past geological change, using geochronological principles; evidence for an Anthropocene epoch.
- Eduqas: rates of change differ from those in the past; the Anthropocene is a proposed epoch with no consensus; evaluate the debate.

### Explained

Geological boundaries are defined by changes that can be traced worldwide in rocks (for example a global marker layer, the "golden spike"). Proposed markers for the Anthropocene: plutonium and other radionuclides from nuclear weapons tests (from the 1950s), plastics, fly ash particles, a sharp shift in carbon isotopes and CO₂ in ice, nitrogen isotopes from fertiliser, and biological changes (spread of domestic chickens, species extinctions and invasions).

**Rates**: atmospheric CO₂ has risen faster in the past 150 years than in any known natural change in the ice core record; current extinction rates are far higher than background rates. Past changes of similar size (for example the Palaeocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum, end-Permian) took thousands of years or more.

**Debate**: for: global, synchronous, preservable markers; changes comparable to those defining other boundaries. Against: too short a time to judge permanence; human influence began gradually much earlier (farming, deforestation); possibly better treated as an informal "event". In 2024 the international stratigraphy body voted not to formally adopt an Anthropocene epoch, so it remains informal, and the debate continues.

### Worked example

A δ¹⁸O record from foraminifera rises by 1.5‰ over an interval. If about 1‰ corresponds to roughly 4 °C of cooling (ignoring ice volume), the change suggests cooling of about 6 °C. If part of the change is due to ice growth, the temperature change is smaller. This shows why oxygen isotope data must be interpreted with care.

### Common mistakes

- Reversing the meaning of δ¹⁸O in shells (higher in shells means colder/more ice).
- Saying sea level has only changed because of ice.
- Treating the Anthropocene as an officially adopted epoch.

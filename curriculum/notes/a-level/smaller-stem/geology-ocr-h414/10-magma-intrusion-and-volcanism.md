# Magma, intrusion and volcanism

This file explains why rocks melt in particular settings, how magma rises and forms intrusions, what controls the style of eruption and the shape of volcanoes, and the hazards volcanoes cause. Rock names and textures are in [02](02-igneous-rocks.md); magma evolution in [15](15-igneous-petrology.md).

## Generating magma (OCR 3.2.2(a), (b); Eduqas F2.2c)

### What you need to know

- Solidus (temperature at which a rock starts to melt), liquidus (temperature at which it is fully molten), geotherm, adiabatic conditions.
- Mafic (basaltic) magma by partial melting of mantle at divergent boundaries and hotspots.
- Intermediate (andesitic) and silicic magma at convergent margins where crustal material and water are carried down; magma mixing.
- Eduqas: four settings: divergent margins, convergent margins, mantle plumes, deeply buried lower continental crust in orogenic belts (granite).

### Explained

Normally the geotherm lies below the mantle solidus, so the mantle is solid. Melting needs the rock to cross its solidus. Three ways:

1. **Decompression melting**: hot mantle rises quickly (at ridges and in plumes), so it stays almost as hot (adiabatic: no heat lost) while pressure falls. The solidus falls with lower pressure, so the rising mantle crosses it and partially melts (about 10 to 20%), producing basaltic magma. This happens beneath **divergent margins** and **mantle plumes** (hotspots).
2. **Flux melting (adding water)**: at **convergent margins**, the subducting slab releases water as it is heated. Water lowers the melting point of the overlying mantle wedge, which partially melts to give basaltic magma that rises and interacts with crust to give andesitic magma. Partial melting of the subducted oceanic crust itself can also contribute.
3. **Heating crust**: in **orogenic belts** where continental crust is thickened, the deep crust heats up (radioactive heat, plus heat from basaltic magma underplating it) and partially melts, giving granitic (silicic) magma. Assimilation of crust by mafic magma also makes magmas more silicic.

Partial melting makes a melt richer in silica than its source, because low-melting minerals melt first. So partial melting of peridotite gives basalt; partial melting of basalt or crust gives andesite or granite. **Magma mixing**: when two magmas meet (for example basalt intruding a granite chamber), they can mix to give intermediate compositions, sometimes leaving blobs (enclaves) of one in the other.

### Worked example

A graph shows the continental geotherm and the wet lower-crust solidus crossing at 700 °C. If the geotherm is 25 °C km⁻¹ with 10 °C at the surface, the depth of melting = (700 − 10) ÷ 25 = 27.6 km. Thickening the crust in a collision raises temperatures at that depth, so melting begins.

## Intrusion (OCR 3.2.2(c), (d), (f); Eduqas F2.2b)

### What you need to know

- How magma rises through the crust and affects country rock; diapirs and transgressive sills.
- Major intrusions (plutons, batholiths) and minor intrusions (dykes, sills); chilled and baked margins; metamorphic aureoles.
- Diagnostic characteristics of dykes, sills and lava flows.
- Detecting intrusion with geodetic and geophysical data: harmonic tremor, 3D seismic visualisation, tiltmeters, GPS.

### Explained

Magma is less dense than surrounding rock, so it rises by buoyancy. It moves by:

- **Diapirism**: a large, hot, viscous body (granite) rises like a teardrop, softening and pushing aside the country rock.
- **Stoping**: blocks of roof rock break off and sink into the magma (may remain as xenoliths).
- **Fracture propagation**: fluid basaltic magma forces open cracks, forming dykes (vertical, cut across bedding) and sills (horizontal, along bedding). A **transgressive sill** steps up from one bedding plane to a higher one.

| | Dyke | Sill | Lava flow |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | Sheet, steep, discordant | Sheet, parallel to bedding, concordant | Sheet at the surface |
| Margins | Chilled on both sides; baked rock on both sides | Chilled top and base; baked rocks above and below | Chilled base and top; baked only beneath |
| Top surface | | No weathering; may be transgressive | Often vesicular, weathered, reddened top; fragments of it in the bed above |
| Crystal size | Fine to medium, coarsest in the centre | Medium (dolerite) | Fine, vesicular |

**Plutons and batholiths**: large, coarse-grained bodies (granite) emplaced at depth, with wide metamorphic aureoles. **Chilled margins** are finer-grained edges where magma cooled quickly against cold country rock; **baked margins** are country rock hardened and recrystallised by heat.

**Detecting moving magma**: rising magma causes swarms of small earthquakes and **harmonic tremor** (continuous vibration from fluid movement); 3D visualisation of earthquake locations shows magma paths; **tiltmeters** and GPS detect ground swelling (inflation) as magma accumulates.

## Eruption (OCR 3.2.2(e), (g); Eduqas F2.2e)

### What you need to know

- Changes in magma properties affect buoyancy so magma reaches the surface: exsolution of volatiles, crystal content, recharge and groundwater changes.
- Practical models of magma properties and buoyancy.
- Silica content and temperature control viscosity and ability to release volatiles; qualitative role of OH⁻ ions on silicate polymerisation.

### Explained

**Viscosity** is resistance to flow. Silica tetrahedra link into chains and networks (polymerisation) in the melt; more silica means more linking and higher viscosity. Higher temperature breaks links, lowering viscosity. Dissolved water (as OH⁻) breaks Si–O–Si links, lowering viscosity, until it escapes as gas.

| Magma | SiO₂ | Temperature | Viscosity | Gas content |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Basaltic | about 50% | 1100 to 1200 °C | Low | Low |
| Andesitic | about 60% | 900 to 1000 °C | Medium | Medium to high |
| Rhyolitic | over 70% | 700 to 850 °C | High | High |

**Why eruptions happen**: as magma rises, pressure falls and dissolved gases (H₂O, CO₂, SO₂) come out of solution as bubbles (exsolution). Bubbles reduce bulk density, increasing buoyancy, so magma rises faster. Crystallisation concentrates water in the remaining melt (second boiling). A new batch of hot magma from below (recharge) can heat and stir a chamber and trigger eruption. Groundwater meeting magma flashes to steam (phreatic eruptions).

In low-viscosity basalt, gas escapes easily: gentle (effusive) eruptions. In viscous silicic magma, gas cannot escape, so pressure builds until the magma fragments into ash: explosive eruptions.

### Practical

**OCR PAG3**: model magma viscosity with syrup or wax at different temperatures (time the flow down a slope or the fall of a ball bearing), or model gas exsolution with carbonated drinks or bicarbonate and vinegar in a viscous liquid. Control volume, slope angle and temperature measurement.

## Volcanic landforms (OCR 3.2.2(h); Eduqas F2.2b)

### What you need to know

Viscosity, rate of extrusion, gas content and eruption frequency control landforms: fissure and central eruptions; subaerial and submarine; plateau (flood) basalts, shield, composite and caldera volcanoes; pyroclastic deposits.

### Explained

| Landform | Magma and style | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fissure eruption and lava plateau | Very fluid basalt erupting from long cracks, large volumes | Deccan Traps; Antrim plateau |
| Shield volcano | Fluid basalt from a central vent, gentle slopes | Hawaii |
| Composite (strato) volcano | Andesite; alternating lava and pyroclastic layers, steep cone | Mount Fuji, Etna (mixed) |
| Caldera | Huge explosive eruption empties the magma chamber; summit collapses | Yellowstone, Santorini |
| Submarine eruptions | Pillow lavas, hyaloclastite (shattered glass) | Mid-ocean ridges |

## Volcanic hazards and monitoring (OCR 3.2.2(i); Eduqas F2.2d, F2.2e)

### What you need to know

- Hazards: blast and explosion; ash fall, pyroclastic flows (nuées ardentes) and gases; lava flows; debris flows and mudflows (lahars).
- The hazard depends on composition, viscosity and gas content.
- OCR: plot and interpret isopachyte maps (lines of equal thickness of ash).
- Eduqas: monitoring (ground deformation, gravity and thermal anomalies, gas emissions, seismic activity) and the extent to which risk can be managed.

### Explained

| Hazard | Description | Mitigation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lateral blast | Sideways explosion when a bulging flank collapses | Exclusion zones |
| Ash fall | Fragments fall over large areas; roofs collapse, crops buried, aircraft engines damaged, breathing problems | Strengthened roofs, masks, flight bans |
| Pyroclastic flow (nuée ardente) | Hot (up to 700 °C), fast (over 100 km h⁻¹) avalanche of gas and ash down slopes | Evacuation only |
| Volcanic gases | CO₂ (can suffocate in hollows), SO₂ (acid rain), fluorine (poisons livestock) | Monitoring, evacuation |
| Lava flows | Usually slow; destroy property | Diversion barriers, water cooling |
| Lahars | Mudflows of ash and water (rain, melting ice), fast, follow valleys | Hazard maps, warning systems, dams |

Basaltic volcanoes mainly produce lava flows; andesitic and rhyolitic volcanoes produce the most dangerous explosive hazards.

**Isopachyte maps**: lines join points of equal ash thickness. They form elongate oval shapes stretched downwind; thickness decreases away from the vent. The vent lies near the thickest part; the shape shows wind direction; volume can be estimated from the area between isopachs.

**Monitoring**:

- Seismic: earthquake swarms and harmonic tremor as magma moves.
- Ground deformation: tiltmeters, GPS, satellite radar (InSAR) detect swelling.
- Gravity: increase as dense magma accumulates.
- Thermal: infrared satellite images and cameras show heating.
- Gas: increased SO₂ and CO₂ emissions, changes in their ratio.

Monitoring allows forecasting days to weeks ahead for many volcanoes, but timing and size of eruptions are hard to predict, and false alarms erode trust.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the asthenosphere is molten and is the direct source of all magma.
- Saying basaltic eruptions are always harmless. Fissure eruptions can release huge amounts of gas.
- Confusing pyroclastic flows with lava flows.

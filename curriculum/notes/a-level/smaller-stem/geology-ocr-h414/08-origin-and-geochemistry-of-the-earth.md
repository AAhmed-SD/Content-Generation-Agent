# The origin and geochemistry of the Earth

This file covers what the Earth is made of overall, how it formed, where its internal heat comes from, how elements behave chemically (the Goldschmidt classification) and how the Earth separated into compositional layers. Planet formation is also in the GCSE [Astronomy](../../../gcse/smaller-stem/astronomy-edexcel/12-formation-of-planetary-systems.md) notes.

## Bulk composition (OCR 3.1.2(a); Eduqas F1.1b)

### What you need to know

- The bulk composition of the Earth is inferred from chondrite meteorites and the Sun's composition.
- Use normalised diagrams (element concentrations divided by chondrite values), qualitatively.

### Explained

Chondrites are stony meteorites that were never melted. Apart from volatile elements (H, He, C, N, noble gases), their composition matches the Sun's (measured by spectroscopy of sunlight). Because the Sun holds over 99% of the solar system's mass, chondrites are taken as the original solid building material of the planets. The bulk Earth is therefore assumed to be broadly chondritic: mostly Fe, O, Si and Mg, with Ni, S, Ca and Al.

Normalised diagrams: dividing each element's concentration in a rock by its concentration in chondrites gives a ratio. A value of 1 means "same as the primitive Earth"; values above 1 mean the element is concentrated in that rock (crustal rocks are enriched in large ions such as K, U, Th, rare earths); below 1 means depleted (crust is depleted in Ni, Cr and Pt-group elements, which went to the core or stayed in the mantle).

## The nebular hypothesis (OCR 3.1.2(b))

### What you need to know

A qualitative account of how the solar system and Earth formed, with evidence from protoplanetary discs and impact craters.

### Explained

1. About 4.6 billion years ago, a cloud of gas and dust (a nebula) collapsed under gravity, perhaps triggered by a nearby supernova.
2. It spun faster and flattened into a disc; most mass gathered at the centre to form the Sun.
3. In the disc, dust grains stuck together into larger bodies (accretion), then planetesimals (kilometres across), then protoplanets by collisions.
4. Near the Sun it was too hot for ices to condense, so the inner planets are rocky and metallic; further out, ices condensed and giant planets formed.
5. Late, large impacts continued (the Moon probably formed from debris when a Mars-sized body hit the early Earth).

**Evidence**: telescopes image protoplanetary discs around young stars (with gaps cleared by forming planets); impact craters on the Moon, Mercury and Mars record intense early bombardment; meteorites date to about 4.57 billion years; planets orbit in the same direction in nearly the same plane.

## Internal heat (OCR 3.1.2(c); Eduqas F2.2a)

### What you need to know

Geothermal energy comes from:

- heat of formation: gravitational potential energy released by differentiation (dense iron sinking to form the core); latent heat released as the inner core solidifies; kinetic energy of early bombardment
- radioactive decay of K, U and Th.

### Explained

- Accretion: colliding bodies converted kinetic energy to heat, probably melting the outer Earth (a magma ocean).
- Differentiation: as molten iron sank to the centre, gravitational potential energy was converted to heat.
- Inner core growth: as the outer core slowly freezes onto the inner core, latent heat is released, helping drive outer-core convection.
- Radioactive decay: long-lived isotopes ⁴⁰K, ²³²Th, ²³⁵U and ²³⁸U release heat; they are concentrated in the continental crust and mantle. About half of the Earth's surface heat flow today comes from radioactive decay; the rest is primordial heat still escaping.

## Goldschmidt classification (OCR 3.1.2(d); Eduqas F1.1a)

### What you need to know

- Four groups: lithophile, siderophile, chalcophile, atmophile.
- Qualitative understanding of preferred compounds (oxides and silicates, sulfides). Enthalpy and free energy are not needed.

### Explained

| Group | Meaning | Examples | Where concentrated |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lithophile ("rock-loving") | Bond with oxygen, forming silicates and oxides | Si, Al, Na, K, Ca, Mg, U, Th, rare earths | Crust and mantle |
| Siderophile ("iron-loving") | Dissolve in metallic iron | Fe, Ni, Co, Au, Pt, Pd | Core |
| Chalcophile ("copper-loving") | Bond with sulfur, forming sulfides | Cu, Zn, Pb, S, Ag, As, Hg | Sulfide ores, some in core and mantle |
| Atmophile ("vapour-loving") | Gases | H, N, C (as CO₂), noble gases | Atmosphere and hydrosphere |

Some elements fall in more than one group (iron is siderophile, lithophile and chalcophile depending on conditions).

## Differentiation (OCR 3.1.2(e); Eduqas F1.1a, F1.1c)

### What you need to know

- Differentiation into layers of distinct composition and density by partitioning of each Goldschmidt group between core, mantle, crust, atmosphere and hydrosphere.
- Meteorites as a possible source of rare siderophiles in the crust; crustal abundance and concentration factor.
- Eduqas: the crust is mostly eight elements: O, Si, Al, Fe, Ca, Na, K, Mg; silicates are the main rock-forming minerals.

### Explained

When the early Earth was largely molten, dense metallic iron (carrying siderophiles) sank to form the core. The remaining silicate mantle kept lithophiles. Partial melting of the mantle over time extracted the most incompatible lithophiles (K, U, Th, Si, Al, Na) into the crust, which is less dense. Gases (atmophiles) were released by volcanic outgassing to form the atmosphere and, as water condensed, the oceans.

Siderophile elements such as gold and platinum should have gone almost entirely into the core, yet the mantle and crust contain more than expected. One explanation is a "late veneer": meteorites added after core formation brought these elements to the surface.

**Crustal abundance** (approximate, by mass): O 46%, Si 28%, Al 8%, Fe 5%, Ca 4%, Na 3%, K 3%, Mg 2%. Together these make about 98%, so most minerals are silicates of these elements.

**Concentration factor** = concentration of an element in an ore ÷ its average crustal abundance. This links to economic geology ([18](18-mining-geology.md)).

### Worked example

Average crustal abundance of copper is 0.006%. An ore has 0.9% copper. Concentration factor = 0.9 ÷ 0.006 = 150.

## Geochemical layers from direct evidence (OCR 3.1.2(f); Eduqas F4.1a)

### What you need to know

The composition of the layers is inferred from direct evidence: deep mines and boreholes, ophiolites, kimberlite pipes, mantle xenoliths, and seismology (Lehmann, Gutenberg and Moho discontinuities).

### Explained

- Deep mines and boreholes (the deepest reaches about 12 km) sample the upper crust only.
- Ophiolites: slices of oceanic crust and upper mantle thrust onto continents at collision zones (for example Troodos in Cyprus, Oman, the Lizard in Cornwall). They show the full sequence including mantle peridotite.
- Kimberlite pipes: volcanic pipes from very deep (over 150 km) that carry diamonds and mantle fragments to the surface.
- Mantle xenoliths: fragments of peridotite (olivine, pyroxene) carried up in basalt lavas and kimberlites show that the upper mantle is peridotite.
- Meteorites: stony meteorites resemble the mantle; iron meteorites resemble the core.
- Seismic discontinuities mark composition changes: Moho (crust to peridotite mantle), Gutenberg (silicate mantle to iron core), Lehmann (liquid to solid iron).

### Common mistakes

- Saying the core is made of siderophiles "because they are heavy". Siderophiles dissolve in iron metal; that is why they followed it.
- Treating kimberlites as samples of the core. They come from the upper mantle.

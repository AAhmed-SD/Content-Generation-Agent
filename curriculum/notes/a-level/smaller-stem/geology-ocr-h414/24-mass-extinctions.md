# Mass extinctions

Most of the time life changes gradually, but the fossil record shows several short intervals when a large proportion of species died out worldwide. These mass extinctions mark major boundaries in the geological timescale and reset the course of evolution.

## What a mass extinction is (Eduqas F3.2f, G3.1b)

### What you need to know

- The rock record shows long periods of slow change interrupted by sudden catastrophes causing mass extinctions.
- Possible causes: asteroid impact (Alvarez hypothesis), large-scale volcanism (flood basalts), changes in land and sea level, rapid climate change. Evaluate contrasting hypotheses.

### Explained

A mass extinction is the loss of a large proportion (often over half) of species across many groups and environments, worldwide, in a geologically short time. Background extinction continues all the time at a low rate. Five major mass extinctions are usually recognised: end-Ordovician, late Devonian, end-Permian, end-Triassic and end-Cretaceous.

Kill mechanisms often combine: rapid warming or cooling, ocean acidification (CO₂ dissolving), anoxia (low oxygen) in the oceans, collapse of food chains when photosynthesis stops, and loss of habitat when sea level falls.

## The three named extinctions (OCR 7.1.3(a); Eduqas G3.1c)

### End-Ordovician (Ordovician-Silurian boundary, about 444 Ma)

- **Effect**: second largest; about 85% of marine species lost (trilobites, brachiopods, graptolites, corals hit).
- **Cause**: a short, intense glaciation as Gondwana sat over the South Pole. Ice sheets grew, sea level fell sharply, draining the shallow shelf seas where most life lived (habitat elimination); oceans cooled. A second pulse came as the ice melted and sea level rose, with low-oxygen water spreading.
- **Evidence**: tillites and glacial striations in North Africa (then near the pole); a positive oxygen isotope shift (colder, more ice); a sea level fall recorded in shelf sequences; extinction concentrated in warm-water tropical faunas.

### End-Permian (Permian-Triassic boundary, about 252 Ma)

- **Effect**: the largest; over 90% of marine species and about 70% of land vertebrate species lost; trilobites, rugose and tabulate corals, and most brachiopods vanished.
- **Cause**: the Siberian Traps, a huge flood basalt province, erupted millions of cubic kilometres of lava. Magma intruded and baked coal and evaporite deposits, releasing extra CO₂, methane and halogens. Effects: rapid global warming, ocean acidification, ocean anoxia (black shales), possibly ozone damage.
- **Evidence**: radiometric dating of the Siberian Traps matching the extinction; a sharp negative carbon isotope shift (input of ¹²C-rich carbon); black shales and pyrite showing anoxia; oxygen isotopes indicating very high sea temperatures; "coal gap" (no coal for millions of years after, as forests died); fungal spike in some sections.

### End-Cretaceous (Cretaceous-Palaeogene, also called K-T or K-Pg, about 66 Ma)

- **Effect**: about 75% of species lost, including non-bird dinosaurs, ammonites, belemnites, marine reptiles and many plankton.
- **Causes**:
  - An asteroid about 10 km across struck Chicxulub, Mexico (Alvarez hypothesis). Dust and soot blocked sunlight (impact winter, photosynthesis collapsed); sulfur from evaporites in the target rocks produced acid rain and cooling; wildfires; tsunamis.
  - The Deccan Traps (India) flood basalts erupted over the same interval, releasing CO₂ and SO₂ and stressing ecosystems.
- **Evidence for impact**: a thin clay layer worldwide at the boundary rich in iridium (rare in the crust but common in meteorites); shocked quartz (with lamellae formed only by extreme pressures); glassy spherules (tektites) from melted rock; soot; the buried Chicxulub crater (about 180 km wide), dated to the boundary; tsunami deposits around the Gulf of Mexico.
- **Evidence for volcanism**: dating of Deccan lavas shows major eruptions shortly before and after the boundary; some groups were declining before the impact.
- **Evaluation**: the impact timing and global layer are strongly supported; volcanism may have weakened ecosystems or prolonged recovery. Most geologists consider the impact the main trigger.

## Replacement of dominant groups (OCR 7.1.3(b))

### What you need to know

How mass extinctions led to new groups replacing the dominant forms in major habitats.

### Explained

Extinctions remove dominant groups, leaving empty niches. Survivors diversify rapidly (adaptive radiation) to fill them.

| Extinction | Groups lost or reduced | Groups that took over |
| --- | --- | --- |
| End-Permian | Brachiopods, rugose and tabulate corals, trilobites, many synapsids | Bivalves on the sea floor; scleractinian corals as reef builders (Triassic); archosaurs and then dinosaurs on land |
| End-Triassic | Many large amphibians and other reptiles | Dinosaurs became dominant on land in the Jurassic |
| End-Cretaceous | Non-bird dinosaurs, ammonites, marine reptiles | Mammals radiated into large-bodied herbivores and carnivores; birds diversified; teleost fish in the seas |

Survivors are often small, generalist, widely distributed species able to tolerate harsh conditions, not necessarily the "best adapted" before the event.

### Worked example

A section shows 120 genera below a boundary and 45 above, of which 15 are new. Genera surviving = 45 − 15 = 30. Percentage of genera lost = (120 − 30) ÷ 120 × 100 = 75%.

### Common mistakes

- Saying all dinosaurs died out at the end of the Cretaceous. Birds are surviving dinosaurs.
- Saying the iridium layer proves an impact happened in Mexico. The crater does; the layer shows an impact somewhere.
- Treating gradual decline before a boundary as evidence against any catastrophe; both can operate.

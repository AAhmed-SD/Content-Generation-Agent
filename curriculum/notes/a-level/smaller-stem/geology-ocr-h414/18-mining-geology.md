# Mining geology

Metals are present in the crust only at low concentrations. Geological processes must concentrate them before mining is worthwhile. This file covers how ores form, how they are found, how they are mined and processed, and how the environmental effects of mines are managed. Eduqas-specific deposits (residual and precipitated ores, china clay, aggregates, coal) are in [Eduqas 01](../geology-eduqas-a480qs/01-economic-minerals-coal-and-bulk-materials.md). The environmental science view is in [Environmental Science 09](../environmental-science-aqa-7447/09-mineral-resources.md).

## Concentration and key terms (OCR 5.5.1(a))

### What you need to know

Resource, reserve, ore mineral, gangue mineral, average crustal abundance, cut-off grade, concentration factor.

### Explained

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Ore | Rock containing a metal (or mineral) that can be extracted at a profit |
| Ore mineral | The valuable mineral (chalcopyrite for copper, galena for lead, cassiterite for tin, sphalerite for zinc) |
| Gangue | Worthless minerals in the ore (quartz, calcite, barite, fluorite), discarded as waste or sold separately |
| Resource | All of the material that may be extractable now or in future |
| Reserve | The known, economically extractable part |
| Average crustal abundance | Mean concentration of an element in the crust |
| Grade | Concentration of the metal in the ore |
| Cut-off grade | The lowest grade worth mining, depending on price and costs |
| Concentration factor | Ore grade ÷ average crustal abundance |

### Worked example

Lead has a crustal abundance of 0.0013%. If the cut-off grade is 2%, the minimum concentration factor = 2 ÷ 0.0013 ≈ 1540. A lode with 6% Pb is 4600 times enriched.

Tonnage: a vein 400 m long, 2 m wide and mined to 150 m depth, with ore density 3.2 t m⁻³: volume = 400 × 2 × 150 = 120 000 m³; mass = 384 000 t. At 6% Pb, contained lead = 23 040 t.

## Processes that concentrate metals

### Magmatic and hydrothermal (OCR 5.3.1(c), 5.3.2(d); Eduqas G4.1a)

Magmatic segregation (layered intrusions, chromite and PGE layers) is in [15](15-igneous-petrology.md); volcanogenic massive sulfides at ridges in [16](16-mid-ocean-ridges.md).

**Hydrothermal veins** (Eduqas G4.1a): hot, watery fluids released from cooling granite, or groundwater heated by it, dissolve metals and carry them through fractures. As the fluid cools, or its pressure or chemistry changes, minerals precipitate in veins (lodes), often zoned outward from the granite with higher-temperature minerals (tin, tungsten) closest and lower-temperature ones (lead, zinc) further away, as in Cornwall. Gangue (quartz, fluorite, barite, calcite) precipitates too. Map relations: veins follow faults and joints, often radiating from or near a granite.

### Secondary enrichment of copper (OCR 5.5.1(b))

Above the water table, oxygenated rainwater oxidises primary sulfides such as chalcopyrite. Copper dissolves (as copper sulfate in acidic water) and is carried down, leaving a leached, iron-oxide-rich cap (gossan) at the surface. At and below the water table, conditions are reducing, so the copper reprecipitates as secondary copper sulfides (and native copper) on primary minerals. This forms a thin but rich enrichment zone, which can double or treble the grade. Gossans are a useful prospecting clue.

### Placer deposits (OCR 5.5.1(c); Eduqas G4.1a)

Placers form where dense, hard, chemically resistant minerals are weathered out of rocks and concentrated by moving water. Light grains are carried away; heavy grains are dropped where velocity falls: inside meander bends, in hollows and potholes on the river bed, below waterfalls, at the base of gravels, and on beaches sorted by waves. Typical placer minerals: gold (very dense, unreactive, malleable so it does not break up), cassiterite (dense, hard, resistant), diamond (very hard, resistant). Placers are cheap to mine (loose sediment).

## Exploration (OCR 5.5.1(d), (e), (f); Eduqas G4.3a)

### What you need to know

- Geophysical methods: magnetic, gravity (Bouguer), seismic and electrical (EM, resistivity) surveys; choosing methods for different targets; limitations.
- Geochemical methods: sampling and analysing stream (river) water, stream sediment, soil and vegetation.
- Integrating existing data and follow-up surveys: target selection, exploration drilling, estimating reserves, limitations.

### Explained

**Geophysical**:

| Method | Detects | Example target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Magnetic | Variations in magnetic field from magnetic minerals | Magnetite, iron ores, some sulfide bodies, mafic intrusions |
| Gravity (Bouguer anomaly) | Density contrasts | Dense sulfide or iron ore (positive anomaly); low-density granite (negative) |
| Electrical / EM / resistivity | Conductivity | Massive sulfides (highly conductive) |
| Seismic | Layering and structure | Mainly for oil and coal; less useful for irregular ore bodies |

Limitations: anomalies show physical contrasts, not metal content; other rocks can cause similar anomalies (graphite conducts like sulfides); depth resolution is limited.

**Geochemical**: weathering releases metals into streams and soils. Sampling stream sediments upstream along a drainage network finds where anomalous values begin, pointing to the source. Soil grids then narrow down the area. Some plants take up metals (or are indicator species), so analysing vegetation helps. Background concentrations must be established to recognise anomalies.

**Integrating data**: start with existing geological maps and satellite images (identifying favourable rocks and structures); then regional geochemistry and geophysics; choose targets where anomalies overlap; then exploration drilling (cores give grade, thickness and depth); interpolate between boreholes to estimate tonnage and grade. Limitations: sparse boreholes, variable grade, faulting; reserves change with prices.

### Practical

**OCR PAG10**: analyse geochemical and geophysical data sets on maps (contouring anomalies, identifying targets), integrate fieldwork with secondary data.

## Mining and processing (OCR 5.5.2(a), (b); Eduqas T1.3a)

### What you need to know

- Principles, economics and sustainability of surface and underground mining: open pit, stoping, longwall retreat; phased development and life cycle of a mine.
- Mineral processing: in situ and heap leaching, crushing, tailings disposal, froth flotation, smelting.
- Eduqas: problems of extraction: face stability, rock falls, subsidence, flooding, surface and groundwater pollution, waste tipping; ways to minimise them.

### Explained

| Method | Description | Suitable for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open pit | Benches cut downward in a large pit | Large, low-grade deposits near the surface; cheaper per tonne; big visual and land impact; high waste-to-ore ratio |
| Stoping | Underground excavation of steep veins in "stopes" from levels and shafts | Narrow, high-grade, steep vein deposits |
| Longwall retreat | A long face in a flat seam is mined by a cutting machine; roof supports move forward and the roof collapses behind | Flat-lying coal seams; causes surface subsidence |

**Life cycle**: exploration, feasibility, planning and permission, development, production (often in phases), closure, restoration and aftercare. Planning includes restoration from the start.

**Processing**:

- Crushing and grinding release ore minerals from gangue.
- Froth flotation: powdered ore is mixed with water and chemicals that make sulfide grains water-repellent; air bubbles carry them to the surface froth, which is skimmed off; gangue sinks.
- Tailings (fine waste) go into tailings dams; failures of these dams can cause disasters, so design and monitoring matter.
- Smelting: heating concentrates (often with coke) to extract metal; releases SO₂ (now captured to make sulfuric acid).
- Heap leaching: crushed low-grade ore piled on lined pads and sprayed with a solvent (acid for copper, cyanide for gold); metal-rich solution is collected. In situ leaching pumps solvent through the ore underground. Both cheap but risk pollution.

**Problems of extraction and controls**: unstable faces (benching, slope angles, drainage, rock bolts); rock falls (netting, scaling); subsidence (leaving pillars, backfill); flooding (pumping, grouting); pollution (settling lagoons, treatment); waste heaps (stable design, revegetation).

## Minewater (OCR 5.5.2(c); Eduqas T1.3a)

### What you need to know

Contaminated minewater from abandoned coal and metal mines: geochemistry (acidity, dissolved metals), passive and active treatment, barriers and dewatering.

### Explained

When pumping stops after a mine closes, groundwater rises and floods the workings. Pyrite in the rocks, exposed to air during mining, has oxidised; the water dissolves the products, becoming acidic and rich in iron, sulfate and other metals (zinc, cadmium, copper). It discharges at the surface where it overflows, staining rivers orange with iron hydroxide (ochre) and harming life.

- **Active treatment**: pumping and dosing with lime to raise pH, then settling out metal hydroxides; effective but expensive and continuous.
- **Passive treatment**: water flows through aeration cascades and settling ponds, then reed beds (wetlands) or limestone drains, where bacteria and plants help precipitate metals; low running cost, needs land.
- **Barriers and dewatering**: keeping water levels low by pumping or sealing shafts, and reactive barriers in the ground that neutralise water as it passes.

## Old workings as repositories (OCR 5.5.2(d))

### What you need to know

Geological controls on using former underground workings to store waste, including radioactive waste and carbon dioxide.

### Explained

Good repository geology: low permeability host rocks (clay, salt, granite with few fractures) so groundwater moves very slowly; stable, aseismic area; no valuable resources (to avoid future drilling); predictable groundwater flow, with long paths and residence times; impermeable cap rocks above. Old mines raise problems: they are full of open voids, fractures and shafts that provide pathways for fluids, and they may flood. Salt mines are attractive for some wastes because salt is impermeable and creeps to seal cracks. CO₂ is better stored in porous rocks in depleted oil and gas fields beneath good caprocks than in mines ([26](26-oil-and-gas-basins.md)).

### Common mistakes

- Using "ore" for any rock with metal in it, regardless of economics.
- Saying placer deposits contain light minerals.
- Saying gossans are rich in copper. They are the leached zone; copper is enriched below.

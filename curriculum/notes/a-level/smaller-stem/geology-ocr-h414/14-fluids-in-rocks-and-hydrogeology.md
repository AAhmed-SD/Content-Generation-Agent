# Fluids in rocks and hydrogeology

Water, oil and gas are stored in the pore spaces of rocks and move through them. The same principles control water supply, hydrocarbon reservoirs ([26](26-oil-and-gas-basins.md)), pollution and engineering problems ([21](21-engineering-geology.md)). Water resources from an environmental angle are in [Environmental Science 08](../environmental-science-aqa-7447/08-the-hydrosphere.md).

## Porosity and permeability (OCR 5.2.1(a); Eduqas G4.2a)

### What you need to know

- Porosity controls storage of fluids; effects of depth, temperature; primary and secondary porosity.
- Permeability controls flow; effects of diagenesis, fracturing and capillary pressure (oil and water).
- Rock texture: grain size, shape, packing, sorting and cementation.

### Explained

**Porosity** = (volume of pores ÷ total volume) × 100%. **Effective porosity** counts only connected pores.

- Primary porosity: formed at deposition, the spaces between grains.
- Secondary porosity: formed later: fractures and joints, dissolution of grains or cement (for example caves and solution channels in limestone), dolomitisation.

**Controls on porosity**:

| Factor | Higher porosity | Lower porosity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sorting | Well sorted | Poorly sorted (small grains fill gaps) |
| Packing | Loose (cubic) packing, about 48% | Tight (rhombohedral) packing, about 26% |
| Grain shape | Irregular, angular (in loose sediment) | Platy grains compact tightly |
| Cementation | Little cement | Much cement fills pores |
| Depth | Shallow | Deep: compaction and pressure solution reduce porosity |
| Temperature | | Higher temperature speeds cementation and pressure solution |

Grain size does not affect porosity in an ideal packing of equal spheres, but it strongly affects permeability.

**Permeability** is the ability to transmit fluid, measured in darcies (or m²). It needs connected pores, and large pore throats. Clay has high porosity (up to 50%) but tiny pores, so very low permeability. Coarse, well sorted, uncemented sand and gravel and fractured rock have high permeability. Diagenesis (cement, compaction) reduces it; fracturing increases it.

**Capillary pressure**: in a rock with two fluids, the one that wets the grains (usually water) clings to grain surfaces and fills small pores. Oil must overcome capillary pressure to enter small pore throats, so fine-grained rocks with narrow pores act as seals even if they have some porosity.

### Practical

**OCR PAG8**: measure porosity of sediment by filling a known volume with water (volume of water absorbed ÷ total volume), or by weighing dry and saturated rock; measure permeability by timing a fixed volume of water flowing through a column of sediment of known length and area.

### Worked example

A dry sandstone core weighs 220.0 g; saturated it weighs 236.5 g. Its volume is 100 cm³. Water absorbed = 16.5 g = 16.5 cm³. Porosity = 16.5 ÷ 100 × 100 = 16.5%.

## Darcy's law (OCR 5.2.1(b); Eduqas G4.2b)

### What you need to know

- Q = −kA (h₂ − h₁) ÷ L, where Q is the discharge (volume per unit time), k the hydraulic conductivity, A the cross-sectional area, (h₂ − h₁) the difference in hydraulic head and L the flow distance.
- Hydraulic gradient = (h₂ − h₁) ÷ L (OCR: recall the hydraulic gradient but not Darcy's law); hydrostatic pressure.

### Explained

Groundwater flows from high hydraulic head to low head. Head is the height to which water would rise in a well (elevation plus pressure). The minus sign shows flow goes "downhill" in head. Flow rate increases with conductivity, area and gradient.

Hydrostatic pressure at depth h in water: p = ρgh.

### Worked example

An aquifer has k = 15 m day⁻¹, cross-section 2000 m², and head drops from 54 m to 46 m over 800 m. Hydraulic gradient = (46 − 54) ÷ 800 = −0.01. Q = −15 × 2000 × (−0.01) = 300 m³ day⁻¹.

## Hydrogeology (OCR 5.2.1(d); Eduqas G4.2d)

### What you need to know

- Confined and unconfined aquifers, aquicludes, aquitards, the water table, piezometric (potentiometric) surfaces, recharge zones; springs (water) and seeps (hydrocarbons); Eduqas: cones of depression.

### Explained

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Aquifer | A rock that stores and transmits water in useful amounts (porous and permeable: sandstone, chalk, fractured limestone) |
| Aquiclude | Rock that does not transmit water (clay, shale) |
| Aquitard | Rock that transmits water slowly (siltstone) |
| Water table | Top of the saturated zone in an unconfined aquifer; it follows topography in a subdued way |
| Unconfined aquifer | Open to the surface; water table free to rise and fall |
| Confined aquifer | Sandwiched between aquicludes; water under pressure |
| Piezometric surface | The level to which water would rise in wells in a confined aquifer |
| Recharge zone | Where water enters the aquifer, usually its outcrop |
| Artesian well | Well in a confined aquifer where the piezometric surface is above the ground, so water rises (or flows out) without pumping |
| Cone of depression | Lowering of the water table (or piezometric surface) around a pumped well |
| Spring | Where the water table meets the ground surface, often at the base of a permeable rock overlying an impermeable one, or at a fault |
| Seep | Where oil or gas reaches the surface |

The London Basin is a classic artesian system: chalk in a syncline, confined beneath London Clay, recharged where it outcrops in the Chilterns and North Downs.

**Cones of depression**: pumping lowers the water level most at the well and less with distance. Overlapping cones from many wells lower the regional water table, dry up springs, and near coasts can draw in seawater (saline intrusion).

## Groundwater quality (OCR 5.2.1(c); Eduqas G4.2d)

### What you need to know

Controls from geochemistry (carbonates and sulfates), filtration by the aquifer, and residence time; connate fluids in sedimentary basins; sources of pollution.

### Explained

- **Geochemistry**: water dissolves minerals from the rock. Limestone and chalk add calcium and hydrogen carbonate (hard water). Gypsum and anhydrite add calcium sulfate. Pyrite oxidation adds sulfate and iron.
- **Filtration**: fine-grained aquifers trap particles and bacteria, so water from granular aquifers is often clean; fractured limestones filter poorly and pollutants travel quickly.
- **Residence time**: longer contact with rock means more dissolved minerals; long residence also allows natural decay of bacteria and some contaminants.
- **Connate water**: water trapped in sediments when they were deposited, often very old and saline (brines) deep in basins.
- **Pollution**: nitrates from fertiliser, pesticides, leachate from landfill, hydrocarbons from leaking tanks, saline intrusion. Long residence times mean polluted aquifers take decades to clean.

### Common mistakes

- Saying clay is a good aquifer because it has high porosity.
- Confusing the water table with the piezometric surface.
- Forgetting the minus sign or the units in Darcy's law.

# Igneous petrology

Magma does not crystallise all at once. Minerals form in a sequence, and separating crystals from liquid changes the composition of the remaining magma. This explains why one parent basalt can produce a range of igneous rocks and why some intrusions contain valuable ores. Basics of magma generation are in [10](10-magma-intrusion-and-volcanism.md). The decay equation and radiometric ages (Eduqas G1.1a skills) are in [06](06-geological-time-and-dating.md).

## Substitution and solid solution (OCR 5.3.1(a)(i); Eduqas G1.1b)

### What you need to know

- Ions substitute for others in crystal structures when their atomic (ionic) radius and charge (valency) are similar.
- Solid solution series: olivine and plagioclase feldspar.
- Zoned crystals.

### Explained

**Olivine** (Mg,Fe)₂SiO₄: Mg²⁺ and Fe²⁺ have the same charge and similar sizes, so they substitute freely. The series runs from forsterite (Mg₂SiO₄) to fayalite (Fe₂SiO₄). Mg-rich olivine has the higher melting point.

**Plagioclase feldspar**: runs from anorthite (CaAl₂Si₂O₈, calcium-rich) to albite (NaAlSi₃O₈, sodium-rich). Ca²⁺ and Na⁺ are similar in size but differ in charge, so the substitution is coupled: Ca²⁺ + Al³⁺ replace Na⁺ + Si⁴⁺, keeping the charge balanced. Ca-rich plagioclase has the higher melting point.

**Zoned crystals**: if a plagioclase crystal grows while the magma cools, the first-formed core is Ca-rich; later layers are more Na-rich because the liquid has changed. If the crystal does not have time to react with the liquid, concentric zones remain, visible in thin section as bands under crossed polars. Zoning shows the magma cooled too quickly for equilibrium.

## Bowen's reaction series (OCR 5.3.1(a)(i); Eduqas G1.1a)

### What you need to know

Bowen's reaction series: the discontinuous series (mafic minerals) and the continuous series (plagioclase).

### Explained

As a basaltic magma cools, minerals crystallise in a predictable order (worked out by Bowen in experiments in the early 20th century):

- **Discontinuous series** (ferromagnesian minerals): olivine → pyroxene → amphibole → biotite. Each step is a reaction: early crystals react with the liquid to form a new mineral with a different structure (more polymerised silicate structure each time).
- **Continuous series**: plagioclase changes gradually from Ca-rich to Na-rich, keeping the same structure.
- The two series join at the bottom: orthoclase (K-feldspar), muscovite and quartz crystallise last, at the lowest temperatures.

Consequences:

- Minerals at the top (olivine, Ca-plagioclase) are typical of mafic and ultramafic rocks (high temperature); minerals at the bottom (quartz, orthoclase, muscovite) of silicic rocks.
- Olivine and quartz do not normally occur together.
- Weathering: minerals that form at high temperatures are least stable at the surface (olivine weathers fast; quartz is resistant).

## Binary phase diagrams (OCR 5.3.1(a)(ii); Eduqas G1.1b)

### What you need to know

Interpret continuous and discontinuous binary phase diagrams: anorthite-albite (plagioclase), forsterite-fayalite (olivine), diopside-anorthite (eutectic).

### Explained

A binary phase diagram plots temperature against composition for a two-component system.

**Continuous (solid solution) diagram: albite-anorthite**

- The upper curve is the **liquidus**: above it, all liquid. The lower curve is the **solidus**: below it, all solid.
- A melt of composition An₅₀ (50% anorthite) cools to the liquidus; the first crystals are richer in anorthite (read horizontally across to the solidus: perhaps An₈₀).
- As cooling continues, crystals and liquid both become richer in albite. If crystals keep reacting with the liquid (equilibrium crystallisation), the final solid has the starting composition An₅₀.
- If crystals are removed or do not react (fractional crystallisation), the liquid becomes ever more albite-rich, and zoned crystals form.
- The forsterite-fayalite (olivine) diagram has the same lens shape.

**Eutectic diagram: diopside-anorthite**

- Two minerals that do not form a solid solution. The liquidus has two branches falling to a lowest point, the **eutectic**.
- A melt on the diopside-rich side first crystallises pure diopside as it cools; the liquid becomes richer in anorthite and moves down the liquidus to the eutectic.
- At the eutectic temperature both minerals crystallise together until all is solid. The eutectic is the lowest temperature at which any liquid can exist; partial melting of a diopside-anorthite rock gives first a melt of eutectic composition.

### Worked example

On an An-Ab diagram, a melt of An₆₀ starts crystallising at 1450 °C, forming crystals of An₈₅. At 1350 °C the liquid is An₃₅ and the crystals An₆₈. By the lever rule, the fraction of crystals = (60 − 35) ÷ (68 − 35) = 25 ÷ 33 ≈ 0.76, so about 76% solid at that temperature.

### Practical

**OCR PAG3**: model crystallisation with salol and thymol, or salol in solution, to show sequential crystallisation and eutectic behaviour; observe zoning in photomicrographs.

## How magma evolves (OCR 5.3.1(b); Eduqas G1.1a)

### What you need to know

- Assimilation, differentiation and fractionation change magma composition: magma mixing, fractional crystallisation, gravity settling, filter pressing.
- Eduqas: origin of the parent magma (mantle or crust); differentiation and fractionation (Bowen); gravity settling forming cumulates; contamination by xenoliths, magma mixing, enclaves; role of temperature, pressure and water in melting points.

### Explained

- **Fractional crystallisation**: early crystals (olivine, pyroxene, Ca-plagioclase) are separated from the liquid, removing Mg, Fe and Ca. The remaining liquid becomes richer in Si, Na, K and water. A basalt magma can evolve towards andesite and, in small amounts, rhyolite.
- **Gravity settling**: dense early crystals (olivine, pyroxene, chromite) sink to the floor of the chamber, forming **cumulate** layers.
- **Filter pressing**: compression squeezes residual liquid out of a crystal mush, separating it from the crystals.
- **Assimilation (contamination)**: hot magma melts and absorbs country rock (xenoliths may remain as partly digested blocks), changing composition (often adding silica, potassium).
- **Magma mixing**: two magmas combine; incomplete mixing leaves rounded enclaves of one magma in another.

**Melting and water**: adding water lowers the melting point of rocks greatly (wet granite melts at about 650 °C, dry granite above 950 °C); higher pressure raises the dry melting point but lowers the wet one. This matters for melting in subduction zones and thickened crust.

## Layered intrusions and ores (OCR 5.3.1(c))

### What you need to know

Layered intrusions and metal ores formed by magmatic differentiation: chalcophile cumulates and platinum group elements (PGE).

### Explained

Large mafic and ultramafic intrusions (for example the Bushveld Complex in South Africa, the Skaergaard intrusion in Greenland, and the Rum layered intrusion in Scotland) show repeated layers like sedimentary beds: dunite and chromitite at the base, then pyroxenite, then gabbro and anorthosite. They form by crystals settling (or growing on the floor) as the magma chamber cools and is periodically recharged with new magma.

**Ores**:

- Chromite (Cr) forms dense cumulate layers.
- If a magma becomes saturated in sulfur, droplets of immiscible sulfide liquid form. Chalcophile (Cu, Ni) and siderophile (Pt, Pd, the platinum group elements) metals partition strongly into these droplets, which are dense and sink, forming thin but rich layers (such as the Merensky Reef in the Bushveld, a major world source of platinum).

### Common mistakes

- Saying Bowen's series means all magmas end up as granite. Most basalt solidifies completely as basalt or gabbro; only small fractions evolve.
- Reading phase diagrams vertically instead of horizontally (crystal composition is read across to the solidus at the same temperature).

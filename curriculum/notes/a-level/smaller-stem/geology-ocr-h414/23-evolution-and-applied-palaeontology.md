# Evolution and applied palaeontology

The fossil record shows life becoming more diverse and complex over time, and shows how body plans were adapted to different ways of life. This file covers the main stages in the history of life and the adaptations of the key groups. Fossil identification basics are in [05](05-fossils-and-the-fossil-record.md); extinctions in [24](24-mass-extinctions.md); exceptional preservation in [25](25-lagerstatten.md).

## The history of life (Eduqas G3.1a, G3.1d)

### What you need to know

- Precambrian life (possibly from 3.8 billion years ago); the Ediacaran fauna (about 565 Ma), the oldest diverse multicellular soft-bodied organisms.
- The Cambrian explosion: mineralised skeletons and a wide variety of marine invertebrates by the early Cambrian; possible causes.
- Diversification in stages: Cambrian, Palaeozoic and modern faunas; Sepkoski's diversity curves.
- Migration onto land in the Palaeozoic: plants; vertebrates from fish to amphibians, amphibians to reptiles, reptiles to mammals and birds. Morphology of fossil vertebrates (including dinosaurs) to interpret mode of life.
- Gradualism versus punctuated equilibrium.

### Explained

- **Early life**: chemical and isotopic traces of life may date back about 3.8 billion years; stromatolites (layered mats built by cyanobacteria) are known from about 3.5 billion years. For most of Earth history, life was single-celled.
- **Ediacaran fauna** (late Neoproterozoic): soft-bodied organisms, some frond-like (for example *Charnia*, found in Charnwood Forest, England), some disc or quilted forms; their relationships to later animals are debated.
- **Cambrian explosion** (about 540 to 520 Ma): most major animal groups (phyla) appear rapidly in the fossil record, many with hard parts (shells, skeletons). Possible causes: rising oxygen allowing larger, more active animals and skeletons; predation driving the evolution of armour; evolution of genes controlling body plans; increased nutrients and calcium in seawater after the Snowball glaciations; the evolution of eyes. Hard parts also made fossilisation more likely, exaggerating the "explosion".
- **Three evolutionary faunas** (Sepkoski's analysis of marine families): the Cambrian fauna (trilobites, early brachiopods) dominated the Cambrian; the Palaeozoic fauna (articulate brachiopods, crinoids, rugose and tabulate corals, cephalopods) from the Ordovician to the Permian; the modern fauna (bivalves, gastropods, fish, echinoids) dominant since the Permian extinction. Diversity curves show a rise in the Ordovician, a long plateau through the Palaeozoic, then steady growth through the Mesozoic and Cenozoic, interrupted by mass extinctions.
- **Life on land**: plants colonised land in the Ordovician to Silurian; vascular plants spread in the Devonian; large forests and coal swamps in the Carboniferous. Arthropods followed plants. Vertebrates: fish → amphibians (Devonian) → reptiles (Carboniferous, with the amniotic egg) → mammals (Triassic) and birds (Jurassic, from theropod dinosaurs).

**Gradualism versus punctuated equilibrium**:

- Gradualism: species change slowly and steadily through time.
- Punctuated equilibrium: species remain largely unchanged (stasis) for long periods, then change rapidly during speciation, often in small isolated populations, so transitional forms are rarely preserved.
- The fossil record shows both patterns; gaps in the record make it hard to tell them apart.

## Marine invertebrates (OCR 7.1.2(a))

### What you need to know

- Trilobite adaptations to benthonic epifaunal, benthonic infaunal, nektonic and planktonic niches in the Palaeozoic.
- Ecology of modern reef-building (scleractinian) corals, including symbiotic algae, to interpret fossil tabulate and rugose corals as indicators of reefs.
- Brachiopod adaptations to high- and low-energy environments: cemented, free-lying, pedicle-attached and reef-building forms.
- Morphological similarities and differences between brachiopods and bivalves.

### Explained

**Trilobites**:

| Niche | Adaptations |
| --- | --- |
| Benthonic epifaunal (on the sea floor) | Flattened; broad genal spines spreading weight on soft mud; eyes on top of the head; moderate size |
| Benthonic infaunal (burrowing) | Smooth, streamlined, often reduced or no eyes; spade-like cephalon for ploughing |
| Nektonic (active swimmers) | Streamlined, large eyes giving wide field of view, light build |
| Planktonic (drifting) | Small, light, spiny for buoyancy, or with very large bulging eyes (all-round vision in the water column) |

Enrolment (rolling up like a woodlouse) for protection; trilobite eyes were made of calcite lenses.

**Corals**: modern scleractinian reef corals contain symbiotic algae (zooxanthellae) that photosynthesise and provide food; so reefs need light (shallow, clear water), warmth (about 20 to 29 °C), normal salinity and low sediment. By uniformitarianism, fossil reefs built by corals (tabulate and rugose corals in the Palaeozoic; they are not related closely to scleractinians and whether they had symbionts is uncertain) are taken as indicators of warm, shallow, clear tropical seas. Colonial corals in growth position with reef framework suggest reefs; solitary rugose corals can live in deeper or muddier water.

**Brachiopods**:

- High-energy (shallow, rough water): thick, ribbed shells; attached firmly by a strong pedicle to rock, or cemented by one valve; strophic (straight) hinges in some.
- Low-energy, soft muddy floors: thin shells, broad flattened shapes or spines that spread weight; free-lying (pedicle lost in adults), some with one convex valve resting in mud.
- Reef builders: some Permian forms (richthofenids) cemented together and resembled corals.

**Brachiopods versus bivalves**: both have two valves and filter-feed, an example of **convergent evolution** (similar form from different ancestors). Differences: symmetry (brachiopods: plane of symmetry through each valve; bivalves: between the valves), muscles, the brachiopod pedicle and lophophore (feeding organ supported by an internal brachidium) versus bivalve gills and siphons; brachiopods only marine. Brachiopods dominated Palaeozoic shelves; bivalves dominated after the Permian extinction.

## Vertebrates on land (OCR 7.1.2(b); Eduqas G3.1a)

### What you need to know

- How amphibians evolved from marine (lobe-finned) fish in the Devonian and adapted to land in the Carboniferous: skull morphology, fin bones, limb bones, teeth, body shape, tail fin, scales.
- The amniotic egg and its advantage for life on land.
- Dinosaur adaptations: saurischian (sauropod herbivores and theropod carnivores) and ornithischian dinosaurs.
- Birds evolved from theropods; function of feathers; convergent evolution between birds and pterosaurs.

### Explained

**Fish to amphibians**: lobe-finned fish had fleshy fins with an internal bone pattern (one upper bone, two lower bones) like the limbs of tetrapods; lungs; and labyrinthodont (folded-enamel) teeth like early amphibians. Transitional forms (for example *Tiktaalik*, with a flattened head, a neck and sturdy fins; and early tetrapods like *Acanthostega* with limbs bearing digits but still with gills and a tail fin) show stepwise change in the Devonian, perhaps in shallow, oxygen-poor streams and swamps. By the Carboniferous, amphibians had stronger limbs and girdles to support the body out of water, but still needed water to breed.

**Amniotic egg**: a shelled egg with membranes (amnion, chorion, allantois) and a yolk, enclosing the embryo in its own fluid, allowing gas exchange through the shell and storing waste. It frees reproduction from water, so reptiles (and later birds and mammals) could colonise dry land.

**Dinosaurs**: classified by hip structure.

- **Saurischians** ("lizard-hipped", pubis pointing forward):
  - Sauropods: huge quadrupedal herbivores with long necks (reaching high vegetation), small heads, peg-like teeth (stripping leaves; swallowed stones to grind food), pillar-like legs, air sacs in bones to reduce weight.
  - Theropods: bipedal carnivores with sharp serrated teeth, clawed hands, hollow bones, balancing tail; includes the ancestors of birds.
- **Ornithischians** ("bird-hipped", pubis pointing backward): all herbivores, with a beak at the front of the jaw and grinding teeth (ornithopods such as *Iguanodon*, armoured ankylosaurs, plated stegosaurs, horned ceratopsians).

Mode of life from morphology: teeth show diet; limb proportions and trackways show posture and speed; armour and horns show defence.

**Birds and pterosaurs**: birds evolved from small feathered theropods (shared features: hollow bones, wishbone, three main fingers, feathers). Feathers probably first evolved for insulation and display, later for flight. *Archaeopteryx* (Jurassic) has a mixture of bird and reptile features ([25](25-lagerstatten.md)). Pterosaurs were flying reptiles (not dinosaurs) with wings of skin stretched on a very long fourth finger. Birds' wings are feathers on the arm and fused hand. Both evolved flight independently: convergent evolution (lightweight hollow bones, keeled breastbone, large brain and eyes).

### Common mistakes

- Calling pterosaurs or marine reptiles dinosaurs.
- Saying birds evolved from ornithischian ("bird-hipped") dinosaurs. They evolved from saurischian theropods.
- Saying brachiopods are a type of bivalve.

# Uniformitarianism, facies and sedimentary environments

"The present is the key to the past": by studying how sediments form today, geologists interpret ancient sedimentary rocks. This file covers the rock cycle and uniformitarianism, facies, sedimentary structures, graphic logs and the main depositional environments. Rock types and grain-size analysis are in [03](03-sedimentary-rocks-weathering-and-diagenesis.md); turbidites, deltas and Walther's law in [13](13-turbidites-deltas-and-banded-iron-formations.md).

## The rock cycle and uniformitarianism (OCR 4.1.1(a), (b); Eduqas F3.1a, F3.1b, F3.2f)

### What you need to know

- Recognise the rock cycle in the field, photographs, diagrams and maps, including the origin of an angular unconformity.
- How uniformitarianism and the rock cycle model developed, including ideas of catastrophism, gradualism, mass extinctions and changing conditions and rates through time; the contributions of James Hutton and William Smith.

### Explained

The **rock cycle** links surface processes (weathering, erosion, deposition, lithification), driven by solar energy, with internal processes (burial, metamorphism, melting, uplift), driven by internal heat. An **angular unconformity** shows a whole cycle: rocks were deposited, buried, deformed and uplifted, eroded, then new sediment was deposited on top. Hutton's famous unconformity at Siccar Point (Scotland), with gently dipping Devonian sandstones on near-vertical Silurian greywackes, convinced him that the Earth had gone through repeated cycles over immense time.

**James Hutton** (late 18th century): argued that present-day processes, acting slowly over vast time, explain rocks (later called uniformitarianism); recognised that granite was once molten and intruded other rocks; saw "no vestige of a beginning".

**William Smith** (around 1800): showed that each rock layer contains a characteristic set of fossils in a definite order (faunal succession), so strata could be traced across country; produced the first geological map of England and Wales (1815).

**Catastrophism** (earlier view): Earth's features formed by sudden, violent events (often linked to Biblical floods). **Gradualism**: change by slow, steady processes. Modern view: uniformity of process (the same physical laws) but not always of rate: long periods of slow change are interrupted by rare catastrophic events (large eruptions, impacts, mass extinctions). Conditions have also changed through time (for example no free oxygen early on, so banded iron formations formed then but not now).

## Facies (OCR 4.1.1(c); Eduqas F3.1c)

### What you need to know

- A facies is a body of rock with distinctive characteristics (lithology, sedimentary structures, fossils) that reflect its environment of deposition. Lithofacies (rock characteristics) and biofacies (fossil content).
- Facies associations; facies as the basic unit of sedimentary geology; uniformitarianism applied by comparison with modern sequences.

### Explained

A facies is defined by what you can see: grain size, composition, colour, structures, fossils. Each records particular conditions (energy, water depth, salinity, oxygen). Environments produce a group of related facies (a **facies association**): a river channel and its floodplain; a beach, shoreface and offshore muds. Interpreting an ancient environment means matching the facies association to one forming today.

## Sediment transport and sedimentary structures (OCR 4.1.2(a); Eduqas F2.1d, F2.1e)

### What you need to know

- Characteristics of sediments transported by wind, glaciers, rivers and in shallow seas.
- Structures: bedding, laminations, cross-bedding, ripple marks (symmetrical and asymmetrical), graded bedding, desiccation cracks, salt pseudomorphs, imbricate structure, sole structures (load and flame structures, flute casts).
- Use structures to interpret way-up and environment in the field and on photographs; sketch them.

### Explained

| Agent | Sediment characteristics |
| --- | --- |
| Wind | Very well sorted fine to medium sand; well rounded, frosted grains; no clay (carried away); large-scale cross-bedding |
| Glacier | Unsorted mixture of all sizes (till, diamictite); angular, striated clasts; no bedding |
| River | Moderately sorted; channel gravels and sands; floodplain silts and muds |
| Shallow sea | Well sorted sands near shore; muds offshore; marine fossils |

| Structure | Formed by | Shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bedding / lamination | Changes in sediment supply | Layers under 1 cm are laminations; quiet water if fine |
| Cross-bedding | Migration of ripples or dunes; sand avalanches down the lee face | Current direction (dip of foresets); way-up (foresets curve into base, truncated at top) |
| Asymmetrical ripples | One-way current; gentle stoss side, steep lee side | Current direction; rivers, tidal currents |
| Symmetrical ripples | Waves moving back and forth | Shallow water with waves; way-up (sharp crests point up) |
| Graded bedding | Fall-out from a waning current (turbidity current) | Coarse at base, fine at top: way-up |
| Desiccation (mud) cracks | Mud drying out and shrinking | Exposure to air; V-shaped cracks narrow downwards: way-up |
| Salt pseudomorphs | Cubic halite crystals dissolved and replaced by sediment | Evaporation, hot arid climate |
| Imbrication | Flat pebbles stacked overlapping, dipping upstream | Current direction in rivers |
| Load and flame structures | Dense sand sinking into soft mud; mud squeezed up between | Rapid deposition; way-up |
| Flute casts | Scoured hollows on a mud surface filled by sand (seen on the base of sandstone) | Current direction (steep end upstream); way-up; turbidites |

## Graphic logs (OCR 4.1.2(b); Eduqas SP6)

### What you need to know

Construct and interpret graphic logs of modern sediments and ancient rocks, including fossil assemblages, structures and directional data (palaeocurrents).

### Explained

A graphic log is a vertical column drawn to scale showing each bed: the horizontal width shows grain size (wider = coarser), symbols show lithology, structures, fossils, and side columns show palaeocurrent arrows and notes. Start at the base. Look for trends: fining-upward sequences (river channels: coarse base becoming fine) and coarsening-upward sequences (deltas and prograding shorelines: offshore muds overlain by shoreface sands).

## Depositional environments (OCR 4.1.2(c) to (g); Eduqas F2.1e)

### Explained

**Fluvial (rivers)**:

- Meandering rivers: single sinuous channel with a floodplain. The channel migrates sideways by eroding the outer bank and depositing a point bar on the inner bank. This produces fining-upward sequences: erosive base with pebble lag, cross-bedded channel sandstone, rippled finer sand, then floodplain silts and clays with roots and desiccation cracks. Channels form lens-shaped sandstone bodies within muds.
- Braided rivers: many shallow, shifting channels separated by bars, with abundant coarse sediment and variable discharge (glacial outwash, deserts). Mostly sand and gravel, little mud, sheet-like bodies.
- Alluvial fans: cone-shaped deposits where a steep mountain stream reaches a plain and drops sediment; poorly sorted angular breccias and conglomerates, debris flows.

**Hot desert**:

- Wadis: dry valleys with flash floods carrying poorly sorted gravels and breccias.
- Dunes (aeolian): very well sorted, rounded, frosted, red (haematite-coated) sands with large-scale cross-bedding (metres high, steep foresets).
- Playa lakes: temporary lakes in basins that evaporate, leaving muds with desiccation cracks and evaporites (halite, gypsum).
- Controlled by gradual wind action plus episodic high-energy flash floods.

**Shallow siliciclastic seas**: from the beach out to deeper water, energy decreases. Beach: well sorted, rounded sands, low-angle lamination, shell fragments. Shoreface and shelf above wave base: sand sheets reworked by currents and storms, cross-bedding and symmetrical ripples. Below wave base (where waves no longer stir the bed): muds deposited from suspension, with burrows and complete fossils.

**Shallow carbonate seas** (warm, clear, tropical, little sediment from land):

- Reef limestone: corals and other organisms in growth position, forming a rigid framework with no bedding.
- In front of the reef (fore-reef): broken reef debris (bioclastic limestone) on slopes.
- Behind the reef (back-reef lagoon): quiet, muddy carbonate (micrite) with whole shells.
- Oolitic limestone: ooids formed in warm, shallow, agitated water supersaturated with CaCO₃ (shoals).

**Deep-water carbonate seas** (above the carbonate compensation depth, CCD): the CCD is the depth (about 4 km) below which calcium carbonate dissolves as fast as it arrives, because cold, high-pressure water holds more CO₂. Above it, calcareous plankton accumulate:

- Chalk: very pure, fine limestone of coccoliths, deposited in quiet, clear seas with little land-derived sediment (the Cretaceous chalk of southern England).
- Micritic limestones: fine lime mud.
- Chert and flint: silica from siliceous organisms (sponges, radiolaria, diatoms) dissolves and reprecipitates during diagenesis as nodules or layers; flint in chalk. Forms where little siliciclastic sediment arrives (sediment-starved seas) and on continental slopes.

Below the CCD, only siliceous oozes and red clays accumulate.

### Practical

**OCR PAG4 and PAG6 / Eduqas SP5, SP6**: describe sedimentary rocks fully in the field and in hand specimen; construct graphic logs for unfamiliar sequences; measure palaeocurrents from cross-bedding and plot them on a rose diagram (circular data); interpret environments.

### Worked example

A log from base to top: erosive surface with pebbles; 3 m cross-bedded sandstone (foresets dipping east); 1 m rippled siltstone; 2 m red mudstone with root traces and desiccation cracks; then another erosive surface. Interpretation: meandering river. Fining-upward cycles record channel migration; current flowed east; floodplain was periodically exposed.

### Common mistakes

- Saying uniformitarianism means rates were always the same.
- Reading cross-bedding current direction from the bed boundary instead of the foreset dip.
- Calling any limestone a reef limestone.

2. Catastrophism: features formed by sudden violent events (1); uniformitarianism: slow present-day processes over long time (1); modern: same processes and laws (1) but rates vary, with occasional catastrophes such as impacts / mass extinctions (1).

# Geological time and dating

Geologists place events in order (relative dating) and give some of them ages in years (absolute or numerical dating). Combined, these build the geological timescale. Radioactive decay physics is also in [A-level Physics 18](../../physics/18-radioactivity-decay-and-nuclear-radius.md).

## Relative dating principles (OCR 4.2.1(a), (c); Eduqas F3.2a, F2.3f)

### What you need to know

Superposition, original horizontality, way-up criteria, cross-cutting relationships, included fragments, unconformities, and evolutionary change in fossils. Apply them to outcrops, photographs, maps and cross-sections to work out geological histories. Know their limitations.

### Explained

| Principle | Statement |
| --- | --- |
| Superposition | In an undisturbed sequence, younger beds lie on top of older beds |
| Original horizontality | Sediments are deposited in near-horizontal layers; tilted or folded beds were deformed later |
| Way-up criteria | Structures that show which way was up when deposited: graded bedding (coarse at base), cross-bedding (truncated tops, curved bases), ripple marks, desiccation cracks (V-shapes point down), load and flame structures, geopetals, pillow lavas, burrows. Needed when beds may be overturned |
| Cross-cutting relationships | A feature (fault, dyke, intrusion, unconformity) is younger than the rocks it cuts |
| Included fragments | Fragments (xenoliths, pebbles) are older than the rock that contains them |
| Unconformities | A surface of erosion or non-deposition representing a time gap; rocks below are older and have been uplifted and eroded |
| Faunal succession | Fossil species appear and disappear in a definite order, so the same fossils indicate the same age |

**Baked margins and chilled margins**: a sill is intruded if the beds above and below are both baked; a buried lava flow bakes only the bed below it, and the bed above may contain eroded fragments of the lava. This tells you the order of events.

**Unconformities**:

- Angular unconformity: older tilted or folded beds are overlain by younger beds at a different angle. History: deposition, deformation and uplift, erosion, subsidence, renewed deposition.
- Disconformity: parallel beds above and below, but an erosion surface between.
- Nonconformity: sediments on eroded igneous or metamorphic rock.

**Limitations of relative dating**: gives order not ages; unconformities hide unknown amounts of time; overturned or faulted sequences are hard to read; correlation between separated areas is uncertain.

### Worked example

A cross-section shows folded sandstones and shales cut by a granite, both truncated by an erosion surface, overlain by horizontal limestones, all cut by a fault that displaces the limestones and is cut by a dyke. Order: (1) sandstones and shales deposited; (2) folding; (3) granite intrusion (cuts folds); (4) uplift and erosion (unconformity); (5) limestone deposition; (6) faulting; (7) dyke intrusion.

## Correlation (OCR 4.2.1(b), (d); Eduqas F3.2c, F3.2d)

### What you need to know

- Lithostratigraphic correlation: matching rock units by sequence of beds, thickness and composition; lateral variation; diachronous beds.
- Biostratigraphic correlation: first appearance, stratigraphic range, extinction and fossil assemblages; zone fossils.
- Features of good zone fossils: wide geographic distribution, abundant, easily preserved, rapid evolution (short range), independent of facies, easily identified.
- Eduqas: graptolites (number and position of stipes, theca shape, early Palaeozoic) and cephalopod sutures (goniatite, ceratite, ammonite: late Palaeozoic and Mesozoic) in relative dating; assessing them as zone fossils.

### Explained

**Lithostratigraphic correlation** matches beds between boreholes or outcrops by rock type and sequence, using distinctive marker beds (a volcanic ash, a coal seam). Problem: a rock unit may be **diachronous**, meaning it is a different age in different places. For example, a beach sand deposited as the sea advances (transgression) is older at the start and younger further inland. So matching rock type does not always match time.

**Biostratigraphy**: a zone is a body of rock defined by its fossil content, usually named after a zone fossil. Range zones use the first and last appearance of a species; assemblage zones use a group of fossils occurring together. The overlap of ranges of two species gives a narrow time band.

**Zone fossils**:

| Requirement | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Rapid evolution, short time range | Gives fine time resolution |
| Wide geographic distribution | Correlation over long distances |
| Abundant and readily preserved | Likely to be found |
| Facies independent (found in many rock types) | Correlates different environments |
| Easily identified | Reliable use |

**Graptolites** (Ordovician and Silurian): planktonic so widespread and facies-independent (but best preserved in black shales); evolved quickly. Trends: early forms had many stipes, hanging down (pendent); later forms had fewer stipes, becoming two-stiped and then single-stiped (monograptids, Silurian), and stipes changed from hanging down to growing upward (scandent). Thecal shapes became more complex (hooked, isolated). Weakness: fragile, mainly preserved in quiet anoxic muds.

**Cephalopod sutures**: sutures became more complex through time:

- Goniatite (Devonian to Permian): simple zigzag, with smooth saddles (pointing to the aperture) and lobes (pointing back).
- Ceratite (Triassic): saddles smooth, lobes frilled.
- Ammonite (Jurassic and Cretaceous): both saddles and lobes highly frilled.

Ammonites are excellent zone fossils for the Jurassic: free-swimming, widespread, rapidly evolving, many zones of under a million years each. Weakness: some lived in particular water depths, and shells can drift after death.

## Radiometric dating (OCR 2.2.2(a); Eduqas F3.2b, G1.1a)

### What you need to know

- Radioactive decay of parent isotopes to daughter isotopes at a constant rate; half-life.
- Use decay to give the numerical age of minerals and rocks; plot and interpret half-life curves.
- Eduqas: potassium-argon (⁴⁰K → ⁴⁰Ar) and samarium-neodymium (¹⁴⁷Sm → ¹⁴³Nd); calculate ages; assumptions, accuracy and limitations. A level: N = N₀e^(−λt), logarithms, percentage error.
- OCR: qualitative history of other ways to estimate the Earth's age; suitable minerals; dating sedimentary and metamorphic rocks.

### Explained

Radioactive (parent) isotopes decay to stable daughter isotopes at a rate unaffected by temperature, pressure or chemistry. **Half-life** (t½) is the time for half the parent atoms to decay.

| Half-lives passed | Parent left | Daughter : parent ratio |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1/2 | 1 : 1 |
| 2 | 1/4 | 3 : 1 |
| 3 | 1/8 | 7 : 1 |
| 4 | 1/16 | 15 : 1 |

Equation: N = N₀e^(−λt), where λ = ln 2 ÷ t½. Rearranged: t = (1/λ) ln(N₀/N). If D is daughter produced: N₀ = N + D, so t = (1/λ) ln(1 + D/N).

**Methods**:

- K-Ar: half-life about 1.25 billion years. Used on K-bearing minerals (micas, feldspars) in igneous rocks, especially volcanic rocks. Argon is a gas, so the clock starts when the rock cools and traps argon. Weakness: argon can leak out if the rock is reheated (age too young) or excess argon can be trapped (too old).
- Sm-Nd: half-life about 106 billion years, so used for very old rocks (Precambrian), meteorites and mafic rocks. Both elements are rare-earth elements that behave similarly, so the system is hard to disturb.

**Which rocks can be dated**: the clock records when a mineral crystallised or last cooled below its "closure temperature". Igneous rocks give crystallisation ages. Metamorphic rocks give the age of metamorphism (minerals reset by heating). Sedimentary rocks usually cannot be dated directly, because their grains are older than the deposit; they are bracketed by dating igneous rocks above and below (lava flows, ash beds, cross-cutting dykes), or by dating minerals formed at deposition (glauconite).

**Assumptions**: closed system (no parent or daughter gained or lost); no daughter present at the start (or it can be corrected for); decay constant known accurately; the mineral formed at the event being dated.

**Earlier estimates of the Earth's age** (OCR, qualitative): counting generations in written records (a few thousand years); rates of sediment accumulation; salt accumulation in the oceans; Kelvin's cooling of the Earth (tens of millions of years, too short because he did not know about radioactive heating). Radiometric dating of meteorites gives about 4.6 billion years.

### Worked example

A mica has ⁴⁰Ar : ⁴⁰K = 3 : 1 (ignoring the branch of K decay to Ca for simplicity). That is two half-lives: 2 × 1.25 = 2.5 billion years.

With the equation: a mineral has 60% of its original parent left; t½ = 700 million years. λ = 0.693 ÷ 700 = 9.9 × 10⁻⁴ per Ma. t = ln(1 ÷ 0.60) ÷ λ = 0.511 ÷ 9.9 × 10⁻⁴ ≈ 516 Ma.

Percentage error: an age of 516 ± 8 Ma has a percentage error of 8 ÷ 516 × 100 = 1.6%.

## The geological column (OCR 2.2.2(b); Eduqas F3.2e)

### What you need to know

- The Phanerozoic is divided into eras and periods using a biostratigraphic relative sequence; numerical ages come from radiometric dating. Dates need not be memorised.
- Outline of Palaeozoic, Mesozoic and Cenozoic faunas; basic identification of trilobites, corals, brachiopods, bivalves and cephalopods (see [05](05-fossils-and-the-fossil-record.md)).
- Eduqas: use the column to place events in order and define the absolute age of some events.

### Explained

| Era | Periods (oldest at bottom) | Typical fauna |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cenozoic | Quaternary, Neogene, Palaeogene | Mammals, birds, bivalves, gastropods, modern corals |
| Mesozoic | Cretaceous, Jurassic, Triassic | Ammonites, belemnites, dinosaurs, marine reptiles, scleractinian corals, bivalves |
| Palaeozoic | Permian, Carboniferous, Devonian, Silurian, Ordovician, Cambrian | Trilobites, graptolites, brachiopods, rugose and tabulate corals, goniatites; fish then amphibians and reptiles |

Boundaries between eras are mass extinctions (end-Permian, end-Cretaceous), so the faunas change sharply ([24](24-mass-extinctions.md)). The column was built from relative order using fossils (William Smith's faunal succession) and later calibrated with radiometric dates from interbedded volcanic rocks.

### Practical

**OCR PAG5 and PAG6**: identify fossil groups and use them to assign relative ages; apply relative dating principles to field exposures, photographs and maps to construct a geological history.

### Common mistakes

- Saying radiometric dating gives the age of a sandstone. It dates the original source minerals unless minerals grew at deposition.
- Saying a fault is older than the rocks it cuts.
- Saying "half-life is half the time the element lasts".

## Mark scheme

1. A feature that cuts across rocks is younger than them (1); example: dyke / fault / unconformity cutting beds (1).
2. Sill bakes rocks above and below (1); lava only bakes the rock below (1); lava may have vesicular / weathered top and fragments in the bed above; sill may be transgressive (1).
3. Deposition of horizontal beds (1); folding / tilting (by compression) and uplift (1); erosion forming an irregular surface (1); subsidence and deposition of new horizontal beds on top (1).
4. Sea advances over land gradually (1); beach deposited at different times at different places, so one rock unit has different ages (1).
5. Up to 4: rapid evolution, short ranges (1); planktonic so widespread / facies independent (1); abundant and recognisable (1); fragile, mainly preserved in black shales, limited to Ordovician to early Devonian (1).
6. 7 : 1 means 3 half-lives (1); 3 × 1.25 = 3.75 billion years (1).
7. λ = 0.693 ÷ 1.25 × 10⁹ = 5.54 × 10⁻¹⁰ y⁻¹ (1); t = ln(1/0.85) ÷ λ (1); = 0.1625 ÷ 5.54 × 10⁻¹⁰ = 2.9 × 10⁸ years (1).
8. Grains formed earlier in source rocks, so give source age not deposition age (1); date interbedded lavas / ashes or cross-cutting intrusions (1); bracket the age between them / date minerals formed at deposition (1).
9. Argon is a gas (1); escapes when heated so less daughter measured, giving a younger age (1).

# Igneous rocks: classification and textures

Igneous rocks form when magma or lava cools and crystallises. Composition tells you about the source of the magma; crystal size, shape and texture tell you how and where it cooled. Magma generation and igneous bodies are in [10](10-magma-intrusion-and-volcanism.md); magma evolution is in [15](15-igneous-petrology.md).

## Classification by composition and crystal size (OCR 2.1.2(a); Eduqas F2.2b)

### What you need to know

- Composition groups: silicic (felsic, acid), intermediate, mafic (basic), ultramafic.
- Crystal size boundaries. OCR: coarse > 5 mm, medium 1 to 5 mm, fine < 1 mm. Eduqas: coarse > 3 mm, medium 1 to 3 mm, fine < 1 mm. Use the boundaries for your board.
- OCR rocks: granite, microgranite, rhyolite; diorite, microdiorite, andesite; gabbro, dolerite, basalt; peridotite. Eduqas rocks: granite (silicic); gabbro, dolerite, basalt (mafic); peridotite (ultramafic).
- Identify igneous rocks in hand specimen, photographs and thin-section diagrams from their diagnostic properties.

### Explained

| | Coarse (plutonic) | Medium (hypabyssal) | Fine (volcanic) | Main minerals | Colour |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Silicic (> 66% SiO₂) | Granite | Microgranite | Rhyolite | Quartz, orthoclase, Na-plagioclase, muscovite, biotite | Pale (pink, white, grey) |
| Intermediate (52 to 66%) | Diorite | Microdiorite | Andesite | Plagioclase, hornblende, some biotite, little quartz | Medium grey, speckled |
| Mafic (45 to 52%) | Gabbro | Dolerite | Basalt | Ca-plagioclase, pyroxene (augite), olivine | Dark grey to black |
| Ultramafic (< 45%) | Peridotite | | | Olivine, pyroxene | Dark green |

Colour index: the percentage of dark (ferromagnesian) minerals. Silicic rocks are pale (low colour index); mafic rocks are dark. Ultramafic rocks have the highest density, silicic the lowest.

Name an igneous rock by first judging crystal size (with a hand lens and scale), then estimating composition from colour and minerals.

## Textures, crystal shape and cooling history (OCR 2.1.2(b); Eduqas F2.2b, SP8, SP9)

### What you need to know

- Texture, crystal shape and crystal size are evidence of the depth of formation and the rate of cooling.
- Textures: equicrystalline (equigranular), glassy, vesicular, amygdaloidal, flow banding, porphyritic; Eduqas also fragmental (tuff).
- Crystal shape: euhedral (perfect faces), subhedral (some faces), anhedral (no faces).
- Structures (Eduqas): pillow structure, aa and pahoehoe surfaces, columnar joints.
- Draw and annotate textures; measure temperature (OCR, linked to cooling experiments).

### Explained

**Cooling rate and crystal size**: crystals grow as atoms move through the melt and attach to growing crystals. Slow cooling (deep plutons insulated by surrounding rock) gives time for few, large crystals. Fast cooling (lava at the surface) produces many nuclei and little time for growth, so small crystals. Instant quenching (lava into water, or very viscous silicic lava) gives glass with no crystals (obsidian).

| Texture | Description | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equicrystalline | Crystals all about the same size | Cooled at a steady rate |
| Porphyritic | Large crystals (phenocrysts) in a finer groundmass | Two-stage cooling: slow at depth, then faster after rising |
| Glassy | No crystals, conchoidal fracture | Very rapid cooling (obsidian) |
| Vesicular | Gas holes (vesicles) | Gas came out of solution as pressure fell near the surface; lava |
| Amygdaloidal | Vesicles filled later with minerals (calcite, quartz, zeolites) | Lava altered by later fluids |
| Flow banding | Bands of different colour or crystal content | Viscous lava flowing (rhyolite) |
| Fragmental (tuff) | Broken crystals, rock and glass fragments | Pyroclastic: explosive eruption deposit |

**Crystal shape**: the first minerals to crystallise grow freely in the melt, so they are euhedral. Later minerals fill the spaces between, so they are anhedral. In granite, biotite and feldspar may be euhedral to subhedral while quartz, crystallising last, is anhedral. Shape therefore shows the order of crystallisation.

**Structures**:

- Pillow lava: rounded, bulbous masses formed when lava erupts under water and the surface chills immediately; shows submarine eruption, and the shape of the pillows can show way-up.
- Pahoehoe: smooth, ropy surface of fluid basalt lava; aa: rough, clinkery, spiny surface of cooler, more viscous basalt.
- Columnar joints: polygonal (often hexagonal) columns formed by contraction as a lava flow or sill cools evenly; columns are perpendicular to the cooling surfaces.

### Worked example

A thin section shows plagioclase phenocrysts up to 4 mm in a groundmass of crystals less than 0.1 mm, with vesicles. Interpretation: porphyritic and vesicular, so the magma partly crystallised slowly at depth (phenocrysts), then erupted as lava, cooled quickly (fine groundmass) and degassed near the surface (vesicles). If the groundmass is dark with plagioclase, augite and olivine, the rock is a porphyritic basalt.

### Practical

**OCR PAG3 (crystalline processes)**: investigate crystal size against cooling rate using salol (phenyl salicylate). Melt salol and place drops on microscope slides at different temperatures (warm, room temperature, chilled on ice). Measure slide temperature with a thermometer or probe; observe crystal size with a microscope or hand lens with a scale. Colder slides give smaller crystals. Safety: salol irritates eyes; avoid hot surfaces.

**Eduqas SP8 and SP9**: full rock descriptions of igneous hand specimens and field exposures (colour, crystal size, shape, minerals, texture, structure) and identification of minerals and textures from photomicrographs to deduce cooling history.

### Common mistakes

- Saying porphyritic rocks cooled slowly. They had two cooling rates.
- Calling dolerite "medium-grained basalt" without naming it.
- Saying vesicles mean the rock is a lava. Shallow intrusions can be vesicular too, though it is uncommon; vesicles usually indicate lava.

# Whole basin facies analysis

Basin analysis combines sedimentology, palaeontology, structure and dating to reconstruct the history of a sedimentary basin. The graphic log is the main tool. OCR uses two British basins: the Lower Palaeozoic Welsh Basin and the Jurassic rocks of the UK. Facies and environments are in [12](12-uniformitarianism-facies-and-sedimentary-environments.md); turbidites in [13](13-turbidites-deltas-and-banded-iron-formations.md); zone fossils in [06](06-geological-time-and-dating.md).

## The Welsh Basin (OCR 7.2.3(a))

### What you need to know

- Geological setting and sedimentary conditions in the Welsh Basin through the Cambrian, Ordovician and Silurian.
- Palaeoenvironments from facies analysis (sediments and fossils): deep-sea turbidites and shelf deposits; reef limestones.
- Zonation using trilobites, corals and graptolites as zone fossils.

### Explained

**Setting**: in the early Palaeozoic, Wales lay on the micro-continent Avalonia, on the southern side of the Iapetus Ocean, in southern latitudes moving north. The Welsh Basin was a subsiding marine basin on the edge of Avalonia, bordered to the south-east by a stable shelf (the Midland Platform, in the Welsh Borders and English Midlands). Subduction of Iapetus produced volcanic arcs, so the Ordovician includes thick volcanic rocks (Snowdonia). The basin closed during the Caledonian orogeny at the end of the Silurian and into the Devonian, folding and cleaving the rocks (slates of North Wales).

**Facies**:

| Facies | Rocks and fossils | Environment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basin turbidites | Thick sequences of graded greywacke sandstones with sole marks, alternating with mudstones (Bouma sequences) | Deep basin floor, sediment from turbidity currents off the shelf margin |
| Black graptolitic shales | Dark mudstones with pyrite and abundant graptolites, laminated, no burrows | Deep, quiet, anoxic basin; planktonic graptolites sank into it |
| Shelf sandstones and mudstones | Cross-bedded sandstones, siltstones, brachiopods, trilobites, burrows | Shallow, oxygenated shelf (Midland Platform) |
| Reef limestones | Limestones with tabulate and rugose corals, stromatoporoids, crinoids, brachiopods (Silurian Wenlock Limestone of Wenlock Edge and Dudley) | Warm, clear, shallow tropical shelf sea |
| Volcanic rocks | Lavas, tuffs, ash-flow deposits (Ordovician) | Volcanic arc and back-arc activity |

Moving from the Midland Platform north-westwards into the basin, facies change from shelf limestones and sandstones to deep-water turbidites and graptolitic shales (a facies change across the basin margin). Through time, Silurian facies show Wales moving into tropical latitudes (reefs) and the basin filling as Iapetus closed.

**Zonation**:

- Graptolites are the main zone fossils for the Ordovician and Silurian in the deep-water facies (rapid evolution, planktonic and widespread; see the trends in [06](06-geological-time-and-dating.md)).
- Trilobites zone the Cambrian (for example *Paradoxides* in the Middle Cambrian) and shelf facies of the Ordovician.
- Corals and brachiopods are less useful as zone fossils (facies-dependent, slower evolution) but indicate shelf environments.
- Using different groups in different facies, and places where they occur together, allows correlation from basin to shelf.

## The UK Jurassic (OCR 7.2.3(b))

### What you need to know

- Geological setting and cyclical sedimentation in shallow seas.
- Facies analysis of the sediments: structures, sediment types (shales, limestones, siltstones, sandstones, ironstones) and fossils; burrows and other trace fossils for palaeoenvironments.
- Key macrofossils (ammonites, belemnites, bivalves); marine reptiles (ichthyosaurs, plesiosaurs) and flying reptiles (pterosaurs).
- Zonation and correlation using ammonites and belemnites; their use for palaeoenvironments.

### Explained

**Setting**: in the Jurassic, Britain lay at about 30 to 40° N as Pangaea broke apart. Warm, shallow seas covered much of England, with islands of older rocks (Wales, the London-Brabant Massif, Scotland) supplying sediment. Rifting in the North Sea created deeper basins. Sea level rose and fell repeatedly.

**Cyclical sedimentation**: the Jurassic of England (for example the Dorset coast, the Cotswolds and the Yorkshire coast) shows repeated cycles: deeper-water mudstones and shales, passing upwards into siltstones, sandstones and shallow-water limestones (often oolitic) or ironstones, then back to mudstones. These reflect shallowing-upward cycles from sea-level changes and sediment supply (Walther's law).

| Facies | Environment |
| --- | --- |
| Dark shales and mudstones with ammonites, belemnites, bivalves, sometimes complete marine reptiles; fine laminations (for example the Lias of Lyme Regis and Whitby) | Offshore, quiet, sometimes poorly oxygenated sea floor below wave base |
| Siltstones and sandstones with burrows and cross-bedding | Shallower shelf, above storm wave base |
| Oolitic limestones (Bath stone) with cross-bedding | Very shallow, warm, agitated carbonate shoals |
| Shelly and coral limestones (for example the Corallian) | Shallow, clear, warm water; patch reefs |
| Ironstones (ooidal iron-rich rocks; Cleveland, Northamptonshire) | Shallow sea with slow deposition and iron supply from weathered land |
| Deltaic sandstones and coals (Middle Jurassic of Yorkshire) | Delta top and channels |

**Trace fossils**: vertical burrows show shallow, high-energy sands; horizontal feeding traces show quieter muds; absence of burrows and laminated dark shales show anoxic bottoms.

**Fossils as environment indicators**: ammonites and belemnites were free-swimming in open marine water; bivalves show substrate and energy (burrowers in sand, cemented oysters on hard grounds); marine reptiles (ichthyosaurs, fish-shaped hunters; plesiosaurs, long-necked paddle swimmers) show open sea; pterosaurs flew from nearby land.

**Zonation**: ammonites evolved very rapidly, swam freely and were widespread, so the Jurassic is divided into many ammonite zones (each lasting well under a million years), allowing very fine correlation across Britain and Europe. Belemnites are useful where ammonites are scarce (in the later Jurassic and Cretaceous of northern Europe). Limitation: ammonite distributions can be facies-related and shells could drift after death.

## Basin-wide investigation (OCR 7.2.3(c))

### What you need to know

A practical investigation combining fieldwork and secondary data (maps, seismic data, well logs, fossils) to understand palaeoenvironments and geological history across a basin.

### Explained

Steps in a basin study:

1. Log sections in the field (graphic logs), recording lithology, structures, fossils and palaeocurrents.
2. Collect secondary data: geological maps, borehole logs, seismic sections.
3. Interpret facies and environments for each section.
4. Correlate sections using zone fossils and marker beds; build a fence diagram or cross-section.
5. Map facies across the basin at each time slice (palaeogeographic maps).
6. Relate changes to tectonics (subsidence, faulting) and sea-level changes; identify unconformities.

### Practical

**OCR PAG4 and PAG12**: construct and correlate graphic logs from Jurassic or Lower Palaeozoic sections; integrate fieldwork with published maps and well data.

### Common mistakes

- Using corals as fine-scale zone fossils in the Welsh Basin.
- Ignoring facies when correlating: the same rock type is not always the same age.

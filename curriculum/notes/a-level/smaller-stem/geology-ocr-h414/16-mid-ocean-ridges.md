# Mid-ocean ridges

The mid-ocean ridge system is the largest volcanic system on Earth, about 65 000 km long. Here new oceanic lithosphere is made, and hot water circulating through the young crust forms metal ores. Option T5 (Eduqas) builds on this in [Eduqas 05](../geology-eduqas-a480qs/05-geology-of-the-lithosphere.md).

## Spreading rate and ridge shape (OCR 5.3.2(a); Eduqas T5.2d)

### What you need to know

- Relationship between spreading rate and sea-bed morphology: water depth, fast and slow ridges.
- Calculate spreading rates from different data (magnetic anomalies, radiometric ages, drilling, hotspot tracks).
- Calculate numerical ages from decay rates (recall of N = N₀e^(−λt) not required for OCR).

### Explained

| | Slow-spreading (Mid-Atlantic Ridge, about 2 to 5 cm yr⁻¹ total) | Fast-spreading (East Pacific Rise, about 10 to 15 cm yr⁻¹) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Profile | Rugged, steep, broad | Smooth, gentle, low broad rise |
| Axis | Deep axial rift valley (1 to 3 km deep) bounded by normal faults | Axial high; no deep rift |
| Magma supply | Intermittent, small chambers | Continuous, steady magma lens |
| Faulting | Much normal faulting; core complexes | Less faulting |

**Depth and age**: as lithosphere moves away from the ridge it cools, contracts and becomes denser, so it subsides. Depth increases roughly with the square root of age: ridges stand about 2.5 km deep, old ocean floor about 5 to 6 km. Fast ridges have gentler flanks because the lithosphere has travelled further before cooling.

**Spreading rate** = distance ÷ age. Using magnetic anomalies: find the distance from the ridge axis to a magnetic stripe boundary of known age. Rates may be half-rates (one side) or full (both sides).

### Worked example

A reversal boundary dated 4.2 Ma lies 105 km west of the ridge axis and 90 km east. Half-rates: 105 ÷ 4.2 = 25 km Ma⁻¹ = 2.5 cm yr⁻¹ west; 90 ÷ 4.2 = 21.4 km Ma⁻¹ = 2.1 cm yr⁻¹ east. Full spreading rate = 4.6 cm yr⁻¹ (asymmetric spreading).

Radiometric: a basalt has 98.5% of its ⁴⁰K left; t½ = 1.25 × 10⁹ years. t = (t½ ÷ ln 2) × ln(1 ÷ 0.985) = 1.803 × 10⁹ × 0.0151 = 2.7 × 10⁷ years (27 Ma). If it is 540 km from the ridge, half-rate = 540 ÷ 27 = 20 km Ma⁻¹ = 2.0 cm yr⁻¹.

## Evidence for ridge structure (OCR 5.3.2(b); Eduqas T5.2a, T5.2b)

### What you need to know

- Ophiolite complexes and geophysical surveys: gravity (free-air and Bouguer anomalies), reflection seismic and geoelectrical (EM).
- Eduqas: oceanic crust has seismic layers 1, 2 and 3 (sediments; pillow lavas and sheeted dykes; gabbro), with evidence from ophiolites and ocean drilling.

### Explained

**Ophiolite sequence** (top to bottom), seen in Troodos (Cyprus), Oman and the Lizard (Cornwall):

| Unit | Seismic layer | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deep-sea sediments (chert, mudstone) | Layer 1 | Low velocity |
| Pillow lavas | Layer 2 | Basalt erupted under water |
| Sheeted dyke complex | Layer 2 | Hundreds of vertical dykes, each intruded into the last; records repeated spreading |
| Gabbro (isotropic, then layered cumulates) | Layer 3 | The frozen magma chamber |
| Peridotite (cumulates over depleted mantle harzburgite) | Mantle | The petrological Moho lies at the base of the cumulates |

Ophiolites are thought to be slices of oceanic lithosphere (often formed above subduction zones) thrust onto continents during collision. Ocean drilling has confirmed the upper part of this sequence directly.

**Geophysics**:

- Seismic reflection and refraction: velocities match the layers; a reflector at a few km depth beneath fast ridges marks a thin melt lens on top of a crystal mush.
- Gravity: free-air anomaly near zero along most ridges (isostatic compensation); Bouguer anomalies are low beneath the axis, showing hot, low-density mantle with melt.
- EM (geoelectrical) surveys: high conductivity beneath the ridge shows partial melt in the upwelling mantle (see [07](07-physical-structure-of-the-earth.md)).

## Making oceanic crust (OCR 5.3.2(c); Eduqas G1.1c)

### What you need to know

Adiabatic (decompression) melting of the mantle; magma evolution; crystal mush zones; continuous and discontinuous magma chambers at fast and slow ridges. Eduqas: magma chambers interpreted from models and ocean survey data (for example ship-based surveys of slow ridges).

### Explained

1. Plates move apart; mantle rises passively beneath the ridge.
2. Decompression melting of about 10 to 20% of the peridotite produces basaltic magma, which rises and collects in the crust.
3. **Fast ridges**: a continuous, long-lived but thin melt lens sits on top of a large zone of crystal mush (partly crystallised magma). Melt erupts as pillow lavas and fills cracks as dykes; the mush crystallises as gabbro.
4. **Slow ridges**: magma supply is low and episodic; chambers are small, short-lived and discontinuous along the ridge. Much of the spreading is taken up by faulting, and mantle rock may be exposed on the sea floor in core complexes. Crust is thinner and more variable.
5. Fractional crystallisation in the chambers produces cumulate gabbros and evolves the erupted basalts slightly (MORB, mid-ocean ridge basalt).

## Hydrothermal processes and massive sulfides (OCR 5.3.2(d))

### What you need to know

Hydrothermal processes at ridges and the formation of massive sulfide ores; metasomatism; chalcophile elements.

### Explained

Cold seawater sinks through cracks in the young, hot crust, is heated near the magma chamber (to 350 to 400 °C), and reacts with basalt. The hot fluid becomes acidic and reducing and leaches metals (Cu, Zn, Fe, Pb) and sulfur from the rocks. This chemical change of the rock by fluids is **metasomatism**. The buoyant hot fluid rises and vents at the sea floor as **black smokers**: when it meets cold, oxygenated seawater, metal sulfides (pyrite, chalcopyrite, sphalerite) precipitate instantly as black particles and build chimneys and mounds. Accumulations of these sulfides form **volcanogenic massive sulfide (VMS) deposits**, rich ores of copper and zinc. Ancient examples are preserved in ophiolites (the Troodos copper deposits, mined since ancient times; Cyprus gave copper its name). The process is advection: heat and chemicals carried by moving fluid.

Unusual ecosystems around vents depend on chemosynthetic bacteria that use hydrogen sulfide for energy.

### Practical

**OCR PAG8 and PAG12**: model hydrothermal convection (heated water with dye), research and present data on ophiolites and vent systems.

### Common mistakes

- Saying ridges are high because of volcanic piles. They are high because the lithosphere is hot and less dense.
- Calling the sheeted dykes "a single giant dyke".

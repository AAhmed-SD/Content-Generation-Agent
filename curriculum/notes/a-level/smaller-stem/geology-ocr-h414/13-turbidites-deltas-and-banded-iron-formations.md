# Applied sedimentology: turbidites, deltas, Walther's law and banded iron formations

Some sedimentary processes are rare or hard to watch (turbidity currents in the deep sea) and some no longer happen at all (banded iron formations). Geologists understand them with physical principles, laboratory models and comparisons with modern environments. Basic sediment transport and the Hjulström curve are in [03](03-sedimentary-rocks-weathering-and-diagenesis.md).

## Physical principles and models (OCR 5.1.1(a); Eduqas G1.3a)

### What you need to know

- Stokes' law (recall not required): v = g d² (ρp − ρw) ÷ (18η).
- Bedforms, flow velocity and grain size (flume studies).
- Flocculation of clay.
- Phi scale: recall and use φ = −log₂(D ÷ D₀).
- Model sedimentation in practicals: graded bedding, ripples, delta mouth switching.
- Eduqas: apply the Hjulström graph; slope and intercept of linear graphs.

### Explained

**Stokes' law** gives the settling velocity v of a small sphere in still fluid: g gravity, d diameter, ρp particle density, ρw fluid density, η fluid viscosity. Because v ∝ d², halving the grain size quarters the settling velocity. So fine clay settles extremely slowly, which explains why clay stays in suspension and why graded beds form (coarse grains settle first). It applies only to small grains (silt and clay) in still water.

**Flume studies**: a channel with sand and flowing water. As velocity rises over fine to medium sand, the bed changes: flat bed with no movement, then ripples, then dunes (larger, with cross-bedding), then upper flat bed (planar lamination), then antidunes. Coarse sand skips ripples. So bedform type and cross-bedding scale indicate ancient flow velocity and grain size.

**Flocculation**: see [03](03-sedimentary-rocks-weathering-and-diagenesis.md). In salt water, clays clump and settle faster, so estuaries and deltas trap mud.

### Worked example

Using Stokes' law: a quartz grain d = 0.05 mm (5 × 10⁻⁵ m), ρp = 2650 kg m⁻³, ρw = 1000 kg m⁻³, η = 1.0 × 10⁻³ Pa s, g = 9.81 m s⁻². v = 9.81 × (5 × 10⁻⁵)² × 1650 ÷ (18 × 10⁻³) = 9.81 × 2.5 × 10⁻⁹ × 1650 ÷ 0.018 = 2.25 × 10⁻³ m s⁻¹ (about 2 mm s⁻¹). A grain one-tenth that size settles 100 times more slowly.

### Practical

**OCR PAG4**: settling tube: drop mixed sediment into a tall water column and observe graded bedding; flume or tray: increase flow to form ripples; delta model: sand fed into a tank with a slope, watch the channel switch (avulsion) as the delta lobe builds up. Control water depth, sediment volume and slope.

## Turbidity currents and the Bouma sequence (OCR 5.1.1(b); Eduqas G1.3a)

### What you need to know

- Turbidity currents as dense, sediment-laden flows down underwater slopes; the Bouma model of a turbidite.
- Sole structures (flute casts, tool marks), rip-up clasts, climbing ripples, laminations; rates of deposition of turbidites compared with calcareous and siliceous oozes.

### Explained

A turbidity current is a fast, turbulent flow of water carrying suspended sediment, denser than the surrounding water, so it rushes down continental slopes and submarine canyons. It can be triggered by earthquakes, slope failure or floods. As it slows on the basin floor it deposits a turbidite.

**Bouma sequence** (base to top), recording a waning current:

| Division | Description | Process |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Erosive base | Flute casts and tool marks (grooves cut by objects dragged along), seen as casts on the sandstone base; load casts; rip-up clasts of mud | High-velocity erosion of underlying mud |
| A | Massive or graded coarse sand to granules | Rapid dumping from suspension |
| B | Parallel-laminated sand | Upper flat bed |
| C | Cross-laminated sand, ripples, climbing ripples | Lower velocity, ripples migrating while sediment still falling |
| D | Parallel-laminated silt | Settling from a dilute current |
| E | Mud (pelagic or turbidite mud) | Slow settling after the current, then normal deep-sea mud |

Repeated turbidites give rhythmic sandstone-shale sequences (greywacke and mudstone, as in the Welsh Basin). Each turbidite is deposited in hours to days, while deep-sea oozes (calcareous and siliceous plankton) accumulate at only millimetres per thousand years. So the turbidite thickness represents almost no time compared with the thin mud between.

## Deltas (OCR 5.1.1(c), (d))

### What you need to know

- Simple delta model (topset, foreset, bottomset) and more complex deltaic cyclothems: delta top (peat or coal, seat earth, channel sandstone), mouth bars, delta front, prodelta.
- Walther's law; comparison of Carboniferous delta sequences with modern deltas.

### Explained

A delta forms where a river enters a standing body of water, slows, and deposits sediment faster than waves and tides remove it.

**Simple (Gilbert) model**: horizontal topsets (river deposits on top), steeply inclined foresets (sand avalanching down the front), and fine bottomsets (mud on the floor in front). Common in lakes.

**Marine delta environments**:

- Prodelta: offshore muds settling from suspension.
- Delta front and mouth bars: sands deposited where the channel meets the sea; coarser upwards as the delta builds out.
- Delta top (plain): distributary channels (sandstone), levees, swamps with peat (which becomes coal), and seat earths (fossil soils with roots below coal seams).

As the delta progrades (builds seaward), a vertical section shows a **coarsening-upward sequence**: prodelta mud → delta front silt and sand → mouth bar sand → delta-top channel sands and coal.

**Delta switching** (avulsion): the active lobe builds out, then the river breaks through its levee to a shorter route. The abandoned lobe subsides and is flooded by the sea, laying marine mud on top. The cycle repeats.

**Cyclothem**: a repeated sequence (in the Carboniferous Coal Measures of Britain) of marine shale with marine fossils → coarsening-upward mudstone, siltstone, sandstone → seat earth with roots → coal → marine shale again. Each records delta progradation, swamp growth, then flooding (by switching, subsidence, or sea-level rise). Modern analogues: the Mississippi delta (lobes switching every 1000 or so years).

## Walther's law (OCR 5.1.1(d); Eduqas G1.3b)

### What you need to know

- In a conformable vertical sequence, the facies that succeed one another vertically were deposited side by side laterally.
- Marine transgressions and regressions; diachronous boundaries; extending 2D data (logs, sections) to 3D.

### Explained

Environments sit side by side (beach next to shoreface next to offshore). As they shift (the shoreline moving seaward or landward), each environment's deposits are laid on top of its neighbour's. So a vertical sequence records lateral changes, provided there are no gaps (unconformities).

- **Regression** (sea retreats, or delta progrades): shallower facies on deeper ones: coarsening upwards.
- **Transgression** (sea advances): deeper facies on shallower ones: fining upwards.

Facies boundaries are **diachronous**: the beach sand at one place is older than the same beach sand further inland during a transgression. Using Walther's law with borehole logs, geologists predict where reservoir sands will continue between wells (3D interpretation).

### Worked example

A borehole log shows, from base: marine mudstone, siltstone with marine shells, cross-bedded sandstone, rooted seat earth, coal. Walther's law: offshore, delta front, mouth bar or channel, swamp were neighbouring environments. The delta was building seaward (regression). A borehole further seaward would show the same sequence but with more mudstone and the coal thinner or absent.

## Banded iron formations (OCR 5.1.1(e))

### What you need to know

- BIFs deposited in the Palaeoproterozoic under different atmospheric and ocean chemistry; importance as iron ore; photoferrotrophs; the Great Oxidation Event (GOE); oxidation of Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺. Detailed redox chemistry not required; recognise minerals from given formulae.

### Explained

BIFs are rocks of alternating thin bands of iron oxides (haematite Fe₂O₃, magnetite Fe₃O₄) and chert (silica). They mostly formed 2.5 to 1.8 billion years ago and are the world's main source of iron ore (Western Australia, Brazil).

**Formation**: the early atmosphere and deep ocean had almost no free oxygen, so iron dissolved from hydrothermal vents and weathering stayed in solution as Fe²⁺. In shallow water, photosynthetic organisms (cyanobacteria producing oxygen, and photoferrotrophs, bacteria that use light to oxidise Fe²⁺ directly) converted Fe²⁺ to insoluble Fe³⁺, which precipitated as iron oxide layers. Alternating bands may record seasonal or cyclic changes in productivity and supply.

**The Great Oxidation Event** (about 2.4 billion years ago): oxygen began accumulating in the atmosphere. Once the oceans became oxygenated, dissolved Fe²⁺ was used up, so most BIF deposition ended. BIFs cannot form in today's oxygen-rich oceans, so there is no modern analogue: an example of uniformity of process but not of conditions.

### Common mistakes

- Saying turbidites form in shallow water because they contain sand.
- Applying Walther's law across an unconformity.
- Calling BIFs evaporites or volcanic deposits.

# Fossils and the fossil record

Fossils are evidence of past life preserved in rocks. They tell us about ancient organisms, how they lived and the environments where they lived, and they are the basis of relative dating ([06](06-geological-time-and-dating.md)). Evolution of specific groups is in [23](23-evolution-and-applied-palaeontology.md).

## Body and trace fossils; preservation (OCR 2.2.1(a); Eduqas F3.1f)

### What you need to know

- Fossils are the preserved remains of organisms (body fossils) or traces of their activity (trace fossils: burrows, tracks, trails; Eduqas also coprolites).
- Modes of preservation: actual remains, unaltered hard parts, petrification by replacement (calcification, silicification, pyritisation), carbonisation, moulds and casts.

### Explained

| Mode | What happens | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Actual remains | Soft tissue preserved by freezing, amber or tar | Insects in amber; mammoths in permafrost |
| Unaltered hard parts | Original shell or bone kept | Young bivalve shells |
| Replacement (petrification) | Original material dissolved and replaced molecule by molecule by another mineral, keeping detail | Calcite (calcification), silica (silicification, for example petrified wood), pyrite (pyritisation, in oxygen-poor muds, for example pyritised ammonites) |
| Carbonisation | Volatiles driven off under pressure, leaving a thin carbon film | Plant leaves, graptolites |
| Mould | Shell dissolves leaving its impression; external mould (outside), internal mould (infill of the inside) | Bivalve moulds in sandstone |
| Cast | A mould later filled by sediment or mineral | Cast of a shell |

Trace fossils are formed in place, so they cannot be transported without being destroyed. This makes them reliable evidence of the environment where the organism lived.

## The fossil record: bias and reliability (OCR 2.2.1(b); Eduqas F3.1g, F3.1h)

### What you need to know

- Taphonomy: what happens to an organism between death and discovery.
- Life assemblages (preserved where they lived, without much transport) and death assemblages (transported before burial); derived fossils (eroded from older rock and redeposited).
- Preservation potential; the record is biased (towards marine organisms with resistant hard parts living in low-energy environments and buried rapidly) and incomplete (predation, scavenging, bacterial decay, diagenesis, weathering, erosion, metamorphism destroy evidence).
- Morphological definition of species (species recognised from their form, since breeding cannot be tested).

### Explained

**Preservation potential** is high when: the organism has hard parts (shell, bone, wood); it lives where sediment accumulates (seas, lakes); burial is rapid (protects from scavengers and decay); conditions are anoxic (slow decay). Most soft-bodied organisms and land animals in eroding upland areas are rarely preserved. Exceptional deposits that preserve soft parts are Lagerstätten ([25](25-lagerstatten.md)).

| Life assemblage | Death assemblage |
| --- | --- |
| Fossils in growth or life position (burrowers upright in burrows, corals attached) | Fossils broken, worn, sorted by size, or jumbled |
| Range of ages and sizes | Often similar sizes (sorted by currents) |
| Bivalves with both valves together, closed | Valves separated; may be concave-up or convex-up depending on current |
| Delicate parts intact | Fragments, possibly from different environments mixed |
| Low energy | Higher energy, transport |

**Derived fossils** are older fossils eroded and redeposited in younger sediment. They can give a false (too old) age.

**Morphological species**: palaeontologists define fossil species by shape (morphology). This may split one variable species into several, or join different species that look alike.

## Fossil groups (OCR 2.2.2(b) basic identification; Eduqas F3.1d, F3.1e, SP16, SP17)

### What you need to know

Identify the main groups from their hard parts (Eduqas lists these features in detail):

- Brachiopods (marine): shell shape and symmetry, pedicle and brachial valves, foramen, hinge line, muscle scars.
- Bivalves (marine and freshwater): shape and symmetry, number and size of muscle scars, hinge line, teeth and sockets, gape, pallial line and sinus, umbones.
- Cephalopods (marine): coiled and chambered shell, suture lines.
- Corals (marine): colonial or solitary, septa.
- Trilobites (marine): cephalon, glabella, genal spines, eyes, thorax (number of segments), pygidium.
- Graptolites (marine): stipes, thecae.
- Plants (land): leaf, stem, root.
- Trace fossils: tracks and trails, burrows, coprolites.
- Eduqas: function and mode of life from morphology for bivalves (burrowing or not) and trilobites (benthonic or pelagic).

### Explained

**Brachiopods vs bivalves** (both have two valves):

| Feature | Brachiopod | Bivalve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Symmetry | Plane of symmetry cuts through each valve (each valve symmetrical); valves differ in size | Plane of symmetry between the valves (valves mirror each other); each valve usually asymmetrical |
| Valves | Pedicle valve (larger, with a hole, the foramen, for the fleshy stalk) and brachial valve | Left and right valves |
| Attachment | Pedicle through foramen | Byssus threads, cementing, or free |
| Hinge | Teeth and sockets on hinge line | Teeth and sockets; ligament |
| Muscle scars | Several small scars | One or two large adductor scars |
| Habitat | Marine only | Marine and freshwater |

Bivalve mode of life: deep burrowers have elongate, thin, smooth shells, a gape (gap where the siphons and foot protrude) and a pallial sinus (indent in the pallial line, where siphons were withdrawn), so a deep sinus means long siphons and deep burrowing. Shallow burrowers have thicker, rounded, ornamented shells and little or no sinus. Non-burrowers may be attached by byssus (mussels), cemented (oysters, irregular shape), or free-lying, sometimes swimming (scallops, with a flat shape).

**Cephalopods** (ammonoids, nautiloids, belemnites): chambered shell; septa divide chambers; where a septum meets the outer shell it forms a suture line. The animal lived in the last (body) chamber; the others held gas for buoyancy, linked by a tube (siphuncle). Free-swimming (nektonic).

**Corals**: solitary (one horn-shaped corallite) or colonial (many corallites). Septa are radial vertical plates. Groups: rugose (solitary or colonial, septa in multiples of four on the hand specimen, Palaeozoic), tabulate (always colonial, tiny septa, horizontal tabulae, Palaeozoic), scleractinian (modern and Mesozoic onward, six-fold septa).

**Trilobites**: three lobes along the body (a central axis and two side pleural lobes) and three parts head to tail: cephalon (head, with the raised glabella in the centre, eyes and often genal spines), thorax (segments, allowing enrolment), pygidium (tail shield). Benthonic (bottom-dwelling) forms: flattened, often with large or no eyes, wide genal spines spreading weight on soft mud; burrowers may be blind. Pelagic (swimming) forms: streamlined, large wrap-around eyes, light bodies. Detail in [23](23-evolution-and-applied-palaeontology.md).

**Graptolites**: colonial, planktonic; each stipe (branch) carries cups (thecae) each housing an individual. Preserved as carbon films in black shales. Number of stipes and shape of thecae change rapidly through time (see [06](06-geological-time-and-dating.md)).

**Plants**: leaves, stems and roots (for example roots in seat-earths below coal seams, *Stigmaria*).

**Trace fossils**: tracks (footprints) and trails (continuous grooves), burrows (vertical dwelling burrows in high-energy sands; horizontal feeding burrows in quiet muds), coprolites (fossil dung).

## Fossils as palaeoenvironmental indicators (OCR 2.2.1(c); Eduqas F3.1d)

### What you need to know

- Trace fossils show behaviour (dwelling, protection, feeding, qualitative locomotion) and environment.
- Body fossils: skeleton thickness, robustness, ornament, sensory organs, and geopetal structures (way-up indicators inside fossils).

### Explained

- Vertical dwelling burrows (for example U-shaped burrows) protect animals in shifting, high-energy sands of shorelines; horizontal grazing traces and spiral feeding patterns occur in quiet, deep, food-poor muds where animals search systematically. Tracks show whether an animal walked, crawled or swam.
- Thick, robust shells with strong ribs indicate high-energy water; thin, delicate shells indicate quiet water. Spines may stop sinking into soft mud or deter predators.
- Large eyes suggest active swimmers or dim light; no eyes suggest burrowing or deep, dark water.
- Corals need warm, shallow, clear, well-lit marine water.
- Geopetal structures: sediment partly fills a shell cavity, the rest filled later by calcite. The flat top of the sediment was horizontal when deposited, so it shows the way-up and original horizontal.

### Practical

**OCR PAG5 / Eduqas SP16, SP17**: classify unknown fossils with a key using distinguishing features; produce scaled, annotated drawings using a hand lens or microscope, including a scale bar and labels of diagnostic features. Calculate magnification as image size ÷ real size.

### Common mistakes

- Saying brachiopods and bivalves are closely related because both have two shells. They are different phyla.
- Saying all fossils are found where the organism lived.
- Drawing fossils with shading and no scale.

## Mark scheme

1. Burial in oxygen-poor mud with sulfate-reducing bacteria (1); original material replaced / infilled by pyrite (1).
2. Mould: impression left after the shell dissolves (1); cast: mould later filled with sediment or mineral giving a replica (1).
3. Any three: hard parts favoured; marine favoured; low-energy / depositional environments favoured; rapid burial needed; soft-bodied organisms rarely preserved; later destruction by erosion / metamorphism.
4. Brachiopod: plane of symmetry passes through each valve, valves unequal (1); bivalve: plane of symmetry between valves, valves mirror images (1).
5. Deep burrower (1); deep sinus means long siphons to reach the surface (1); elongate thin shell for moving through sediment (1).
6. Any three: broken / abraded fragments; separated valves; sorted by size; mixed species from different habitats; aligned by currents.
7. Sediment settled into the bottom of the cavity with a horizontal top (1); remaining space filled later with cement so flat surface points to original top / horizontal (1).
8. Formed in place / cannot be transported (1); record behaviour reflecting conditions such as energy, oxygen, food supply (1).

# The physical structure of the Earth

We cannot sample deeper than about 12 km, so most knowledge of the Earth's interior comes from indirect evidence: seismic waves, gravity, magnetism, electrical conductivity and density. Two separate ways of dividing the Earth must be kept apart: by **composition** (crust, mantle, core; see [08](08-origin-and-geochemistry-of-the-earth.md)) and by **mechanical behaviour** (lithosphere, asthenosphere, mesosphere, outer and inner core). The crust is not the lithosphere, and the Moho is not the base of the lithosphere.

## Layers by rheology (OCR 3.1.1(a), (c), (f); Eduqas F4.1a)

### What you need to know

- Rheology is how a material deforms (flows or breaks) under stress.
- Layers: lithosphere (rigid, brittle; crust plus uppermost mantle, divided into plates); asthenosphere (weak, rheid, plastic, with 1 to 5% partial melt); lower mantle (mesosphere, stronger); liquid outer core; solid inner core.

### Explained

| Layer | Depth (approx.) | Behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lithosphere | 0 to about 100 km (thin under ridges, thick under old continents) | Cold, rigid; breaks (brittle) near the surface, moves as plates |
| Asthenosphere | about 100 to 250 km (up to 700 km in some definitions) | Hot, weak, flows slowly (a rheid: a solid that flows over long times); small partial melt |
| Mesosphere (lower mantle) | to 2900 km | Solid, stronger owing to pressure, but convects slowly |
| Outer core | 2900 to 5150 km | Liquid; flows |
| Inner core | 5150 to 6370 km | Solid, owing to pressure |

The asthenosphere's weakness allows the lithosphere plates to move over it.

## Seismic evidence (OCR 3.1.1(b); Eduqas F4.1a, F4.1b)

### What you need to know

- P (primary, compressional) and S (secondary, shear) body waves, and surface waves; time-distance curves.
- Velocity changes show layers; refraction and the P and S shadow zones; how the state and depth of the outer and inner core are found.
- Velocity-depth models; density distribution with depth.
- Continental and oceanic crust: thickness, composition, structure; the Moho; ocean drilling (Eduqas: for example JOIDES Resolution expedition 360 attempting to drill to the Moho).

### Explained

**Waves**:

- P waves: compressional; travel through solids, liquids and gases; fastest.
- S waves: shear; travel only through solids (liquids have no shear strength); about 0.6 times P speed.
- Surface waves: slowest, largest amplitude, cause most damage.

Velocity increases with rigidity and decreases with density in the wave equations; in practice velocities mostly increase with depth because rigidity rises faster than density. Waves refract (bend) at boundaries where velocity changes, and also curve gradually as velocity increases with depth.

**Key boundaries**:

- Mohorovičić discontinuity (Moho): base of the crust; P velocity jumps from about 6 to 7 to about 8 km s⁻¹. About 7 km under oceans, 30 to 70 km under continents.
- Low velocity zone (about 100 to 250 km): P and S slow down slightly; evidence for partial melt in the asthenosphere.
- Gutenberg discontinuity (2900 km): core-mantle boundary. P velocity drops sharply (from about 13.6 to 8 km s⁻¹) and S waves stop, so the outer core is liquid.
- Lehmann discontinuity (about 5150 km): P velocity increases; inner core solid (weak S waves converted from P can travel through it).

**Shadow zones**:

- S-wave shadow zone: beyond about 103° from the epicentre no direct S waves arrive, because they cannot pass through the liquid outer core. The size of the shadow tells us the radius of the core.
- P-wave shadow zone: between about 103° and 142°, P waves are refracted strongly at the core boundary (velocity drops), bending them away and leaving a zone with no direct P arrivals. Faint P waves found in the shadow zone revealed the solid inner core (Lehmann, 1936).

**Crust types**:

| | Oceanic crust | Continental crust |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thickness | 6 to 10 km | 30 to 70 km |
| Composition | Basalt and gabbro (mafic) | Granitic upper crust, more mafic lower crust (average intermediate) |
| Density | about 3.0 g cm⁻³ | about 2.7 g cm⁻³ |
| Age | under 200 million years | up to 4 billion years |
| Structure | Layered (sediment, pillow lavas, sheeted dykes, gabbro; see [16](16-mid-ocean-ridges.md)) | Complex, deformed |

Ocean drilling recovers real samples: deep holes in oceanic crust have reached gabbro but not yet the Moho; results suggest the Moho in slow-spreading crust may be a boundary where altered mantle (serpentinite) meets fresh mantle, not always a clean crust-mantle boundary, so its interpretation is being revised.

## Gravity and isostasy (OCR 3.1.1(d))

### What you need to know

- Gravity anomalies: free-air and Bouguer.
- Isostatic equilibrium and isostatic rebound as evidence of the behaviour of lithosphere and asthenosphere.

### Explained

A gravity anomaly is the difference between measured gravity and the value expected for that place.

- **Free-air correction** removes the effect of height above sea level (gravity weakens with distance from the centre). The free-air anomaly is large over mountains if they are not compensated.
- **Bouguer correction** also removes the attraction of the rock between the station and sea level. The Bouguer anomaly reveals density differences below. Over large mountain ranges, Bouguer anomalies are strongly negative, showing low-density roots beneath them.

**Isostasy**: the lithosphere "floats" on the weaker asthenosphere, like blocks of wood in water. A thick, high-standing mountain range has a deep root of low-density crust. Where a region is in isostatic equilibrium, the free-air anomaly is close to zero.

**Isostatic rebound**: when a load is removed (ice sheets melting, erosion), the lithosphere slowly rises as asthenosphere flows back beneath. Scandinavia and Scotland are still rising after the last ice age; raised beaches show this. The rate of rebound gives the viscosity of the mantle. Adding a load (ice, sediment, volcanoes) makes the lithosphere sink.

### Worked example

Ice 2000 m thick (density 0.9 g cm⁻³) sits on mantle (3.3 g cm⁻³). At equilibrium, depression = 2000 × 0.9 ÷ 3.3 ≈ 545 m. When the ice melts, the land can rise by up to about 545 m.

## EM surveys at ridges (OCR 3.1.1(e))

### What you need to know

Electromagnetic surveys detect electrical conductivity; partially molten rock conducts much better than solid rock, so EM surveys can map the asthenosphere and melt beneath mid-ocean ridges.

### Explained

Molten rock and saline fluids conduct electricity well. Magnetotelluric and controlled-source EM surveys measure how natural or induced electromagnetic fields vary with depth. Beneath mid-ocean ridges, a zone of high conductivity shows where partial melt is present, outlining the asthenosphere and the melt supply to the ridge (see [16](16-mid-ocean-ridges.md)).

## Density of the Earth (OCR 3.1.1(g); Eduqas F4.1a, SP18)

### What you need to know

The whole-Earth mean density (about 5.5 g cm⁻³) is much greater than surface rocks (about 2.7 to 3.0 g cm⁻³), so the interior must be much denser: the core must be metallic.

### Explained

Mean density = mass ÷ volume. Mass comes from gravity measurements; volume from the radius. 5.5 g cm⁻³ against crustal rocks of 2.7 to 3.0 means deeper material must average well above 5.5. Mantle rocks (peridotite) are about 3.3 at the surface and denser at depth; the core must be about 10 to 13 g cm⁻³, matching iron-nickel under pressure (and iron meteorites).

### Worked example

Earth's radius 6.37 × 10⁶ m, mass 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg. Volume = (4/3)πr³ = 1.08 × 10²¹ m³. Density = 5.97 × 10²⁴ ÷ 1.08 × 10²¹ = 5510 kg m⁻³ = 5.5 g cm⁻³.

### Practical

**Eduqas SP18**: measure the densities of representative rocks (granite, basalt, peridotite) by mass and displacement, and compare with the mean density of the Earth to argue for a dense core.

## The geodynamo (OCR 3.1.1(h); Eduqas F4.1a)

### What you need to know

The Earth's magnetic field probably comes from a geodynamo: convection in a rotating, electrically conducting fluid (the liquid iron outer core). This is indirect evidence for a liquid metallic outer core.

### Explained

Heat from the inner core and the latent heat of crystallisation drive convection in the liquid iron-nickel outer core. The Earth's rotation organises the flow into columns. Moving conducting fluid in a magnetic field induces electric currents, which sustain the field (a self-sustaining dynamo). The field reverses irregularly, recorded in rocks as palaeomagnetism ([09](09-plate-tectonics.md)). A permanent magnet is impossible because the interior is above the Curie temperature.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the asthenosphere is liquid or is the magma source for all volcanoes.
- Saying S waves are absorbed by the mantle. They pass through the solid mantle but not the liquid outer core.
- Saying lithosphere and crust are the same.

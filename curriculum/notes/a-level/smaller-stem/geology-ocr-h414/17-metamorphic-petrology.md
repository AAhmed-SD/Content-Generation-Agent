# Metamorphic petrology

Metamorphic rocks record the temperatures and pressures they experienced, and the stresses that deformed them. This file covers how to read those conditions from minerals and fabrics, and how rock type and conditions control the style of deformation. The basic rock names are in [04](04-metamorphic-rocks.md).

## Mineral stability, grade and facies (OCR 5.4.1(a); Eduqas G1.2a, G1.2b, G1.2c)

### What you need to know

- Minerals are stable or metastable at the conditions where they formed; changing temperature or directed stress over time grows new minerals with different stability fields.
- Mineral changes depend on parent rock composition and the temperature-pressure field.
- Metamorphic grade; metamorphic facies and stability fields; index minerals in mudstone: chlorite, biotite, garnet, kyanite, sillimanite; the Al₂SiO₅ polymorphs (andalusite, kyanite, sillimanite); retrograde metamorphism.
- Contact and regional metamorphism of mudstone and shale from low to high grade.
- Simple pressure-temperature-time (P-T-t) paths for contact and regional metamorphism.

### Explained

**Index minerals in pelites** (regional metamorphism, Barrovian zones first mapped in the Scottish Highlands by George Barrow): chlorite → biotite → garnet → (staurolite) → kyanite → sillimanite. Each first appears at a particular grade. The parent must be aluminium-rich (mudstone) for these to form; a sandstone or limestone at the same conditions forms quartzite or marble, which do not record grade clearly.

**Al₂SiO₅ polymorphs**: andalusite, kyanite and sillimanite have the same composition but different crystal structures, each stable in a different field on a P-T diagram that meets at a triple point (about 500 °C and 0.4 GPa):

- Andalusite: low pressure (contact metamorphism, chiastolite in aureoles).
- Kyanite: high pressure, moderate temperature (deep regional metamorphism).
- Sillimanite: high temperature.

Finding one of them places the rock in that field.

**Metamorphic facies**: a facies is a range of P-T conditions that produces a characteristic mineral assemblage in rocks of a given composition (often named from metabasalt minerals). Examples: zeolite (low T and P), greenschist (chlorite, low-moderate), amphibolite (hornblende, plagioclase; moderate-high), granulite (very high T), blueschist (high P, low T: subduction zones, with blue amphibole glaucophane), eclogite (very high P), hornfels facies (high T, low P: contact aureoles).

**P-T-t paths**:

- Contact: pressure stays roughly constant (shallow); temperature rises then falls as the intrusion cools: a horizontal loop at low pressure.
- Regional (collision): burial raises pressure and temperature; peak temperature is often reached after peak pressure, as heat conducts in; then uplift and erosion lower both: a clockwise loop.
- Subduction: pressure rises much faster than temperature (cold slab): blueschist then eclogite.

**Retrograde metamorphism**: as rocks cool, high-grade minerals may change back to lower-grade ones if water is available (garnet altering to chlorite). Usually limited, because water has been driven off, which is why high-grade assemblages survive at the surface.

### Practical

**Eduqas SP20**: the "metamorphic aureole" simulation: model heat spreading from a hot "intrusion" into surrounding material (for example a heated block or a model with thermochromic material) and relate temperature with distance to the zones of an aureole. **OCR PAG7**: plot isograds on a map from mineral data.

## Isograds (OCR 5.4.1(a)(ii))

An isograd is a line on a map joining points where an index mineral first appears. It represents a surface of roughly equal metamorphic grade. Spacing shows the gradient: closely spaced isograds mean a rapid change in grade (steep thermal gradient, as around an intrusion); widely spaced isograds mean gradual change (regional). Isograds cutting across folds show metamorphism outlasted or post-dated folding.

### Worked example

On a map, the garnet isograd lies 4 km from the sillimanite isograd. If garnet first appears at about 500 °C and sillimanite at about 650 °C, the average horizontal gradient of peak temperature = 150 ÷ 4 = 37.5 °C km⁻¹.

## Metamorphic fabrics and textures (OCR 5.4.1(b); Eduqas G1.2d)

### What you need to know

- Fabrics form from directed stress over time during orogeny: slaty cleavage, schistosity, gneissose banding, porphyroblastic and granoblastic textures, crenulation cleavage. Use fabrics to reconstruct conditions.
- Eduqas: granoblastic, porphyroblastic, mylonitic textures; contact, regional and dynamic metamorphism with recrystallisation, ductile flow and shear.
- Recognise fabrics in samples, photographs and thin-section diagrams.

### Explained

| Fabric | Description | Conditions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slaty cleavage | Very fine aligned clay and mica; rock splits into sheets | Low grade, compression |
| Schistosity | Visible aligned mica flakes, wavy | Medium grade |
| Gneissose banding | Segregated light (quartz, feldspar) and dark (biotite, hornblende) bands | High grade |
| Porphyroblastic | Large new crystals (porphyroblasts, for example garnet) in a finer matrix; may contain inclusion trails | Growth during or after deformation |
| Granoblastic | Equal-sized interlocking polygonal grains, no alignment | Contact metamorphism or high-grade with no stress (hornfels, marble, quartzite) |
| Crenulation cleavage | An earlier cleavage folded into small crinkles, with a new cleavage along the crinkle limbs | Two deformation events |
| Mylonitic | Very fine, streaky, strongly sheared rock with stretched grains | Dynamic metamorphism in ductile shear zones |

Inclusion trails in porphyroblasts preserve an earlier fabric. If the trails are straight and continuous with the matrix fabric, the crystal grew after deformation; if curved (S-shaped), it rotated as it grew during deformation.

## Deformation style: competence and conditions (OCR 5.4.1(c); Eduqas G2.1a)

### What you need to know

- The composition of the rock (competent and incompetent) and conditions (strain rate, temperature, pressure) decide whether rocks fracture or fold.
- Shortening by slip, buckling and pressure solution; boudinage; refolding.

### Explained

- **Competent** rocks (sandstone, limestone, granite) are strong: they fracture or form open, rounded folds (buckling) and keep their thickness.
- **Incompetent** rocks (shale, mudstone, salt) are weak: they flow, thicken in fold hinges and thin on limbs (similar folds), and develop cleavage.
- In mixed sequences, competent layers buckle while incompetent layers flow around them. Slip between layers (flexural slip) allows folding.
- **Pressure solution**: minerals dissolve where stress is highest and redeposit elsewhere, shortening rock without fracturing (stylolites, cleavage seams).
- **Boudinage**: when a competent layer within incompetent rock is stretched, it necks and breaks into sausage-shaped blocks (boudins) while the weaker rock flows into the gaps.
- **Refolding**: a second deformation folds earlier folds, producing interference patterns (domes and basins, hooks).
- Higher temperature and confining pressure, and slower strain rate, favour ductile behaviour (see [11](11-rock-deformation-and-structures.md)).

### Common mistakes

- Using index minerals in a limestone or sandstone. They need an aluminium-rich parent.
- Saying andalusite, kyanite and sillimanite are different compositions.

## Mark scheme

1. Mudstone is Al-rich so forms a series of index minerals at different grades (1); quartz sandstone only recrystallises to quartzite, same mineral at all grades (1).
2. Chlorite → biotite → garnet → (staurolite) → kyanite → sillimanite (2; 1 for partially correct order).
3. Kyanite: high pressure / deep burial, regional (1); garnet moderate-high temperature (1); andalusite: low pressure (1); with cordierite: high temperature contact metamorphism (1).
4. Clockwise loop (1); burial raises pressure first (1); temperature continues rising after peak pressure as heat conducts / radiogenic heat (1); uplift and erosion lower P and T (1).
5. Water driven off during prograde metamorphism (1); retrograde reactions need water so are slow / limited (1).
6. Granoblastic: equal-sized interlocking polygonal grains, no alignment (1); porphyroblastic: large new crystals in a finer matrix (1).
7. Competent layer within incompetent rock (1); stretched / extended (1); competent layer necks and breaks into blocks while weak rock flows into gaps (1).
8. Closely spaced: steep thermal gradient, e.g. near an intrusion (1); widely spaced: gradual change in regional metamorphism (1).

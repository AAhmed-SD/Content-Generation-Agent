# Metamorphic rocks

Metamorphic rocks form when an existing (parent) rock adjusts to new temperature and/or pressure conditions without melting. The shale-to-gneiss series shows the main ideas. Facies, isograds, P-T paths and advanced fabrics are in [17](17-metamorphic-petrology.md).

## Metamorphism (OCR 2.1.4(a); Eduqas F2.2f)

### What you need to know

- Metamorphism is a solid-state, isochemical process: the rock recrystallises and new minerals grow, but the overall chemical composition stays the same (apart from water and CO₂).
- Types: contact (thermal), regional and dynamic.
- It is a response to changes in temperature and/or pressure; Eduqas: interpret contact aureoles and metamorphic foliations on maps and photographs.

### Explained

Minerals are stable only over a range of temperature and pressure. When conditions change, atoms rearrange into minerals stable in the new conditions. This happens in the solid state, by diffusion, often helped by small amounts of fluid. If the rock melts, it becomes igneous instead.

| Type | Main cause | Setting | Scale | Typical texture |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Contact (thermal) | High temperature, low pressure | Around igneous intrusions | Metres to a few kilometres (the aureole) | Non-foliated, granular, hard, may be spotted |
| Regional | Raised temperature and directed pressure | Deep in mountain belts at convergent boundaries | Hundreds to thousands of square kilometres | Foliated |
| Dynamic | High shear stress, often at lower temperature | Fault and shear zones | Narrow zones | Crushed, sheared (mylonite) |

**Contact aureole**: the zone of metamorphosed country rock around an intrusion. Grade is highest at the contact and falls outwards. Width depends on the size and temperature of the intrusion, the country rock type and fluids. On a map, an aureole is a band of metamorphic rock around a granite, wider where the contact dips gently.

## Metamorphic rocks and their parents (OCR 2.1.4(b); Eduqas F2.2g, SP10, SP11)

### What you need to know

- OCR: metaquartzite and marble; slate, phyllite, schist and gneiss from fine-grained rocks.
- Eduqas: marble, metaquartzite, spotted rock, hornfels, slate, schist, gneiss.
- Non-foliated textures in contact metamorphism; foliation (slaty cleavage, schistosity, gneissose banding) in regional metamorphism.
- Use mineralogy and fabric to infer the parent rock.

### Explained

**Non-foliated rocks**:

| Rock | Parent | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Marble | Limestone | Interlocking calcite crystals (sugary), fizzes in acid, fossils destroyed, hardness 3 |
| Metaquartzite | Quartz sandstone (orthoquartzite) | Interlocking quartz, very hard, breaks through grains not round them |
| Spotted rock | Mudstone or shale, outer aureole | Dark spots of new minerals (for example cordierite, andalusite) growing in the original rock |
| Hornfels | Mudstone or shale, inner aureole | Very hard, fine-grained, splintery, dark, no foliation; may contain andalusite or chiastolite |

**Foliated rocks from mudstone (pelite)**, in order of increasing grade:

| Rock | Grain size | Foliation | Key minerals |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Slate | Very fine | Slaty cleavage: splits into thin flat sheets | Tiny aligned clays, chlorite, white mica |
| Phyllite | Fine | Shiny, wavy cleavage surfaces (sheen) | Fine micas |
| Schist | Medium to coarse | Schistosity: visible aligned micas, wavy | Mica, quartz, garnet porphyroblasts |
| Gneiss | Coarse | Gneissose banding: alternating light (quartz, feldspar) and dark (biotite, hornblende) bands | Feldspar, quartz, biotite |

**Foliation** forms when platy and elongate minerals (micas, chlorite) grow perpendicular to the greatest stress. In slate the cleavage may cut across the original bedding at any angle, which shows it is caused by stress not deposition.

**Inferring the parent**: metamorphism is isochemical, so the minerals show the chemistry. Calcite only means limestone; quartz only means sandstone; aluminium-rich minerals (micas, garnet, andalusite, kyanite) mean mudstone; hornblende and plagioclase with no quartz mean a basalt or dolerite (forming amphibolite).

## Metamorphic grade and index minerals (OCR 2.1.4(c); Eduqas F2.2g)

### What you need to know

- As the intensity of metamorphism (grade) increases, different minerals form; index minerals can be used to reconstruct the conditions.

### Explained

Grade is the intensity of metamorphism, mainly temperature. In regionally metamorphosed mudstones, the sequence of index minerals with increasing grade is: chlorite, biotite, garnet, (staurolite), kyanite, sillimanite. In contact aureoles in mudstone, andalusite (chiastolite) and cordierite are typical. The first appearance of an index mineral on a map is joined to form an isograd (see [17](17-metamorphic-petrology.md)).

Low grade: fine-grained, many hydrous minerals (chlorite, clays). High grade: coarse grains, fewer hydrous minerals (water driven off), foliation becomes banding.

### Worked example

Moving away from a granite contact into mudstone, a student records: hornfels with andalusite at 0 to 150 m; spotted rock at 150 to 400 m; unaltered mudstone beyond 400 m. The aureole is 400 m wide; grade decreases outward because temperature fell with distance from the hot intrusion.

### Practical

**OCR PAG1 / Eduqas SP10, SP11**: full description of metamorphic hand specimens and field exposures (colour, crystal size, minerals, fabric) and identification of minerals and textures from photomicrographs, to name the rock and deduce temperature and pressure conditions.

### Common mistakes

- Calling slate a sedimentary rock because it is fine and splits. Slaty cleavage is metamorphic.
- Saying metamorphism involves melting.
- Confusing cleavage in minerals with slaty cleavage in rocks.

## Mark scheme

1. Overall chemical composition of the rock does not change (1); only mineralogy / texture changes (apart from volatiles) (1).
2. Contact: heat from an intrusion, little pressure (1); local aureole, non-foliated (1). Regional: high temperature and directed pressure in mountain belts (1); large areas, foliated (1).
3. Marble (1); any two: interlocking calcite crystals / sugary texture; fizzes in dilute acid; scratched by steel / hardness 3; no fossils (2).
4. Compression / directed pressure (1); platy minerals (clays, micas) grow or rotate (1); aligned perpendicular to maximum stress so rock splits along them (1).
5. Grain size increases (1); slate fine cleavage, phyllite sheen, schist visible micas (1); new minerals appear, e.g. biotite, garnet (1); gneiss shows segregated light and dark bands (1).
6. Basalt / dolerite / mafic igneous rock (1); minerals rich in Ca, Fe, Mg and no free quartz, matching mafic composition (1).
7. Size / temperature of intrusion varies (1); dip of contact: gentle dip gives wider outcrop of aureole at the surface (1); country rock type / fluids differ (1).

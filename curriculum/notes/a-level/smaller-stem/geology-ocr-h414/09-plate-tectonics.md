# Plate tectonics

Plate tectonics is how the Earth loses its internal heat. Rigid lithospheric plates move over the weaker asthenosphere, and their boundaries control where earthquakes, volcanoes and mountain belts occur. GCSE background: [GCSE Geology 07](../../../gcse/smaller-stem/geology-eduqas/07-plate-tectonics.md).

## Heat transfer and the geotherm (OCR 3.2.1(a); Eduqas F2.2a)

### What you need to know

- Energy from inside the Earth drives internal processes; evidence for the geothermal gradient; convection, conduction and advection; estimates of geothermal flux.
- Rocks generally stay below their melting point except locally.

### Explained

- **Geothermal gradient (geotherm)**: temperature increase with depth. In continental crust near the surface it is about 25 to 30 °C per km (measured in mines and boreholes), decreasing with depth.
- **Conduction**: heat passes through solid rock from hot to cold; dominant through the lithosphere.
- **Convection**: hot, less dense mantle rises slowly and cold, dense material sinks, carrying heat.
- **Advection**: heat carried by moving material such as magma or hot water, for example at ridges and hot springs.
- **Heat flow** (W m⁻²) = thermal conductivity × geothermal gradient. Average about 0.09 W m⁻²; highest at mid-ocean ridges, lowest in old continental shields and over trenches.

### Worked example

A borehole shows 18 °C at 200 m and 63 °C at 1700 m. Gradient = (63 − 18) ÷ (1700 − 200) = 45 ÷ 1500 = 0.030 °C m⁻¹ = 30 °C km⁻¹. With conductivity 2.5 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹, heat flow = 2.5 × 0.030 = 0.075 W m⁻².

## Seismic evidence for plates (OCR 3.2.1(b), (c); Eduqas F4.2b, SP19)

### What you need to know

- Earthquakes define plate boundaries; plate interiors are largely aseismic.
- Benioff zones: earthquake foci on an inclined plane beneath trenches.
- Seismic tomography shows mantle structure (subducted slabs, upwelling).
- Interpret seismograms: distance from the epicentre from the S-P interval using time-distance curves; locate the epicentre; use seismograms to show shadow zones.
- Eduqas SP19: investigate relationships between focal depth, magnitude and distance from plate boundaries using Google Earth data; scatter graphs and correlation.

### Explained

Earthquakes mostly occur in narrow belts that outline plates. Shallow earthquakes occur at ridges and transform faults; at convergent margins, foci lie along a plane dipping beneath the overriding plate down to about 700 km (the **Benioff zone**), tracing the subducting slab.

**Seismic tomography**: many earthquake records at many stations are combined to map small differences in wave speed through the mantle. Faster (colder, denser) regions show sinking slabs, some reaching the core-mantle boundary; slower (hotter) regions show upwellings and plumes.

**Locating an epicentre**:

1. On a seismogram, read the time between the first P and first S arrivals (S-P interval).
2. Use a time-distance graph: the gap between the P and S curves increases with distance; find the distance where the gap equals the S-P interval.
3. Draw a circle of that radius around the station on a map.
4. Repeat for three stations; the circles intersect at the epicentre (triangulation).

### Worked example

P waves travel at 8 km s⁻¹ and S waves at 4.5 km s⁻¹. The S-P interval is 70 s. Time for distance d: d/4.5 − d/8 = 70, so d(0.2222 − 0.125) = 70; d = 70 ÷ 0.0972 ≈ 720 km.

## Reconstructing past plate movements (OCR 3.2.1(d); Eduqas F4.2b, F4.2d, F4.2e)

### What you need to know

- OCR: global distribution of features of the same age: orogenic belts (Caledonian orogeny and the Iapetus Ocean), palaeoenvironments, geomagnetics (apparent polar wandering curves), ocean-floor magnetic anomalies, volcanic zones, palaeoecology and glacial geology.
- Eduqas: remanent magnetism, ferromagnetism and Curie temperature; palaeomagnetism shows changes in latitude; ocean-floor magnetic anomalies show sea-floor spreading; ocean floor drilling; geothermal data; geomagnetic and geoelectrical properties.

### Explained

**Palaeomagnetism**: iron-bearing minerals (magnetite) are ferromagnetic. Above the **Curie temperature** (about 580 °C for magnetite) they are not magnetised; as a lava cools below it, their magnetic domains align with the Earth's field, locking in its direction (remanent magnetism). Sediments can also record the field as magnetic grains settle.

- The inclination (dip) of the recorded field depends on latitude: vertical at the magnetic poles, horizontal at the equator. So the inclination gives the palaeolatitude where the rock formed. tan I = 2 tan λ (inclination I, latitude λ).
- **Apparent polar wander (APW) curves**: plotting the pole position calculated from rocks of different ages on one continent gives a path. Since the pole did not really wander far, the continent moved. Different continents give different paths that line up when the continents are put back together, which is strong evidence for drift.

**Magnetic stripes**: the field reverses irregularly every few hundred thousand years. New basalt at a ridge records the current polarity, so parallel stripes of normal and reversed magnetisation form, symmetrical about the ridge. Their widths, matched to the dated reversal timescale, give spreading rates ([16](16-mid-ocean-ridges.md)).

**Other evidence**:

- Matching orogenic belts across oceans: the Caledonian mountain belt of Scotland, Scandinavia, Greenland and eastern North America formed when the Iapetus Ocean closed; the belts line up when the Atlantic is closed.
- Palaeoenvironments and palaeoecology: matching fossils (Glossopteris, Mesosaurus) and climate indicators (coal in Antarctica, glacial deposits in tropical Africa and India) in the wrong latitudes today.
- Ocean drilling: sediment is thin and young at ridges, older and thicker away from them; no ocean floor older than about 180 to 200 million years.
- Heat flow high at ridges, low at trenches.
- Volcanic zones along boundaries; hotspot chains.

## Mantle plumes (OCR 3.2.1(e))

### What you need to know

Evidence: relative plate motion, geological features, heat flow and seismic tomography.

### Explained

A mantle plume is a narrow column of hot mantle rising from deep (possibly the core-mantle boundary). Evidence:

- Hotspot island chains (Hawaii-Emperor) that become older away from the active volcano, showing the plate moving over a fixed source; the bend in the chain records a change in plate direction.
- Large volumes of basalt (flood basalts, Iceland's thick crust).
- High heat flow and a broad topographic swell.
- Seismic tomography shows slow (hot) columns under some hotspots.

The deep origin of plumes is still debated.

## GPS and geodesy (OCR 3.2.1(f); Eduqas F4.2b)

### What you need to know

Resolution and precision of GPS allow direct measurement of present relative plate movement; basic geodesy: the ellipsoid model of the Earth, the geoid, terrestrial reference frames. How GPS works is not required.

### Explained

- The **ellipsoid** is a smooth mathematical shape approximating the Earth (slightly flattened at the poles).
- The **geoid** is the shape the sea surface would take under gravity alone, extended under the continents; it has bumps and hollows of up to about 100 m caused by uneven mass distribution.
- A **terrestrial reference frame** is a fixed system of coordinates (defined by a global network of stations) against which positions can be measured over years.

Permanent GPS stations record their position to millimetre precision. Repeated measurements give velocities of a few cm per year, matching rates from magnetic stripes averaged over millions of years. GPS also shows strain building up across faults and ground deformation at volcanoes.

## Convection and driving forces (OCR 3.2.1(g), (h), (i); Eduqas F4.2c)

### What you need to know

- Subduction zones, plates (cold thermal boundary layer) and plumes act as the active limbs of convection.
- Ridge push: gravity and density differences; passive upwelling at divergent boundaries and sea-floor spreading.
- Slab pull at subduction zones versus ridge push; the forces are still debated.

### Explained

Older ideas pictured mantle convection cells dragging passive plates along. The modern view sees the plates themselves as the top of the convection system: the cold, dense lithosphere sinks at subduction zones (the downgoing limb), and plumes rise from below.

- **Slab pull**: old oceanic lithosphere is cold and denser than the asthenosphere below, so it sinks at subduction zones and pulls the rest of the plate. Plates with long subducting edges (Pacific) move fastest, suggesting slab pull is the largest force.
- **Ridge push**: the ridge stands high because the lithosphere there is hot and thin. The lithosphere slides down the gentle slope away from the ridge under gravity. Mantle beneath the ridge rises passively to fill the gap (decompression melting).
- Other forces: drag at the base of plates, resistance at collision zones.

## How the theory developed (OCR 3.2.1(j); Eduqas F4.2a)

### What you need to know

- The paradigm emerged from earlier, less complete models: contraction theory, geosynclines, continental drift, mantle convection carrying passive plates. OCR does not require names and dates; Eduqas asks for the development from continental drift through active mantle convection carrying passive plates to slab pull and ridge push.
- Interpret developing models; evidence from ocean basin research, continental blocks, isostasy and radioactivity.

### Explained

1. **Contraction theory** (19th century): the Earth was cooling and shrinking, wrinkling the crust like a drying apple to form mountains. Against it: mountains are concentrated in belts, not evenly spread; radioactivity (discovered 1896) showed the Earth was not simply cooling; isostasy showed continents float on denser material.
2. **Geosynclines**: thick sedimentary basins that subsided and were later squeezed into mountains, with vertical movements. They explained thick sequences but not the cause of compression.
3. **Continental drift** (early 20th century): continents moved across the ocean floor. Evidence: jigsaw fit, matching fossils, rocks and mountain belts, past climates. Rejected mainly because no plausible mechanism.
4. **Mantle convection** was proposed as a mechanism, with plates carried passively.
5. **Ocean basin research** (1950s and 1960s): mapping of ridges and trenches, magnetic stripes, young ocean floor and sea-floor spreading. Earthquakes along Benioff zones showed subduction.
6. **Plate tectonics** (late 1960s): rigid plates with three boundary types; then slab pull and ridge push recognised as major driving forces.

## Plate boundaries and the rock cycle (Eduqas F4.2a, F4.2b, F4.2f)

| Boundary | Motion | Features | Rock cycle links |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Divergent (constructive) | Apart | Mid-ocean ridges, rift valleys; shallow earthquakes; basalt volcanism | Basaltic magmatism at spreading centres |
| Convergent (destructive) ocean-ocean or ocean-continent | Together, with subduction | Trench, Benioff zone, volcanic arc (andesite), fold mountains | Andesitic and basaltic magmatism; regional metamorphism; sediment from rapid erosion of rising mountains |
| Convergent continent-continent | Together, collision | Fold mountains (Himalaya), thrusts, thick crust | Granitic magma from crustal melting; regional metamorphism |
| Conservative (transform) | Sideways | Transform faults, shallow earthquakes, no volcanism | |

Tectonics also controls sedimentation: uplift and erosion supply sediment; subsiding basins (rifts, foreland basins) collect it.

### Common mistakes

- Saying convection currents in the mantle are the only or main driving force.
- Confusing true polar wander with apparent polar wander.
- Saying magnetic stripes are caused by the Earth's poles moving.

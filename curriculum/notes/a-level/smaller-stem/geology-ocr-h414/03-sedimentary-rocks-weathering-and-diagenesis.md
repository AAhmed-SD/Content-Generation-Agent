# Sedimentary rocks: weathering, transport, classification and diagenesis

Sedimentary rocks record surface processes driven by external (solar) energy. This file follows sediment from weathering through transport and deposition to lithification, and covers how to classify and describe sedimentary rocks. Sedimentary environments and structures are in [12](12-uniformitarianism-facies-and-sedimentary-environments.md); turbidites, Stokes' law and flume work in [13](13-turbidites-deltas-and-banded-iron-formations.md).

## External energy (Eduqas F2.1a)

Solar heating drives the water cycle (evaporation, rain, rivers, glaciers), winds and waves, and so drives weathering and erosion. Gravity moves material downhill. This is the "surface" half of the rock cycle; internal heat drives the other half.

## Weathering (OCR 2.1.3(a); Eduqas F2.1b)

### What you need to know

- Physical (mechanical) weathering breaks rock into fragments: insolation (heating and cooling), freeze-thaw.
- Chemical weathering of silicate and carbonate rocks: hydrolysis, carbonation (and solution), oxidation; it produces new minerals (clays), solutions and resistant residual minerals.
- Biological weathering combines physical and chemical changes.
- Products: clastic material, clays, evaporites (gypsum, halite) and carbonate sediment. Recognise a mineral in a given equation (OCR: no need to recall formulae).

### Explained

**Physical**:

- Freeze-thaw: water in cracks expands by about 9% on freezing, wedging the rock apart; repeated cycles in cold, wet climates produce angular scree.
- Insolation: in deserts, daily heating and cooling expand and contract the outer layers and different minerals by different amounts, so the rock flakes or crumbles.

**Chemical**:

- Hydrolysis: water (with H⁺ ions) reacts with silicates such as feldspar, producing clay minerals (kaolinite) and ions in solution. Feldspar in granite weathers to clay, which is why granite breaks down into sand-sized quartz grains plus clay.
- Carbonation and solution: rainwater with dissolved CO₂ forms weak carbonic acid, which dissolves calcite: CaCO₃ + H₂O + CO₂ → Ca²⁺ + 2HCO₃⁻. Limestone is removed in solution.
- Oxidation: iron in minerals (pyroxene, olivine, biotite) reacts with oxygen, forming iron oxides (red-brown staining).
- Quartz is very resistant, so it survives as a residual mineral.

**Biological**: roots wedge cracks open (physical); burrowing animals expose rock; lichens and decaying organic matter release acids (chemical).

**Climate control**: chemical weathering is fastest in hot, wet climates; physical weathering dominates in cold or dry climates.

**Products**: clastic grains (rock fragments, quartz); clay minerals; ions in solution, which may later precipitate as evaporites (halite, gypsum) when water evaporates, or be used by organisms to build carbonate shells (carbonate sediment).

## Erosion, transport and deposition (OCR 2.1.3(b)(i); Eduqas F2.1c)

### What you need to know

- Erosion (abrasion, attrition); transport (traction, saltation, suspension, solution).
- The Hjulström curve; length of transport; maturity.
- Deposition sorts sediment: grain size relates to the energy of the environment; quartz and muscovite dominate coarse fractions and clay minerals the fine fraction; flocculation; precipitation.

### Explained

- Abrasion: particles carried by water, wind or ice scrape the bed. Attrition: particles knock into each other, becoming smaller and rounder.
- Traction: large grains roll and slide along the bed. Saltation: sand-sized grains bounce. Suspension: fine silt and clay are carried within the flow. Solution: dissolved ions.

**Hjulström curve** (velocity against grain size, log scales):

- The erosion curve shows the velocity needed to pick up a grain. It is lowest for fine sand (about 0.1 to 0.5 mm). Clay needs a higher velocity because clay particles are cohesive and lie smoothly on the bed.
- The settling (deposition) curve shows the velocity below which a moving grain is deposited. Fine clay stays in suspension at very low velocities.
- Between the curves, grains already moving keep moving (transport).

**Maturity**: the longer and more energetic the transport, the more the sediment is sorted, rounded and enriched in resistant minerals.

| | Immature | Mature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grain shape | Angular | Rounded |
| Sorting | Poor (mixed sizes) | Good (similar sizes) |
| Composition | Many minerals, including feldspar and rock fragments | Mostly quartz |
| Matrix | Much clay matrix | Little matrix |
| Example | Breccia, greywacke, arkose | Orthoquartzite |

**Flocculation**: clay particles carry negative charges and repel each other in fresh water. In salt water, positive ions neutralise the charge, so particles clump into larger flocs that settle quickly, for example in estuaries.

**Precipitation**: dissolved ions crystallise out when water evaporates (evaporites) or when conditions change (carbonate precipitation in warm, shallow seas).

## Grain size and sieve analysis (OCR 2.1.3(b)(ii), (c); Eduqas F2.1d, F2.1e)

### What you need to know

- Grain size classes: gravel (> 2 mm), sand (1/16 to 2 mm), silt (1/256 to 1/16 mm), clay (< 1/256 mm). Eduqas: coarse > 2 mm, fine < 1/16 mm.
- Sieve analysis; the phi scale (at AS, qualitative only for OCR); Folk and Ward formulae need not be recalled.
- Use of sediment comparators to judge size, shape (roundness) and sphericity.
- Statistics: frequency tables, histograms, cumulative frequency, mean, median, mode, standard deviation, interquartile range, normal and skewed distributions.

### Explained

**Phi scale**: φ = −log₂(D/D₀), where D is grain diameter in mm and D₀ = 1 mm. Each whole phi step halves or doubles the size: 1 mm = 0 φ, 2 mm = −1 φ, 0.5 mm = 1 φ, 0.25 mm = 2 φ, 1/16 mm = 4 φ. Coarse grains have negative phi. It converts a geometric scale to equal steps, so sieve data plot neatly.

**Sieve analysis**:

1. Dry and weigh a sample (disaggregate gently).
2. Shake through a stack of sieves with mesh sizes decreasing downwards (for example 2, 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.063 mm) for a fixed time.
3. Weigh the sediment retained on each sieve.
4. Calculate the percentage mass in each class; draw a histogram and a cumulative frequency curve.
5. Read median (φ50) and spread (for example φ84 − φ16) from the curve. Well-sorted sediment has a narrow spread (steep cumulative curve).

**Skewness**: beach sands tend to lose fines (negatively skewed, tail towards coarse grains); river and dune sands differ. Dune sands are very well sorted and fine to medium; river sands less well sorted.

### Worked example

A 200 g sample: 2 mm sieve 6 g, 1 mm 14 g, 0.5 mm 52 g, 0.25 mm 98 g, 0.125 mm 24 g, pan 6 g. Percentages: 3, 7, 26, 49, 12, 3. Modal class: 0.25 to 0.5 mm (1 to 2 φ, medium sand). Most of the sample is in two classes, so it is moderately well sorted.

### Practical

**OCR PAG4 / Eduqas F2.1e**: grain size analysis of sands from contrasting environments (river, beach, dune). Control sample mass, shaking time and sieve set; repeat; compare with a statistical test (for example a t-test or Mann-Whitney U on mean grain size). Safety: dust (mask), heavy sieve stacks.

## Classifying sedimentary rocks (OCR 2.1.3(d); Eduqas F2.1e, F2.1f, SP5, SP7)

### What you need to know

- OCR: siliciclastic rocks classified on colour, composition, grain size, grain shape and sorting (orthoquartzite, arkose, greywacke); carbonates by grain size, cement, composition, fossil content and sorting using the Dunham scheme (mudstone, wackestone, packstone, grainstone).
- Eduqas: identify sandstones (orthoquartzite, arkose, greywacke), shale/mudstone, limestones (shelly, oolitic, chalk), conglomerate, breccia; organic sedimentary rocks (limestone, coal) and chemical rocks (evaporites); analyse biogenic components.
- Describe macro and micro features from hand specimens, exposures and photomicrographs.

### Explained

**Siliciclastic rocks**:

| Rock | Grain size | Description | Origin |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Breccia | > 2 mm | Angular clasts, poorly sorted | Little transport: scree, fault zones, alluvial fans |
| Conglomerate | > 2 mm | Rounded pebbles in sand matrix | High-energy rivers, beaches |
| Orthoquartzite | Sand | Over 90% quartz, well rounded and sorted, silica cement, pale | Mature: beaches, deserts, after long transport |
| Arkose | Sand | Over 25% feldspar, angular, pink or red, poorly sorted | Rapid erosion of granite, arid climate, short transport |
| Greywacke | Sand with > 15% clay matrix | Dark, poorly sorted, angular, rock fragments | Turbidity currents (rapid deposition) |
| Mudstone / shale | < 1/16 mm | Fine; shale is fissile (splits into thin layers) | Quiet water: deep sea, lakes, lagoons |

**Carbonate rocks**: limestones are mostly calcite. Dunham classification is based on texture: is the rock mud-supported or grain-supported?

| Dunham name | Texture |
| --- | --- |
| Mudstone (carbonate) | Mud-supported, less than 10% grains |
| Wackestone | Mud-supported, more than 10% grains |
| Packstone | Grain-supported, with mud between grains |
| Grainstone | Grain-supported, no mud (spaces filled by calcite cement) |

Mud content shows energy: grainstones form where currents winnow away mud (high energy); mudstones in quiet water.

Common types: shelly (bioclastic) limestone of fossil fragments; oolitic limestone of ooids (spherical grains built up in concentric layers by rolling in warm, shallow, agitated water); chalk of microscopic coccoliths (deep, quiet, clear water); reef limestone of corals in growth position.

**Organic and chemical rocks**: coal forms from plant material buried in swamps (see the Eduqas coal file, [Eduqas 01](../geology-eduqas-a480qs/01-economic-minerals-coal-and-bulk-materials.md)). Evaporites (halite, gypsum, anhydrite) precipitate when sea or lake water evaporates in arid climates.

## Diagenesis, lithification, porosity and permeability (OCR 2.1.3(e); Eduqas F2.1g)

### What you need to know

- Diagenesis: all physical and chemical changes after deposition at low temperature and pressure; lithification turns sediment into rock.
- Mechanical compaction; chemical compaction by pressure solution and recrystallisation; growth of cements (silica, calcite, haematite, clay minerals).
- Effects on siliciclastic and carbonate grains, organic material and mud; how these change porosity and permeability.
- Texture (grain angularity, sphericity, size, sorting) controls porosity and permeability, reflecting source rock, climate and processes.

### Explained

1. **Mechanical compaction**: the weight of overlying sediment squeezes grains closer and drives out water. Muds lose most volume (from about 60 to 80% water to about 10 to 20%), turning into mudstone and shale with aligned clay flakes.
2. **Pressure solution (chemical compaction)**: at grain contacts the pressure is highest, so minerals dissolve there and reprecipitate in pore spaces. Grains interlock (sutured contacts); stylolites form in limestones.
3. **Cementation**: minerals precipitate from pore water and bind grains: silica (quartz overgrowths), calcite, haematite (red cement in desert sandstones), clay minerals.
4. **Recrystallisation**: unstable minerals change to stable ones (aragonite shells to calcite).
5. Organic material: buried plant material and plankton change to peat, lignite and coal, or to kerogen and then oil and gas, with rising temperature.

**Porosity** = volume of pore space ÷ total volume × 100. **Permeability** is the ability to let fluids flow through, needing connected pores. Well-sorted, rounded grains have high porosity (packing leaves large pores); poorly sorted sediment has small grains filling spaces between large ones, so porosity is low. Compaction and cementation both reduce porosity and permeability. Clay has high porosity but very low permeability because its pores are tiny. Detail and Darcy's law are in [14](14-fluids-in-rocks-and-hydrogeology.md).

### Common mistakes

- Saying clay is eroded at the lowest velocities. It needs higher velocities to erode than sand, but settles only at very low velocities.
- Mixing up roundness (corner sharpness) and sphericity (how close to a sphere).
- Saying mature sediment is old. Maturity refers to how much it has been weathered and transported.

2. Clay particles cohesive / electrostatically bonded (1); smooth bed, particles lie within the boundary layer (1); sand grains not cohesive so lifted at lower velocity (1).
3. φ = −log₂(0.125) = 3 φ (1).
4. Arkose (1); feldspar unweathered so short transport / rapid erosion (1); granite source (feldspar and quartz) (1); arid climate, little chemical weathering, red / pink (1).
5. Both grain-supported (1); packstone has mud between grains, grainstone has none / cement (1); grainstone: higher energy winnowing away mud (1).
6. Compaction (1); pressure solution at grain contacts (1); cementation by silica / calcite / haematite (1).

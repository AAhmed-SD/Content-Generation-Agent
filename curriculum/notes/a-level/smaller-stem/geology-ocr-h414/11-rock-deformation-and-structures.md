# Rock deformation and geological structures

Tectonic forces deform rocks into folds and faults. This file covers how rocks respond to stress, how to describe and measure structures, and what structures form at each type of plate boundary. Map interpretation and thickness calculations are in [Eduqas 02 Geological maps](../geology-eduqas-a480qs/02-geological-maps.md); competence and metamorphic fabrics in [17](17-metamorphic-petrology.md).

## Stress, strain and rock behaviour (OCR 3.3.1(b); Eduqas F2.3a, G2.1a)

### What you need to know

- Stress is force per unit area; strain is the change in shape or size. Tension, compression and shear.
- Hooke's law and stress-strain curves: elastic and brittle behaviour, ductile (plastic) behaviour, elastic limit, permanent strain, fracture point.
- Temperature, confining pressure, strain rate (and time) control whether rocks behave brittlely or plastically.
- Brittle structures: joints, slickensides, fault breccia.
- Competent rocks (strong, tend to fracture) and incompetent rocks (weak, tend to flow).

### Explained

**Stress-strain curve**:

1. Elastic region: strain is proportional to stress (Hooke's law); removing the stress lets the rock recover.
2. Elastic limit (yield point): beyond it, deformation is permanent.
3. Ductile (plastic) region: permanent strain without breaking (folding).
4. Fracture point: the rock breaks.

A brittle rock reaches the fracture point soon after the elastic limit (little plastic strain). A ductile rock shows a long plastic region.

| Condition | Favours brittle (faults, joints) | Favours ductile (folds) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | Low (shallow) | High (deep) |
| Confining pressure | Low (shallow) | High (deep) |
| Strain rate | Fast | Slow (over long times) |
| Rock type | Competent: sandstone, limestone, granite | Incompetent: shale, salt, mudstone |

**Brittle features**: joints (fractures with no movement, often from cooling, unloading or folding); slickensides (polished, grooved fault surfaces showing the direction of slip); fault breccia (angular fragments broken in the fault zone); fault gouge (fine clay-rich crushed rock).

### Practical

**OCR PAG9**: model deformation using layers of plasticine, sand and flour in a squeeze box, or test the strength of different rocks or models; relate behaviour to confining pressure and rate.

## Describing and measuring structures (OCR 3.3.1(a); Eduqas F2.3b to F2.3f, G2.1b)

### What you need to know

- Bedding: strike, dip, apparent dip; true and apparent thickness.
- Faults: fault plane, throw, dip, dip-slip, strike-slip, hanging wall, footwall, upthrow, downthrow; normal, reverse, thrust; dextral and sinistral; displacement (net slip).
- Folds: limbs, hinge, crest, trough, axial plane, axial plane trace, plunge, symmetrical, asymmetrical, antiform, synform, anticline, syncline. Eduqas A level: amplitude, wavelength, interlimb angle (open, tight, isoclinal), axial plane attitude (upright, inclined, overturned, recumbent), plunging folds; axial planar cleavage; plot limb data as poles on an equal-area stereonet.
- Unconformities.
- Recognise structures in photographs, maps, cross-sections and the field; field sketches; construct cross-sections; use a compass-clinometer; calculate crustal shortening.

### Explained

**Strike and dip**: strike is the compass direction of a horizontal line on a bedding plane. Dip is the angle of maximum slope (measured perpendicular to the strike) and its direction. Written as 040/25 SE (strike, dip, dip direction) or as dip direction/dip (130/25). Apparent dip, seen in a section not perpendicular to strike, is always less than true dip: tan(apparent dip) = tan(true dip) × sin(angle between section and strike).

**Folds**:

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Hinge | Line of greatest curvature |
| Limbs | The sides of the fold |
| Axial plane | Plane through the hinges of successive layers; its trace on the ground is the axial plane trace |
| Anticline / syncline | Oldest rocks in the core / youngest in the core |
| Antiform / synform | Up-arched / down-arched, used when the age order is unknown |
| Symmetrical / asymmetrical | Limbs of equal / unequal length and dip |
| Plunge | Angle of the hinge line below horizontal |
| Interlimb angle | Open (wide), tight (small), isoclinal (limbs parallel) |
| Axial plane attitude | Upright (vertical), inclined, overturned (one limb past vertical), recumbent (axial plane nearly horizontal) |
| Amplitude, wavelength | Height from the median line to the crest; distance between successive crests |

**Stereonet polar plots** (Eduqas): plot the pole (perpendicular) to each limb on an equal-area net. Poles from two limbs fall on a great circle whose own pole is the fold axis (plunge direction and amount). Only plotting poles is needed.

**Faults**:

| Type | Stress | Movement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | Tension (σmax vertical) | Hanging wall moves down relative to footwall; extension; dip usually about 60° |
| Reverse | Compression (σmin vertical) | Hanging wall moves up; shortening; dip over 45° |
| Thrust | Compression | Low-angle reverse fault (under 45°); older rocks pushed over younger |
| Strike-slip (wrench) | Shear (σint vertical) | Horizontal movement along strike; dextral (right-lateral: the far side moves right) or sinistral |

The hanging wall is the block above an inclined fault plane; the footwall is below. Throw is the vertical displacement; heave the horizontal. Net slip is the total displacement on the fault plane. On the upthrow side, older rocks are exposed at the surface after erosion.

**Principal stresses**: σmax (σ1), σint (σ2), σmin (σ3). Faults form at about 30° to σmax, containing σint. So the orientation of σmax, σint and σmin decides fault type.

**Unconformities**: see [06](06-geological-time-and-dating.md).

**Crustal shortening**: measure the length of a folded layer along its curves (original length L₀) and the present horizontal distance (L). Shortening = (L₀ − L) ÷ L₀ × 100%.

### Worked example

A folded bed measured along its curves is 14.0 km long; the folds now span 9.8 km horizontally. Shortening = (14.0 − 9.8) ÷ 14.0 × 100 = 30%.

**Compass-clinometer**: place the edge on the bedding to read strike with the compass held level; rotate 90° and read dip with the clinometer down the steepest slope. Take several readings (valid sampling) and average; record in a field notebook with a grid reference.

### Practical

**OCR PAG6**: measure dip and strike in the field or on models; field sketch structures with scale, orientation and labels; construct cross-sections from maps.

## Slaty cleavage (OCR 3.3.1(c); Eduqas G2.1b)

Compression causes platy minerals to align perpendicular to σmax, forming slaty cleavage parallel to the axial plane of folds formed at the same time (axial planar cleavage). In the field, where cleavage is steeper than bedding, the beds are the right way up on that limb; the cleavage-bedding intersection is parallel to the fold hinge, giving plunge.

## Structural reactivation and inversion (Eduqas G2.1d)

Older faults are planes of weakness that can move again under later stress (reactivation). Folds may be refolded, giving interference patterns. **Inversion**: normal faults formed during extension (bounding a basin) are later reactivated as reverse faults during compression, pushing the basin fill up (or thrusts reactivated as normal faults in extension). Evidence: a fault with normal displacement at depth but reverse at shallow levels; basin sediments thicker on one side of a fault but now uplifted.

## Earthquakes and tectonic environments (OCR 3.3.2)

### What you need to know

- Elastic rebound theory.
- Transform boundaries: shear-dominated, stress transfer.
- Convergent boundaries: compression and shear; fold mountains, overfolds, isoclinal folds, nappes, thrusts.
- Divergent boundaries: tension; graben (rift), horst; spreading rate and topography; oceanic core complexes.

### Explained

**Elastic rebound**: plates move steadily but faults are locked by friction. Rocks either side bend elastically, storing strain energy. When stress exceeds friction, the fault slips suddenly and the rocks spring back to an unstrained shape, releasing energy as seismic waves. Then strain builds again (the earthquake cycle). Movement on one segment transfers stress to neighbouring segments (**stress transfer**), so earthquakes can migrate along a fault (as along the North Anatolian Fault).

**Transform boundaries**: strike-slip faults with shear; bends in the fault produce local compression (pop-up ridges) or extension (pull-apart basins).

**Convergent boundaries**: compression shortens and thickens the crust: tight to isoclinal folds, overturned folds (overfolds), recumbent folds that may be sheared out into **nappes** (large sheets moved many kilometres), and **thrust faults** stacking slices. Gravity can cause sliding of thrust sheets off high mountains.

**Divergent boundaries**: tension produces normal faults. A **graben** is a down-dropped block between two inward-dipping normal faults (rift valley: East Africa); a **horst** is an up-standing block. Slow-spreading ridges (Mid-Atlantic) have a deep axial rift valley and rough topography; fast-spreading ridges (East Pacific Rise) have smooth, gentle topography and an axial high. At slow ridges, large low-angle normal faults (detachments) can expose lower crust and mantle at the sea floor, forming **oceanic core complexes**.

### Practical

**OCR PAG7**: interpret deformation features in hand specimens and photographs from orogenic belts.

### Common mistakes

- Using anticline when the age of the rocks is unknown (use antiform).
- Measuring dip along the strike.
- Saying a thrust is a normal fault because it is low-angle.

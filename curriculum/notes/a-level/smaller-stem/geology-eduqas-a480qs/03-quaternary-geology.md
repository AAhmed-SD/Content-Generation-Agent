# Option T3: Quaternary geology

The Quaternary (the last 2.6 million years) is the most recent period, marked by repeated glacial and interglacial stages. Its deposits are young and fragmentary on land, but the ocean and ice records are nearly complete. Long-term climate change, isotopes and the Anthropocene are in [OCR 22](../geology-ocr-h414/22-the-changing-earth-climate-and-sea-level.md).

## Key Idea 1: causes of climate change

### What you need to know

- T3.1a Milankovitch cycles as a contributory cause of Quaternary climate fluctuations.
- T3.1b Distribution of continents and mountain belts affects ocean and atmosphere circulation and climate; analyse present-day circulation.
- T3.1c The switch from greenhouse to icehouse is moderated by the efficiency of heat transfer from equator to poles; possible roles of the opening of the Drake Passage and the rise of the Himalaya.

### Explained

**Milankovitch cycles** are regular changes in the Earth's orbit and axis that change the distribution of sunlight by latitude and season:

| Cycle | What changes | Period |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eccentricity | Orbit shape between more circular and more elliptical | about 100 000 years (and 400 000) |
| Obliquity | Tilt of the axis, between about 22.1° and 24.5° | about 41 000 years |
| Precession | Wobble of the axis, changing which season occurs at the closest point to the Sun | about 19 000 to 23 000 years |

Ice sheets grow when northern summers are cool (low summer sunlight at 65° N), so snow survives the summer. The cycles alone cause small changes in total sunlight, so feedbacks are needed to amplify them: ice-albedo feedback, changes in CO₂ and CH₄ (lower in glacials), and ocean circulation. Ocean records show glacial cycles of about 41 000 years before about 1 million years ago, and about 100 000 years since.

**Continents and circulation**: ocean currents and winds carry heat from the tropics to the poles. Continents block or channel currents; gateways (narrow seaways) opening or closing change heat transport. Mountains deflect winds and increase weathering.

**Greenhouse to icehouse** (the Cenozoic cooling):

- The opening of the **Drake Passage** between South America and Antarctica (about 34 to 30 million years ago) let the Antarctic Circumpolar Current flow all the way round Antarctica, isolating it from warm currents, so it cooled and ice sheets formed.
- The uplift of the **Himalaya and Tibetan Plateau** increased chemical weathering of silicate rocks, removing CO₂ from the atmosphere, and changed monsoon circulation.
- The closing of the Isthmus of Panama (about 3 million years ago) strengthened the Gulf Stream, bringing moisture north, which may have helped Northern Hemisphere ice sheets grow.

## Key Idea 2: the Quaternary record in Britain

### What you need to know

- T3.2a Glacial, periglacial, fluvioglacial and interglacial deposits and landforms give an incomplete record of climate and environments; ice sheet extent and movement can be deduced.
- T3.2b Evidence for glacial and interglacial stages and shorter cycles superimposed on them.
- T3.2c Ice sheets and sea level: low in glacials, high in interglacials; isostatic response to loading and unloading; raised beaches, drowned valleys; simple rebound calculations.
- T3.2d The fragmentary land record contrasts with the near-complete ocean record: oxygen isotopes in ocean sediments.
- T3.2e Ice core evidence for atmospheric change.

### Explained

**Deposits and landforms**:

| Type | Examples | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glacial | Till (boulder clay: unsorted, angular, striated clasts in clay), moraines, drumlins, erratics, striations on bedrock | Ice cover; erratics and striations show ice-flow direction; till fabric (long-axis orientation) shows flow; moraines mark ice margins |
| Fluvioglacial | Outwash sands and gravels, eskers, kames, varved clays in glacial lakes | Meltwater from ice |
| Periglacial | Ice wedge casts, patterned ground, head (solifluction deposits), frost-shattered rock | Cold, permafrost conditions near (not under) ice |
| Interglacial | Peat, lake and river deposits with warm-climate pollen and fossils | Climate as warm as or warmer than today |

Each glaciation erodes or reworks earlier deposits, so the land record is incomplete. Erratics traced to their source rocks show ice-flow paths; moraines and till limits show ice sheet extent (for example the last ice sheet reached about the line of the Severn to the Wash in eastern England, while an earlier one reached the Thames valley).

**Shorter cycles**: within glacial stages there are stadials (cold) and interstadials (warmer); ice cores and ocean records show rapid swings within decades (for example the Younger Dryas cold snap about 12 900 to 11 700 years ago, recorded in Britain by renewed glaciers in the Scottish Highlands and cold-climate beetles and pollen).

**Sea level and isostasy**:

- Glacial: water locked in ice sheets, so global sea level about 120 m lower; land bridges (Britain joined to Europe across "Doggerland").
- Interglacial: ice melts, sea level rises; valleys drowned (rias, estuaries, submerged forests).
- Isostasy: ice sheets depress the lithosphere; after melting, the land rebounds slowly. Scotland, under the thickest ice, is still rising (raised beaches above present sea level), while southern England is sinking slightly (the peripheral bulge collapsing), worsening relative sea-level rise there.

### Worked example

Ice 1500 m thick (density 900 kg m⁻³) depresses mantle of density 3300 kg m⁻³. Maximum depression = 1500 × 900 ÷ 3300 ≈ 410 m. If only 25% of this rebound remains to happen, the land could still rise about 100 m.

A raised beach is 12 m above present sea level and formed 8000 years ago when global sea level was about 15 m lower than today. Total land uplift = 12 + 15 = 27 m, an average of 3.4 mm per year.

**Ocean oxygen isotopes**: foraminifera in deep-sea cores record δ¹⁸O continuously ([OCR 22](../geology-ocr-h414/22-the-changing-earth-climate-and-sea-level.md)). High δ¹⁸O = more ice, colder. The record is divided into marine isotope stages (odd numbers warm, even numbers cold; the present interglacial is stage 1, the last glacial maximum in stage 2). This gives the standard global framework that the land record is matched to.

**Ice cores** (Antarctica, Greenland): air bubbles give direct samples of past CO₂ and CH₄; isotopes in the ice give temperature; dust and volcanic ash layers record wind strength and eruptions; annual layers can be counted near the top. Antarctic cores show CO₂ about 180 ppm in glacials and 280 ppm in interglacials over 800 000 years, rising and falling with temperature.

## Key Idea 3: Quaternary fossils and dating

### What you need to know

- T3.3a Fossils as evidence for climate fluctuations in Britain: pollen diagrams; vertebrates and invertebrates (for example woolly mammoths, beetles).
- T3.3b Environmental change and climate instability over the past six million years may have shaped human evolution; evaluate fossil evidence for hominin evolution to *Homo sapiens* against environmental and climatic change.
- T3.3c Dating methods: radiocarbon (¹⁴C) dating of organic material; incremental dating (varves, tree rings); isochronous marker beds (volcanic ash layers); applications and limitations.

### Explained

**Pollen diagrams**: cores from lakes and peat bogs are sampled at intervals; pollen grains are identified and counted; percentages of each plant type are plotted against depth. Changes show vegetation succession and climate: for example after the last glaciation, tundra herbs, then birch and pine, then warmth-loving oak, elm and hazel. Limitations: some plants produce or spread more pollen; local versus regional sources; pollen can be reworked.

**Animals**: beetles respond quickly to temperature and many species live today in known climate ranges, so beetle assemblages give summer temperature estimates. Mammals: woolly mammoth, woolly rhinoceros and reindeer indicate cold steppe; hippopotamus and elephants found in British interglacial deposits (for example under central London) indicate warmer conditions than today.

**Hominin evolution**: over the last 6 million years, Africa became drier and more variable, with forests replaced by more open savanna and rapid swings between wet and dry phases. Hominin fossils show bipedalism early (open habitats), later larger brains, tool use and long-distance walking in *Homo erectus*, and the spread of *Homo sapiens* out of Africa. The "variability selection" hypothesis argues that unstable climates favoured adaptable, generalist hominins. Evaluate: fossil record sparse and dating uncertain; correlation in time does not prove cause.

**Dating methods**:

| Method | Principle | Range | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Radiocarbon (¹⁴C) | Living things take in ¹⁴C; after death it decays with a half-life of about 5730 years | About 50 000 years | Only organic material; contamination with modern carbon; past ¹⁴C levels varied so calibration (with tree rings) is needed |
| Varves | Annual pairs of lake layers: coarse, pale summer meltwater silt and fine, dark winter clay; count them | Thousands of years | Only in glacial lakes; missing or doubled layers |
| Tree rings (dendrochronology) | Count annual rings; match patterns between overlapping timbers | About 12 000 years | Needs suitable wood; regional patterns |
| Isochronous marker beds (tephra) | A volcanic ash layer is deposited over a wide area at one moment; identified by chemistry and dated | Links sequences across regions | Needs ash fall; ash can be reworked |

### Worked example

A peat sample has 25% of its original ¹⁴C left. That is two half-lives: 2 × 5730 = 11 460 years before present (before calibration).

### Common mistakes

- Saying Milankovitch cycles alone cause ice ages without feedbacks.
- Saying radiocarbon can date rocks.
- Confusing global (eustatic) and local (isostatic) sea-level change.

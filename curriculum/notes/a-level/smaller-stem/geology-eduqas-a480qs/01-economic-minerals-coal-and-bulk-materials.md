# Earth materials and natural resources

Eduqas G4.1 covers how geological processes concentrate useful materials. Magmatic, hydrothermal and placer ores, exploration and mining are in the shared file [OCR 18 Mining geology](../geology-ocr-h414/18-mining-geology.md); oil and gas in [OCR 26](../geology-ocr-h414/26-oil-and-gas-basins.md). This file adds what Eduqas also names: residual and precipitated ore deposits, china clay, sand and gravel, and coal.

## Metalliferous ores (G4.1a)

### What you need to know

- Igneous associations: magmatic segregation, hydrothermal activity (see [OCR 15](../geology-ocr-h414/15-igneous-petrology.md) and [OCR 18](../geology-ocr-h414/18-mining-geology.md)).
- Sedimentary associations: placer deposits, residual deposits, precipitated deposits.
- Map interpretation of ore bodies (geometry, field relations), sections through ore bodies; calculations of volume and tonnage; units, ratios and percentages.

### Explained

**Residual deposits** form by intense chemical weathering in hot, wet tropical climates. Soluble elements (Na, K, Ca, Mg and silica) are leached away, leaving insoluble ones concentrated in the soil. **Bauxite** (aluminium ore, hydrated aluminium oxides) forms by weathering of aluminium-rich rocks such as granite, basalt or clays where drainage is good. **Laterites** rich in iron or nickel form similarly from mafic and ultramafic rocks. They are flat-lying blankets near the surface, mined by open pit.

**Precipitated (chemical sedimentary) deposits** form when dissolved metals precipitate from water: banded iron formations (see [OCR 13](../geology-ocr-h414/13-turbidites-deltas-and-banded-iron-formations.md)), sedimentary ironstones (Jurassic ooidal ironstones of England), manganese nodules on the deep sea floor, and evaporites (salt, potash, gypsum) from evaporating water.

**Placers**: dense, resistant minerals (gold, cassiterite, diamonds) concentrated by flowing water ([OCR 18](../geology-ocr-h414/18-mining-geology.md)).

**Map relations**: veins appear as narrow lines following faults, often near granite; placer deposits follow present or ancient river valleys and beaches; residual deposits cap plateaus; stratiform deposits (ironstones, evaporites) follow bedding and show outcrop patterns like any bed.

### Worked example

A flat-lying bauxite layer averages 4 m thick over an area of 1.5 km by 0.8 km, with density 2.4 t m⁻³ and 45% Al₂O₃. Volume = 1500 × 800 × 4 = 4.8 × 10⁶ m³. Mass = 1.15 × 10⁷ t. Mass of Al₂O₃ = 5.2 × 10⁶ t.

## China clay (G4.1b)

### What you need to know

Formation of china clay (kaolinite), a non-metallic mineral of economic importance.

### Explained

China clay is kaolinite, formed by the alteration of feldspar in granite. In Cornwall and Devon, the granites were altered in two ways:

- **Hydrothermal alteration (kaolinisation)**: hot, acidic fluids circulating through the cooling granite and along fractures broke down feldspar to kaolinite.
- **Deep weathering**: later tropical weathering added to the alteration near the surface.

The result is soft, decomposed granite of kaolinite, quartz and mica. It is extracted by high-pressure water jets that wash the clay out; the quartz sand and mica are separated, producing huge white waste tips. Kaolinite is used in paper making (filler and coating), ceramics and porcelain, paint and medicines. Altered zones are funnel-shaped, extending to depth along fractures.

## Sand and gravel (G4.1c)

### What you need to know

Formation of sedimentary deposits of sand and gravel used as bulk aggregate.

### Explained

Aggregates are crushed rock or natural sand and gravel used in concrete, roads and construction fill. Britain uses large tonnages each year. Natural sand and gravel deposits form:

- in river terraces and floodplains (well sorted, rounded, hard quartz and flint pebbles)
- as glacial outwash (fluvioglacial sands and gravels) from melting ice sheets in the Quaternary
- offshore on the sea bed (marine dredged aggregate from old river and beach deposits drowned by sea-level rise).

Good aggregate is hard, durable, free of clay, chemically inert (no reactive silica or sulfides) and well graded. Because it is heavy and cheap, transport costs limit how far it can be moved, so pits are close to cities. Extraction issues: loss of farmland, dust, noise, lowering water tables; worked-out pits are often restored as lakes and nature reserves.

## Coal (G4.1d)

### What you need to know

- Coal-forming environments; peat, lignite, bituminous coal and anthracite; coal rank.
- Identify coal types; simple reserve calculations (tonnage of coal in an area).
- Simple analysis of maturity depth (temperature) graphs showing oil and gas windows (see [OCR 26](../geology-ocr-h414/26-oil-and-gas-basins.md)).

### Explained

**Environment**: coal forms from plant material that accumulates faster than it decays, in waterlogged, anoxic swamps on delta tops and coastal plains, in warm, wet climates. Britain's Carboniferous Coal Measures formed in equatorial delta swamps ([OCR 13](../geology-ocr-h414/13-turbidites-deltas-and-banded-iron-formations.md) for cyclothems). Seat earths (fossil soils with roots) underlie coal seams.

**Coalification**: burial increases temperature and pressure, driving off water and volatiles (CO₂, CH₄) and increasing carbon content. This sequence is called increasing **rank**:

| Rank | Carbon content (approx.) | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peat | about 55 to 60% | Brown, fibrous, plant remains visible, high water |
| Lignite (brown coal) | about 60 to 70% | Brown-black, crumbly, some plant structure |
| Bituminous coal | about 75 to 90% | Black, banded (bright and dull layers), dirties fingers, breaks into blocks |
| Anthracite | over 90% | Black, very shiny, hard, conchoidal fracture, clean to handle, burns with little smoke |

Higher rank means higher carbon and energy content and less moisture and volatiles. Very roughly, about 10 m of peat compacts to 1 m of bituminous coal. Coal gives off methane (firedamp, a mining hazard) during coalification; coal seams are also the source of much North Sea gas.

### Worked example

A seam 1.8 m thick underlies an area of 2.5 km². Coal density 1.35 t m⁻³. Volume = 2.5 × 10⁶ × 1.8 = 4.5 × 10⁶ m³. Tonnage = 6.1 × 10⁶ t. If 60% can be extracted (pillars and losses), the reserve is about 3.6 × 10⁶ t.

### Common mistakes

- Saying china clay forms by sedimentary deposition. It forms by alteration of granite in place.
- Calling peat a coal.
- Forgetting extraction losses when estimating reserves.

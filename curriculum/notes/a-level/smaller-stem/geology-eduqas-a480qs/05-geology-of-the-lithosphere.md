# Option T5: Geology of the lithosphere

This option studies the lithosphere as the Earth's cooling outer shell: how it is defined, how oceanic lithosphere is made and destroyed, and how continental lithosphere grows and deforms. It builds on [OCR 07](../geology-ocr-h414/07-physical-structure-of-the-earth.md), [OCR 09](../geology-ocr-h414/09-plate-tectonics.md) and [OCR 16](../geology-ocr-h414/16-mid-ocean-ridges.md).

## Key Idea 1: heat loss and the lithosphere

### What you need to know

- T5.1a The Earth loses heat through its surface, forming a cold, rigid lithosphere: surface heat flow, temperature with depth, rock strength related to temperature. Compare continental and oceanic geotherms with the mantle solidus.
- T5.1b The base of the lithosphere is defined as the 1300 °C isotherm; thickness differs between continents and oceans.
- T5.1c Seismology shows the lithosphere-asthenosphere distinction: the low velocity zone; ray path modelling (refraction through the LVZ); P and S velocity-depth curves.
- T5.1d The crust: seismic estimates of thickness; the Moho; seismic refraction for crustal layers and reflection for internal structure.
- T5.1e Magma in different settings: ocean ridges (MORB), large igneous provinces (LIPs), island arcs, cordilleran mountain belts; heat flow across an ocean basin; evidence for mantle plumes.

### Explained

**Thermal definition**: rock strength depends strongly on temperature. Below about 1300 °C mantle rock is rigid over geological time; above it, mantle flows. So the base of the lithosphere is taken as the 1300 °C isotherm. Heat passes through the lithosphere mainly by conduction (a thermal boundary layer), so the geotherm is steep there; beneath, convection keeps temperatures close to an adiabat.

- Oceanic lithosphere: very thin at ridges (a few km), thickening as it cools with age, to about 100 km after 80 million years.
- Continental lithosphere: thick (about 150 to 250 km under old cratons), with a lower geotherm.

Where the geotherm comes closest to (or crosses) the mantle solidus, small amounts of melt form: this is the low velocity zone of the asthenosphere.

**Heat flow across an ocean basin**: highest at the ridge, decreasing with distance as the lithosphere ages and cools; scattered high values near ridges reflect hydrothermal circulation.

**Low velocity zone (LVZ)**: P and S speeds fall slightly at about 100 to 200 km depth beneath oceans. Waves entering a slower layer refract towards the normal and are bent downwards, creating a shadow zone at certain distances at the surface. Ray path modelling and travel-time curves identify the LVZ, interpreted as small amounts of melt or near-melting rock: the top of the asthenosphere.

**Crust**: seismic refraction (waves travelling along layer boundaries and returning to surface) measures layer velocities and thicknesses: the Moho is where P velocity jumps to about 8 km s⁻¹. Seismic reflection images internal layering, faults and the Moho as reflectors.

**Magma settings**:

| Setting | Magma | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ocean ridges | MORB (tholeiitic basalt) from decompression melting | Mid-Atlantic Ridge |
| Large igneous provinces | Huge volumes of flood basalt in a short time, probably from plume heads | Deccan, Siberian Traps |
| Island arcs | Basalt and andesite from flux melting above subduction | Japan, Aleutians |
| Cordilleran belts | Andesite and granite batholiths where ocean subducts beneath continent | Andes |

**Evidence for plumes**: hotspot tracks with age progression; LIPs; high heat flow and topographic swells; seismic tomography; distinctive geochemistry. Some scientists argue many hotspots are shallow features, so evaluate.

## Key Idea 2: oceanic lithosphere

### What you need to know

- T5.2a Ocean crust layers 1, 2 and 3 (sediments; pillow lavas and sheeted dykes; gabbro) from ophiolites and ocean drilling.
- T5.2b Ophiolites and ocean drilling as evidence for the origin of oceanic crust and upper mantle.
- T5.2c Ocean basin evolution from rift to narrow sea to mature ocean: the Wilson cycle.
- T5.2d Rates and directions of spreading from dating oceanic crust and magnetic anomalies; radiometric dating and drilling to date anomalies; hotspots as an absolute reference frame.

### Explained

Ocean crust layers and ophiolites are covered in [OCR 16](../geology-ocr-h414/16-mid-ocean-ridges.md); the Wilson cycle in [OCR 22](../geology-ocr-h414/22-the-changing-earth-climate-and-sea-level.md).

**Rates and directions**: magnetic anomaly profiles across a ridge are matched to the dated reversal timescale (calibrated by radiometric dating of lavas and by fossils in drilled sediments directly above the basalt). Distance ÷ age gives the half-rate; the trend of transform faults gives the direction. Plate motions relative to each other are relative; hotspots, assumed fixed in the deep mantle, give an absolute frame: the age progression along a hotspot chain gives the plate's speed and direction over the hotspot.

### Worked example

Hawaiian chain: an island 2400 km from the active hotspot has lavas dated 43 Ma (just before the bend). Plate speed = 2400 km ÷ 43 Ma = 56 km Ma⁻¹ = 5.6 cm yr⁻¹, moving away from the hotspot along the chain.

## Key Idea 3: continental lithosphere

### What you need to know

- T5.3a Supercontinents assemble and disperse in cycles (Rodinia, Pangaea); limited evidence beyond 200 Ma.
- T5.3b Low-density continental lithosphere resists subduction, so the oldest rocks are continental; age distribution on maps.
- T5.3c Orogenic belts are sites of lithospheric thickening: continent-continent collision, continent-arc collision, cordilleran belts; ophiolites and accretionary prisms; partial melting and granite; magmatism and delamination; isostatic uplift and gravitational collapse; isostasy.
- T5.3d Plate boundary forces and gravitational spreading cause brittle and ductile deformation at all scales; field interpretation of folds and faults in terms of stress.
- T5.3e Regional structures: fold and thrust belts; nappes with recumbent folds and low-angle thrusts in compressive settings.
- T5.3f Major sedimentary basins controlled by lithospheric extension (extensional basins) and loading (foreland basins).

### Explained

**Supercontinent cycle**: Rodinia (assembled about 1.1 billion years ago, broke up about 750 Ma), Pannotia (briefly about 600 Ma), Pangaea (about 300 to 180 Ma). Evidence for older reconstructions is weaker because all ocean floor older than about 200 Ma has been subducted, so magnetic stripes and ocean ages cannot be used; geologists rely on palaeomagnetism, matching orogenic belts and rock ages across continents.

**Why continents survive**: continental crust (about 2.7 g cm⁻³) and the thick, chemically depleted lithosphere beneath cratons are buoyant, so they resist subduction. Continental crust is added by arc magmatism and collisions and rarely destroyed; hence rocks up to 4 billion years old survive in cratons (shields), surrounded by progressively younger belts (a map pattern of concentric ages in North America).

**Orogenic belts**:

- Continent-continent collision (Himalaya): crust thickened to about 70 km by folding and thrusting; deep crust heats and melts, forming granites.
- Continent-arc collision: an island arc accreted to a continent margin.
- Cordilleran belts (Andes): subduction beneath a continent: thick crust, andesite volcanoes, granite batholiths.
- Accretionary prisms (sediment scraped off subducting plates) and ophiolites (slices of oceanic lithosphere) are added to the edges.
- Delamination: the dense lower lithosphere root may detach and sink, letting hot asthenosphere rise, causing uplift and magmatism.
- Isostasy: thick crustal roots support high mountains; erosion causes isostatic uplift, bringing deep metamorphic rocks to the surface; very high, thick mountain belts may collapse under their own weight by extension (gravitational collapse).

**Deformation**: plate boundary forces (push and pull) and gravitational spreading (high areas spreading outward) create stresses. Shallow, cold rocks deform brittlely (faults, joints); deep, hot rocks ductilely (folds, foliation, shear zones). Field folds and faults are interpreted in terms of principal stress directions ([OCR 11](../geology-ocr-h414/11-rock-deformation-and-structures.md)).

**Regional structures**:

- Fold and thrust belts: stacked thrust sheets with associated folds at the front of mountain belts (the Canadian Rockies, the Moine Thrust Belt of north-west Scotland).
- Nappes: large recumbent folds and thrust sheets moved tens of kilometres along low-angle thrusts (the Alps).

**Sedimentary basins**:

- Extensional (rift) basins: stretching thins the lithosphere; normal faults create grabens and tilted blocks; initial fault-controlled subsidence, then slower thermal subsidence as the lithosphere cools (the North Sea; McKenzie-type stretching). Deep basins collect thick sediment.
- Foreland basins: the weight of thrust sheets in a mountain belt bends the lithosphere down in front of it, forming a basin filled by sediment eroded from the mountains (the Ganges plain, the Molasse basin north of the Alps).

### Common mistakes

- Defining the lithosphere by composition rather than temperature and strength.
- Saying the oldest rocks are on the ocean floor.
- Confusing foreland and rift basins.

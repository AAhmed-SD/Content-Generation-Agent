# Geological maps

A geological map shows where each rock unit crops out at the surface, drawn over topographic contours. Because outcrops are the intersection of 3D structures with the ground, their patterns reveal dip, folds, faults and unconformities. Eduqas Component 1 includes a simplified map and cross-section; Component 3 Section B uses a Geological Survey map extract. Structures are described in [OCR 11](../geology-ocr-h414/11-rock-deformation-and-structures.md); relative dating in [OCR 06](../geology-ocr-h414/06-geological-time-and-dating.md).

## Outcrop patterns and structures (F2.3g; T2.1a)

### What you need to know

- Outcrop patterns of dipping strata and faults relative to topography: V-shaped outcrops in valleys show dip direction; boundaries parallel to contours show near-horizontal beds; straight boundaries crossing relief show steep (vertical) dip.
- Identify fold types from outcrop patterns; identify faults and measure displacements from offsets; identify unconformities.
- Use block diagrams, boreholes, cross-sections, photographs and GIS (including Google Earth) to understand the 3D nature of maps.
- Construct cross-sections from simplified maps; order events.

### Explained

**Horizontal beds**: boundaries follow (are parallel to) topographic contours. Youngest rocks on hilltops.

**Vertical beds**: boundaries are straight lines unaffected by topography.

**Dipping beds: the V-rule**: where a boundary crosses a valley, it forms a V.

- If the bed dips downstream (in the same direction as the valley slopes, but more steeply than the valley floor), the V points downstream.
- If the bed dips upstream, the V points upstream (into the hill).
- (A bed dipping downstream at an angle less than the valley gradient makes a V pointing upstream; this case is less common.)

**Structure contours** (strike lines): lines joining points of equal height on a geological boundary. Draw them by joining two points where the boundary crosses the same topographic contour. They are parallel to strike; spacing gives dip: tan(dip) = vertical interval ÷ horizontal spacing (at map scale). Dip direction is towards the lower structure contour.

**Folds on maps**: outcrops repeat symmetrically about the fold axis. Anticline: oldest rocks in the centre, beds dip away from the axis. Syncline: youngest in the centre, beds dip inwards. Plunging folds give V- or U-shaped "closures": an anticline closes (noses) in the direction of plunge; a syncline opens in the direction of plunge. Wider outcrops on one limb mean gentler dip on that limb (asymmetrical fold).

**Faults on maps**:

- Dip faults (striking across the strike of beds): offset outcrops sideways. After erosion, a boundary on the upthrown side crops out further in the direction of dip than on the downthrown side, because the bed has been lifted and must be followed further down-dip to reach the ground surface.
- Strike faults (parallel to strike): repeat or cut out beds.
- Older rocks crop out on the upthrow side after erosion.
- Normal faults may cut out beds; reverse faults may repeat them.
- Throw can be measured from offset structure contours of the same boundary.
- Strike-slip faults offset steep beds and fold axes horizontally.

**Unconformities**: a boundary that cuts across the outcrops of older beds and structures (folds, faults, intrusions) beneath it; younger beds above have a simpler, different pattern (often parallel to contours if near-horizontal).

**Igneous bodies**: dykes are straight bands cutting across beds; sills follow bedding outcrops; plutons are large irregular outcrops with surrounding aureoles; lava flows follow bedding.

**Cross-section method**:

1. Draw the section line on the map and mark where each boundary and fault crosses it.
2. Draw the topographic profile (vertical scale equal to horizontal if possible, to show true dips).
3. Transfer boundary positions to the profile.
4. Use dips (from structure contours or dip symbols) to draw boundaries below ground, using apparent dip if the section is not perpendicular to strike.
5. Add faults and intrusions; label units and key; title, scale and orientation.

**Ordering events**: use superposition, cross-cutting relationships (faults, dykes, unconformities cutting older features) and included fragments.

## Thickness and outcrop width calculations (G2.1e)

### What you need to know

Calculations of true bed thickness, vertical bed thickness, width of outcrop and angle of dip; using sin, cos and tan.

### Explained

- **True thickness (t)**: measured perpendicular to the bedding.
- **Vertical thickness (v)**: measured vertically (as in a vertical borehole): t = v cos θ, where θ is the dip.
- **Outcrop width (w)** on horizontal ground measured perpendicular to strike: t = w sin θ.

So for a given thickness, gentle dips give wide outcrops and steep dips narrow ones; vertical beds have outcrop width equal to true thickness.

On sloping ground, outcrop width also depends on slope: widest where the ground slopes in the same direction as the dip at a similar angle.

### Worked example

A sandstone outcrop is 150 m wide on flat ground (perpendicular to strike). Dip 20°. True thickness = 150 × sin 20° = 51 m. Vertical thickness in a borehole = t ÷ cos 20° = 51 ÷ 0.940 = 54.6 m.

Structure contours for the base of a bed: 300 m and 200 m contours are 250 m apart on the map. tan θ = 100 ÷ 250 = 0.4, so dip = 21.8°.

## Map applications (T2.2a, T2.2b)

### What you need to know

- Maps are an essential database for interpreting and predicting subsurface conditions: conformable and unconformable formations, metamorphic sequences and igneous bodies (and their aureoles), structural features.
- Applications: design of construction projects; identifying hazards (landslides, subsidence) at sites; locating resources (groundwater: water table, springs, aquifers, artesian wells; coal; oil; natural gas; geothermal energy); environmental issues of extraction; suitability for sustainable waste disposal or brownfield remediation.

### Explained

| Application | What to look for on the map |
| --- | --- |
| Construction (roads, tunnels, dams, buildings) | Strong rock near the surface; avoid faults, weak clays, soluble rocks; dip of beds relative to cuttings (beds dipping into a cutting are prone to sliding); aquifers that may flood excavations ([OCR 21](../geology-ocr-h414/21-engineering-geology.md)) |
| Landslides | Clay below permeable rock on steep slopes; springs at the junction; old landslip symbols |
| Subsidence | Limestone, chalk, gypsum or salt (solution); coal measures (old workings, shafts); shrink-swell clays ([OCR 20](../geology-ocr-h414/20-geohazards-in-the-british-isles.md)) |
| Groundwater | Permeable aquifers (sandstone, chalk, limestone) and their recharge outcrops; confined aquifers in synclines (artesian); springs where aquifer overlies aquiclude ([OCR 14](../geology-ocr-h414/14-fluids-in-rocks-and-hydrogeology.md)) |
| Coal | Coal Measures outcrop and their extension at depth beneath younger cover; faults dividing the coalfield |
| Oil and gas | Anticlines and fault blocks with reservoir rocks sealed by impermeable beds ([OCR 26](../geology-ocr-h414/26-oil-and-gas-basins.md)) |
| Geothermal | Granites with high heat flow (Cornwall); deep aquifers in basins |
| Environmental issues of extraction | Aquifers near quarries; rivers downstream of mines; protected sites |
| Waste disposal and brownfield sites | Impermeable clay with no underlying aquifer; away from faults, springs, rivers; old industrial or mining areas for remediation |

### Common mistakes

- Using the V-rule backwards: check whether the V points up or down the valley and remember the dip-downstream rule.
- Using the outcrop width as the true thickness for dipping beds.
- Drawing faults on cross-sections with the wrong dip direction.

# Option T4: Geological evolution of Britain

Britain contains rocks from every major division of geological time. Its history is one of separate crustal blocks (terranes) assembled during three mountain-building events, while plate motion carried the British area from the southern hemisphere to its present northern latitude. Detailed stratigraphy is not required: the spec asks for "snapshots" that show the plate tectonic controls. Basin examples (Welsh Basin, Jurassic) are developed further in [OCR 27](../geology-ocr-h414/27-whole-basin-facies-analysis.md).

## Key Idea 1: terranes, orogenies and igneous provinces

### What you need to know

- T4.1a Rocks of all major time divisions (Precambrian, Early and Late Palaeozoic, Mesozoic, Cenozoic) in Britain and its shelf; maps at different scales including offshore.
- T4.1b Remote sensing and subsurface methods: potential field (gravity, Bouguer anomaly, and magnetic) surveys; boreholes (for example the Mochras borehole); seismic reflection onshore and offshore.
- T4.1c Orogenies: ages, main structures and dominant trends of the Caledonian and Variscan belts; Alpine influences.
- T4.1d Plutonic, volcanic and metamorphic rocks of the orogenic belts used to reconstruct plate tectonic settings.
- T4.1e The Palaeogene Igneous Province of north-west Britain and the early opening of the North Atlantic.

### Explained

**Broad pattern**: oldest rocks in the north-west (Lewisian gneisses about 3 to 1.7 billion years old in the Outer Hebrides and north-west Highlands); younger rocks generally towards the south-east (Mesozoic and Cenozoic in southern and eastern England).

**Subsurface methods**:

- Gravity: Bouguer anomaly lows over granites (low density) and thick sedimentary basins; highs over dense basement. Magnetic anomalies outline buried igneous bodies and basement.
- Boreholes: the Mochras borehole (on the Welsh coast) drilled nearly 2 km through a thick Mesozoic sequence in a fault-bounded basin, much thicker than expected, showing Jurassic basin subsidence and giving a key Jurassic reference section.
- Seismic reflection: maps buried basins, faults and basement offshore (North Sea, Irish Sea) and onshore, much of it from hydrocarbon exploration.

**Three orogenies**:

| Orogeny | Age | Cause | Trend in Britain | Main features |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Caledonian | Ordovician to early Devonian (about 490 to 390 Ma) | Closure of the Iapetus Ocean; collision of Laurentia (Scotland, northern Ireland) with Avalonia (England, Wales) and Baltica | North-east to south-west (Caledonoid) | Regional metamorphism and folding in the Highlands (Dalradian schists); thrusts (Moine Thrust); major faults (Great Glen, Highland Boundary, Southern Uplands Fault); slates of Wales and the Lake District; granites; arc volcanics (Snowdonia, Lake District); accretionary prism (Southern Uplands) |
| Variscan (Hercynian) | Late Carboniferous to early Permian (about 310 to 290 Ma) | Closure of the Rheic Ocean as Gondwana collided with Laurussia (forming Pangaea) | East-west | Folds and thrusts in Devon, Cornwall and South Wales; Cornubian granite batholith with tin-copper veins and kaolin; Lizard ophiolite; gentle folding of coalfields further north |
| Alpine (influences) | Cenozoic (mainly Oligocene to Miocene) | Africa colliding with Europe, far to the south | East-west | Gentle folds in southern England (Weald anticline, London Basin syncline, Isle of Wight monocline) and inversion of Mesozoic basins |

**Reconstructing settings**:

- Caledonian: the Iapetus suture runs roughly from the Solway Firth towards the Shannon estuary; different fossils (trilobites, brachiopods) north and south of it in the early Ordovician show two continents; the Southern Uplands are an accretionary prism of deep-sea turbidites scraped off the subducting Iapetus plate; arc volcanoes and granites formed above subduction; Dalradian rocks were deeply buried and metamorphosed (Barrovian zones).
- Variscan: granites from crustal melting of thickened crust; the Lizard ophiolite records slices of oceanic crust from the closing Rheic Ocean; thrusts and folds show northward-directed compression.

**Palaeogene Igneous Province** (about 62 to 55 Ma): basalt lava plateaus (Antrim, Mull, Skye with the Giant's Causeway columnar joints), central volcanic complexes with gabbro and granite intrusions (Skye Cuillins, Rum, Mull, Arran), and huge dyke swarms trending north-west (perpendicular to extension). It formed as the North Atlantic began to rift, with an early Iceland mantle plume providing large volumes of basaltic magma. It records the start of the opening of the North Atlantic, separating Greenland from Europe.

## Key Idea 2: northward drift

### What you need to know

- T4.2a Palaeomagnetic field directions in British rocks give palaeolatitude; calculate palaeolatitudes and interpret apparent polar wander curves.
- T4.2b Rocks record climatic change as Britain drifted north: Devonian and Permo-Triassic semi-arid and desert deposits; Carboniferous, Jurassic and Cretaceous tropical shallow marine and coal deposits.

### Explained

The inclination (I) of remanent magnetism gives palaeolatitude (λ): tan I = 2 tan λ ([OCR 09](../geology-ocr-h414/09-plate-tectonics.md)).

### Worked example

A Devonian sandstone has an inclination of 30°. tan λ = tan 30° ÷ 2 = 0.577 ÷ 2 = 0.289; λ = 16°. A Carboniferous limestone with inclination 0° formed at the equator. A Cambrian rock with inclination −60° (pointing upward, southern hemisphere): tan λ = −1.732 ÷ 2 = −0.866; λ = −41° (41° S).

**Drift path**: southern England lay at about 40 to 60° S in the Cambrian, Scotland at about 20° S on Laurentia; the two joined in the Caledonian orogeny; Britain lay south of the equator in the Devonian (desert), crossed the equator in the Carboniferous (tropical swamps and reefs), lay at about 10 to 20° N in the Permo-Triassic (desert belt), at about 30 to 40° N in the Jurassic and Cretaceous (warm shallow seas), and reached its present latitude in the Cenozoic.

| Interval | Deposits | Climate and latitude |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Devonian | Old Red Sandstone: red sandstones, conglomerates, breccias, mudstones | Semi-arid continental, south of the equator, in the rain shadow of the Caledonian mountains |
| Carboniferous | Limestones with corals (early), deltaic Coal Measures (late) | Tropical, near the equator |
| Permo-Triassic | New Red Sandstone: aeolian dune sandstones, breccias, evaporites (Zechstein salt, Triassic halite and gypsum) | Hot desert, tropical arid belt |
| Jurassic | Shallow marine mudstones, limestones, ironstones | Warm, subtropical seas |
| Cretaceous | Chalk | Warm, high sea level |

## Key Idea 3: seven snapshots (T4.3a)

### What you need to know

Relate sedimentary rocks to global plate tectonics and climate in seven intervals, interpreting maps, fossils, rocks and structures.

### Explained

1. **Neoproterozoic**: breakup of the supercontinent Rodinia; global cooling about 700 Ma. Evidence: the Port Askaig Formation (Islay) diamictites (glacial deposits), used in evaluating the Snowball Earth hypothesis ([OCR 22](../geology-ocr-h414/22-the-changing-earth-climate-and-sea-level.md)); the Ediacaran frond *Charnia* in Charnwood Forest, showing early complex life.
2. **Early Palaeozoic**: northern and southern Britain on different continents separated by the Iapetus Ocean. Shallow seas: Cambrian and Silurian sandstones, shales and limestones with corals, brachiopods and trilobites; deep seas: Ordovician black graptolitic shales and turbidites ([OCR 27](../geology-ocr-h414/27-whole-basin-facies-analysis.md)).
3. **Mid Palaeozoic**: Caledonian orogeny and joining into the continent of Euramerica (Laurussia). Continental red beds: Devonian sandstones, breccias, conglomerates and mudstones (rivers, alluvial fans and lakes).
4. **Late Palaeozoic**: Britain drifted north across the equator; possible destruction of an ocean (Rheic) in the Variscan orogeny. Equatorial rainforest conditions: Coal Measures with sandstones, shales, freshwater bivalves and plant remains; coal seams on seat earths with rootlets.
5. **Early Mesozoic**: Laurasia separated from Gondwana by the Tethys Ocean in southern Europe; rifting and subsidence in the North Sea related to Atlantic opening. Continental red beds and evaporites in the Permo-Triassic (semi-arid and desert, hypersaline seas); Jurassic shallow marine shelf deposits.
6. **Late Mesozoic**: continued opening of the Atlantic and North Sea subsidence. Open marine Cretaceous chalk records high global temperatures and sea levels.
7. **Cenozoic**: Alpine orogeny with uplift and folding in southern Britain; continuing North Sea subsidence. Shallow marine and non-marine Palaeogene deposits (London Clay, Hampshire Basin sands) record transgressive-regressive cycles; Palaeogene Igneous Province in the north-west.

### Common mistakes

- Saying Scotland and England were always joined.
- Giving the Caledonian trend as east-west (it is north-east to south-west).
- Saying Britain's past deserts were near today's latitude.

# Momentum, impulse, work, energy, power and efficiency

Momentum and energy are both conserved, and between them they solve most collision and motion problems. This file covers momentum and its conservation, force as rate of change of momentum, impulse and force–time graphs, elastic and inelastic collisions and explosions, work done, power (including P = Fv), efficiency, and conservation of energy with kinetic and gravitational potential energy.

## Overview

AS & A for every board except Edexcel Topic 6 (impulse, two-dimensional collisions, Ek = p²/2m), which is A-level only. Elastic strain energy is in file 09. Angular momentum is in file 24.

### Board differences

- AQA 3.4.1.6 to 3.4.1.8 · Edexcel 21, 22, 25 to 30, 97 to 102, CP9, CP10 · OCR A 3.3, 3.5.1(b) to (e), 3.5.2 · OCR B 4.2 · Eduqas C1.3(d) to (f), C1.4.

- Edexcel CP9: force and change of momentum. Edexcel CP10: ICT analysis of collisions of small spheres.

- AQA mentions ethical transport design (crumple zones, airbags) and estimating energy from food.

## 3.4.1.6 Momentum

*Refs: AQA 3.4.1.6 · Edexcel 21, 22, 97 to 102, CP9, CP10 · OCR A 3.5.1(b) to (e), 3.5.2(a) to (c) · OCR B 4.2(a)(ix), (b)(i), (c)(iv), (vi), (d)(i) · Eduqas C1.3(d), (e), (f). Level: AS & A (Edexcel 97 to 102: A-level only).*

### What you need to know

- Momentum p = mv (a vector).
- The principle of conservation of linear momentum, applied in one dimension (Edexcel, OCR A also two dimensions).
- Force = rate of change of momentum: F = Δ(mv)/Δt.
- Impulse = FΔt = Δ(mv); impulse = area under a force–time graph.
- Impact forces depend on contact time (kicking a ball, crumple zones, packaging).
- Elastic and inelastic collisions; explosions.
- (Edexcel 102) Ek = p²/2m.

### Explained

**Momentum** p = mv, unit kg m s⁻¹ (or N s). It has the direction of the velocity.

**Conservation of momentum.** In any interaction, if no external resultant force acts, the total momentum of the system stays constant. In one dimension:

m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

Choose a positive direction; velocities in the other direction are negative.

**Link to Newton's laws.** In a collision, A pushes on B and B pushes on A with equal and opposite forces (third law) for the same time. So the impulses are equal and opposite, and the momentum gained by one equals the momentum lost by the other. OCR B 4.2(a)(ix) puts it the other way round: if momentum is conserved, the forces must be equal and opposite.

**Newton's second law in momentum form.** F = Δp/Δt. When mass is constant this becomes F = mΔv/Δt = ma. The momentum form also works when mass changes (a rocket, a conveyor belt being loaded, water from a hose hitting a wall: F = (mass per second) × change in velocity).

**Impulse.** FΔt = Δ(mv). For a varying force, impulse = area under the force–time graph. Unit N s (= kg m s⁻¹).

**Reducing impact forces.** For a given change in momentum, a longer contact time means a smaller average force. Crumple zones, airbags, seat belts that stretch, cycle helmets with crushable foam, padded packaging, bending your knees when landing, and soft sports mats all increase stopping time. Conversely, follow-through in a golf swing or kick increases contact time to increase the change in momentum.

**Collisions.**

| Type | Momentum | Kinetic energy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Elastic | Conserved | Conserved |
| Inelastic | Conserved | Not conserved (some becomes thermal, sound, deformation) |
| Perfectly inelastic | Conserved | Maximum loss; the objects stick together |

Total energy is always conserved; only kinetic energy can change. Check whether a collision is elastic by calculating total Ek before and after (Edexcel 101).

**Explosions.** Objects start at rest (zero momentum) and fly apart: total momentum afterwards is still zero, so m₁v₁ = −m₂v₂. Kinetic energy increases (from chemical or elastic energy). Example: the recoil of a gun.

**Ek = p²/2m** (Edexcel 102). Since p = mv, v = p/m and Ek = ½m(p/m)² = p²/2m. Useful when two objects have equal and opposite momenta: the lighter one has more kinetic energy.

**Two dimensions** (Edexcel 99, OCR A 3.5.2(b)). Momentum is conserved separately in two perpendicular directions. Resolve each velocity into x and y components and write one conservation equation for each.

### Worked example

1. A 1.2 kg trolley at 3.0 m s⁻¹ hits a stationary 0.80 kg trolley; they stick together. Find their velocity and the kinetic energy lost.

   1.2 × 3.0 = 2.0v, v = 1.8 m s⁻¹. Ek before = ½ × 1.2 × 9 = 5.4 J. After = ½ × 2.0 × 1.8² = 3.24 J. Loss = 2.16 J (inelastic).

2. A 0.045 kg golf ball leaves the club at 65 m s⁻¹. Contact lasts 0.50 ms. Find the average force.

   F = Δp/Δt = 0.045 × 65 / 0.50 × 10⁻³ = 5850 N.

3. A 4.0 kg rifle fires a 0.010 kg bullet at 400 m s⁻¹. Find the recoil speed.

   0 = 0.010 × 400 + 4.0v, v = −1.0 m s⁻¹ (1.0 m s⁻¹ backwards).

4. A force–time graph is a triangle rising from 0 to 300 N in 0.02 s and falling back to 0 at 0.05 s. A 0.40 kg ball starts at rest. Find its final speed.

   Area = ½ × 0.05 × 300 = 7.5 N s. v = 7.5/0.40 = 18.75 m s⁻¹.

5. (Edexcel/OCR A, 2D) A 2.0 kg puck moving at 3.0 m s⁻¹ east hits a stationary 1.0 kg puck. Afterwards the 2.0 kg puck moves at 2.0 m s⁻¹ at 30° north of east. Find the velocity of the 1.0 kg puck.

   x: 6.0 = 2.0 × 2.0 cos 30° + 1.0v_x, v_x = 6.0 − 3.46 = 2.54 m s⁻¹. y: 0 = 2.0 × 2.0 sin 30° + 1.0v_y, v_y = −2.0 m s⁻¹. Speed = √(2.54² + 2²) = 3.23 m s⁻¹ at 38° south of east.

### Common mistakes

- Forgetting that velocity (and momentum) is negative in the opposite direction.
- Saying kinetic energy is conserved in all collisions.
- Saying energy is lost in an inelastic collision; kinetic energy is transferred to other stores.
- Writing impulse = Ft for a varying force instead of using the area.
- Saying a longer stopping time reduces the change in momentum (it reduces the force).

### Practical

**Edexcel CP9:** investigate the relationship between force and change of momentum. A trolley is pulled by a constant force (hanging mass over a pulley); two light gates (or a light gate and data logger) give the velocity at two points and the time between. Calculate Δ(mv)/Δt and compare with the applied force; or use a force sensor for a collision and integrate the force–time graph.

**Edexcel CP10, OCR A PAG1, OCR B 4.2(d)(i):** analyse collisions with ICT. Film ball bearings or air-track gliders colliding with a scale in view (or use light gates on an air track). Use frame-by-frame video analysis to find velocities before and after; calculate total momentum and kinetic energy before and after. For two-dimensional collisions on a table top, resolve velocities along two axes.

## 3.4.1.7 Work, energy and power

*Refs: AQA 3.4.1.7 · Edexcel 25, 29, 30 · OCR A 3.3.1(a), (b), (e), 3.3.3(a) to (c) · OCR B 4.2(a)(iv), (vi), (c)(vii), (x) · Eduqas C1.4(a), (b), (e), (g). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Work done W = Fs cos θ, where θ is the angle between the force and the displacement.
- Power is the rate of doing work = rate of energy transfer: P = ΔW/Δt = Fv.
- Work done by a variable force = area under a force–displacement graph.
- Efficiency = useful output power ÷ input power (or useful energy out ÷ energy in), as a ratio or a percentage.

### Explained

**Work.** Work is done when a force moves its point of application in the direction of the force. W = Fs cos θ. Unit: joule (1 J = 1 N m). Work done = energy transferred.

- θ = 0: W = Fs.
- θ = 90°: W = 0. A force at right angles to motion does no work (e.g. the centripetal force on an orbiting satellite; carrying a bag horizontally at constant height).
- θ = 180°: negative work (friction and drag take energy out).

**Variable force.** Work = area under the force–displacement graph. For a spring obeying Hooke's law, the graph is a straight line from the origin, so W = ½Fx (file 09).

**Power.** P = W/t (unit W = J s⁻¹). For a force moving an object at constant velocity v: P = Fs/t = Fv. A car at a steady top speed has driving force = resistive force, so engine power = resistive force × speed.

**Efficiency.**

efficiency = useful energy output / total energy input = useful power output / total power input

It is always less than 1 (100%) because some energy is dissipated, usually as thermal energy.

### Worked example

1. A crate is dragged 15 m along the floor by a rope at 30° to the horizontal with tension 120 N. Find the work done by the rope.

   W = 120 × 15 × cos 30° = 1560 J.

2. A car's engine provides 45 kW at a steady 30 m s⁻¹. Find the total resistive force.

   F = P/v = 45 000/30 = 1500 N.

3. An electric motor lifts a 2.0 kg mass through 1.5 m in 4.0 s. It draws 0.80 A at 12 V. Find its efficiency.

   Useful power = mgh/t = 2.0 × 9.81 × 1.5 / 4.0 = 7.36 W. Input = 12 × 0.80 = 9.6 W. Efficiency = 0.77 (77%).

### Common mistakes

- Using sin θ in place of cos θ when the angle is between force and displacement.
- Saying work is done when holding a heavy object still (no displacement, no work, even though you tire).
- Using P = Fv with a driving force that is not the force actually moving at v.
- Giving efficiency greater than 100%.

### Practical

**Efficiency of an electric motor** (suggested by AQA). Use a small motor to wind up a string and lift a known mass through a measured height. Measure current and p.d. with an ammeter and voltmeter and time with a stopwatch (or light gate). Efficiency = mgh / (VIt). Repeat for different masses. Errors: the mass accelerating at the start (so not all work is mgh), timing errors, varying current; lift through a larger height to reduce percentage uncertainty in time.

## 3.4.1.8 Conservation of energy

*Refs: AQA 3.4.1.8 · Edexcel 26, 27, 28 · OCR A 3.3.1(c), (d), 3.3.2(a) to (c) · OCR B 4.2(a)(v), (c)(viii), (ix) · Eduqas C1.4(c), (d), (f). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The principle of conservation of energy.
- ΔEp = mgΔh and Ek = ½mv².
- Energy problems with gravitational potential energy, kinetic energy and work done against resistive forces.
- (Eduqas) The work–energy relationship Fx = ½mv² − ½mu²; dissipative forces such as friction and drag.

### Explained

**Conservation of energy.** Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one store to another. The total energy of a closed system is constant.

**Kinetic energy** Ek = ½mv². Derivation (OCR A): a constant force F accelerates mass m from rest over distance s: work done Fs = mas, and v² = 2as, so Fs = ½mv².

**Gravitational potential energy** near the Earth's surface: ΔEp = mgΔh (g roughly constant). For large heights see file 14.

**Energy equations.** Write: energy at start = energy at end + energy dissipated.

- Falling object, no air resistance: mgh = ½mv², so v = √(2gh), independent of mass.
- With resistance: mgh = ½mv² + F_resistive × distance.
- A pendulum swings: Ek at the bottom = mgh at the top (height above the lowest point).

**Work–energy relationship.** The work done by the resultant force equals the change in kinetic energy: Fx = ½mv² − ½mu² (Eduqas C1.4(d)). Braking distance comes straight from this: F_brake × d = ½mv².

**Dissipative forces** (Eduqas C1.4(f)) such as friction and drag do negative work and transfer energy to thermal stores, so mechanical energy (Ek + Ep) falls.

**Energy from food** (AQA). Food labels give energy in kJ (or kcal; 1 kcal ≈ 4.2 kJ). A daily intake of about 10 MJ means an average power of 10⁷/86 400 ≈ 120 W.

### Worked example

1. A 0.20 kg ball is dropped from 5.0 m. Find its speed when it hits the ground (no air resistance).

   v = √(2 × 9.81 × 5.0) = 9.9 m s⁻¹.

2. A 65 kg cyclist freewheels from rest down a hill that drops 12 m vertically over a 150 m road, reaching 11 m s⁻¹. Find the average resistive force.

   Ep lost = 65 × 9.81 × 12 = 7652 J. Ek gained = ½ × 65 × 121 = 3933 J. Work against resistance = 3720 J. F = 3720/150 = 24.8 N.

3. A 1100 kg car travelling at 20 m s⁻¹ brakes to rest in 32 m. Find the average braking force.

   F = ½mv²/d = 0.5 × 1100 × 400 / 32 = 6875 N.

### Common mistakes

- Using the distance along the slope instead of the vertical height in mgh.
- Forgetting to square v in ½mv².
- Saying a heavier object reaches a higher speed falling the same height (ignoring drag, the speed is the same).

## Check questions

1. State the principle of conservation of linear momentum. (2 marks)
2. A 0.15 kg ball hits a wall at 12 m s⁻¹ and rebounds at 9.0 m s⁻¹. Contact lasts 0.040 s. Find the change in momentum and the average force on the ball. (4 marks)
3. Explain how a car's crumple zone reduces injuries in a crash. (3 marks)
4. A 3.0 kg mass moving at 4.0 m s⁻¹ collides with a 1.0 kg mass at rest. Afterwards the 3.0 kg mass moves at 2.0 m s⁻¹ in the same direction. Find the speed of the 1.0 kg mass and show whether the collision is elastic. (4 marks)
5. Derive Ek = p²/2m. (2 marks)
6. A pump lifts 200 kg of water per minute through 12 m. Its input power is 600 W. Calculate its efficiency. (3 marks)
7. A 70 kg skier starts from rest and descends 40 m vertically. 30% of the energy is dissipated. Calculate the final speed. (3 marks)
8. A truck has a maximum power of 300 kW and a top speed of 30 m s⁻¹ on a level road. Calculate the resistive force at top speed and explain why the truck cannot go faster. (3 marks)

## Mark scheme

1. Total momentum of a system remains constant (1); provided no external (resultant) force acts (1).
2. Δp = 0.15 × (9.0 − (−12)) = 3.15 kg m s⁻¹ (2); F = 3.15/0.040 = 79 N (2).
3. Crumple zone deforms, increasing the time over which the car (and occupants) stop (1); same change in momentum (1); so smaller force, since F = Δp/Δt (1).
4. 12 = 6.0 + 1.0v, v = 6.0 m s⁻¹ (2); Ek before = 24 J, after = 6 + 18 = 24 J (1); elastic (1).
5. v = p/m (1); Ek = ½m(p/m)² = p²/2m (1).
6. Useful power = (200 × 9.81 × 12)/60 = 392 W (2); efficiency = 392/600 = 0.65 (65%) (1).
7. Ep = 70 × 9.81 × 40 = 27 470 J (1); Ek = 0.70 × 27 470 = 19 230 J (1); v = √(2 × 19 230/70) = 23 m s⁻¹ (1).
8. F = P/v = 300 000/30 = 10 000 N (1); at top speed all the power is used to do work against resistive forces (1); resistance increases with speed, so no spare power/force to accelerate (1).

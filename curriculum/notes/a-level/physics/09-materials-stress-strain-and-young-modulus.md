# Materials: Hooke's law, stress, strain, Young modulus and material structure

How do solids stretch, and when do they break? This file covers Hooke's law and spring constants, elastic and plastic behaviour, force–extension and stress–strain graphs, elastic strain energy, the Young modulus and how to measure it (AQA required practical 4), and, for OCR B and Eduqas, how the structure of metals, ceramics, glasses and polymers explains their behaviour.

## 3.4.2.1 Bulk properties of solids

*Refs: AQA 3.4.2.1 · Edexcel 53, 55, 56, 58 · OCR A 3.4.1(a) to (d), 3.4.2(a), (b), (c), (e), (f) · OCR B 3.2(a)(i) to (iii), (b)(i) to (iv), (c)(i), (d)(i) · Eduqas C2.5(a), (c) to (g). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Density ρ = m/V (AQA).
- Hooke's law F = kΔL; k is the stiffness or spring constant; the elastic limit.
- Tensile stress and tensile strain; breaking stress.
- Elastic strain energy = ½FΔL = area under a force–extension graph.
- Plastic behaviour, fracture and brittle behaviour on force–extension graphs.
- Energy conservation with elastic strain energy (spring energy → kinetic and gravitational potential energy).
- Simple stress–strain curves.
- Limit of proportionality, elastic limit, yield point, elastic and plastic deformation (Edexcel 55).
- (OCR B, Eduqas) How the microstructure of metals, glasses, ceramics and polymers explains their mechanical behaviour.

### Explained

**Density** ρ = m/V, unit kg m⁻³. To find the density of a regular solid, measure dimensions with vernier calipers or a micrometer and mass with a balance; for an irregular solid, find its volume by displacement of water.

**Hooke's law.** The extension of a spring or wire is directly proportional to the applied force, up to the limit of proportionality: F = kΔL, where k (N m⁻¹) is the spring constant (stiffness). A stiffer spring has a larger k.

**Springs in combination** (OCR B 3.2(d)(i)).

- Series: 1/k_total = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂ (two identical springs in series: k/2; each stretches by the full force).
- Parallel: k_total = k₁ + k₂ (two identical: 2k; the force is shared).

**Key points on a force–extension (or stress–strain) graph for a ductile metal such as copper.**

| Point | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Limit of proportionality | End of the straight-line region; beyond it Hooke's law no longer applies |
| Elastic limit | The largest force after which the material still returns to its original length when the force is removed |
| Yield point | Where the material suddenly stretches a lot for little or no extra force; plastic flow begins |
| Ultimate tensile strength (UTS) | The maximum stress the material can take |
| Breaking point (fracture) | Where it breaks; breaking stress |

**Elastic and plastic deformation.**

- Elastic: the material returns to its original shape when the force is removed. Atoms are pulled slightly apart but bonds are not broken; energy stored is recovered.
- Plastic: permanent deformation. In metals, planes of atoms slip past one another. When unloaded, the material keeps a permanent extension; unloading follows a line parallel to the original elastic line.

**Material behaviour.**

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stiff | High Young modulus; small strain for a given stress | Steel, diamond |
| Strong | High breaking stress | Steel, Kevlar |
| Ductile | Can be drawn into wires; large plastic deformation before fracture | Copper |
| Brittle | Breaks with little or no plastic deformation | Glass, cast iron, ceramics |
| Tough | Absorbs a lot of energy before breaking (resists crack growth) | Rubber, many metals |
| Hard | Resists surface indentation or scratching | Diamond |
| Malleable | Can be hammered into shape | Gold |

**Graphs to recognise.**

- Ductile metal (copper): straight line, then curve, a long plastic region, necking (a narrow waist forms), then fracture.
- Brittle (glass, cast iron): straight line all the way to sudden fracture; no plastic region.
- Polymer (rubber): curved from the start (Hooke's law only roughly obeyed), large strain, low Young modulus. Loading and unloading curves differ (hysteresis): the area between them is energy transferred to thermal energy, which is why bouncing rubber warms up.
- Polythene: stretches plastically and a great deal; it "necks" and draws out.

**Elastic strain energy.** Work done stretching = area under the force–extension graph. Within Hooke's law: E = ½FΔL = ½k(ΔL)². For a non-linear graph, count squares. When a stretched catapult or spring is released, elastic strain energy becomes kinetic energy (and gravitational potential energy if it goes upwards): ½kx² = ½mv². Some energy is always dissipated.

Energy per unit volume = ½ × stress × strain (area under the stress–strain graph).

**Breaking stress** is the stress at which the material breaks. It is a property of the material, not of the sample's size.

**Transport design** (AQA). Crumple zones are designed to deform plastically, absorbing kinetic energy over a longer distance and time and reducing forces on passengers.

**Structure of materials** (OCR B 3.2(a)(iii), Eduqas C2.5(d) to (g)).

- *Crystalline solids* (metals, many ceramics): atoms in a regular repeating lattice.
- *Amorphous solids* (glass, many ceramics): atoms are disordered, with no long-range order.
- *Polymers*: long chain molecules, tangled together (rubber, polythene).

*Metals.* Metals are made of many small crystals (grains). The lattice contains dislocations: lines where an extra half-plane of atoms is squeezed in. Under stress, a dislocation can move through the lattice one row of bonds at a time, so planes slip past each other at much lower stress than if whole planes had to slide at once. This is why metals are ductile. Metals are strengthened by blocking dislocation movement: adding foreign atoms (alloying, e.g. carbon in steel), creating more dislocations that tangle (work-hardening), or making smaller grains (more grain boundaries). Ductile fracture: after yielding, the wire thins at one point (necking), stress there rises, and the wire tears apart.

*Glasses and ceramics.* No mobile dislocations, so they cannot deform plastically. They obey Hooke's law up to fracture. Tiny surface cracks concentrate stress at their tips; under tension the crack tip bonds break one after another and the crack runs through the material (brittle fracture by crack propagation). Breaking stress is raised by:

- reducing surface imperfections (thin glass fibres are very strong);
- putting the surface under compression, so cracks are squeezed shut (toughened glass, cooled so its surface is in compression; pre-stressed concrete, cast round stretched steel rods so the concrete is compressed). Under compression, brittle materials are strong.

*Rubber.* Long chains are coiled and tangled, held loosely by a few cross-links. Stretching straightens the chains against their thermal motion (which tends to coil them up again), so it is easy at first (low Young modulus, large strain). Once the chains are straight, the bonds within the chains must be stretched and it becomes much stiffer.

**Evidence for the size of particles** (OCR B 3.2(a)(ii)). A scanning tunnelling microscope (STM) images individual atoms on a surface, showing spacing of about 0.1 to 0.3 nm. In the oil-drop experiment, a drop of oil of known volume spreads on water into a layer one molecule thick: thickness = volume ÷ area, giving molecular size of order 10⁻⁹ m.

### Worked example

1. A spring stretches 40 mm under a 6.0 N load. Find k and the energy stored.

   k = 6.0/0.040 = 150 N m⁻¹. E = ½ × 6.0 × 0.040 = 0.12 J.

2. A catapult with k = 400 N m⁻¹ is pulled back 0.15 m and fires a 0.020 kg stone. Find the maximum speed of the stone.

   ½kx² = ½ × 400 × 0.0225 = 4.5 J = ½ × 0.020 × v², v = 21 m s⁻¹.

3. Two springs of k = 200 N m⁻¹ are joined in series. A 4.0 N weight hangs from them. Find the total extension.

   k_total = 100 N m⁻¹. Extension = 0.040 m.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the elastic limit and the limit of proportionality are always the same point.
- Calling a material that breaks at a high stress "tough" (that is strong; tough means absorbs much energy).
- Using ½FΔL when the graph is not a straight line.
- Saying brittle materials are weak; glass is strong in compression.
- Saying dislocations make metals stronger (mobile dislocations make them weaker and ductile; tangled or pinned ones make them stronger).

## 3.4.2.2 The Young modulus

*Refs: AQA 3.4.2.2, RP4 · Edexcel 54, 56, 57, CP5 · OCR A 3.4.2(c), (d) · OCR B 3.2(a)(iv), (b)(ii), (c)(ii), (d)(ii) · Eduqas C2.5(b). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Tensile stress σ = F/A; tensile strain ε = ΔL/L; Young modulus E = stress/strain = FL/(AΔL).
- Use a stress–strain graph to find the Young modulus (gradient of the linear part).
- One simple method of measuring the Young modulus (AQA RP4).

### Explained

- **Stress** = force ÷ cross-sectional area, σ = F/A. Unit Pa (N m⁻²).
- **Strain** = extension ÷ original length, ε = ΔL/L. No unit (a ratio).
- **Young modulus** E = σ/ε = FL/(AΔL). Unit Pa. It measures stiffness of the *material*, independent of the sample's dimensions (unlike k, which depends on the length and thickness of the sample). E only applies within the limit of proportionality.

| Material | E / GPa |
| --- | --- |
| Rubber | 0.01 to 0.1 |
| Polythene | 0.2 to 1 |
| Bone | about 15 |
| Copper | about 120 |
| Steel | about 200 |

Link with k: k = EA/L.

### Worked example

1. A steel wire 2.0 m long, diameter 0.50 mm, stretches 1.2 mm under a 40 N load. Find E.

   A = π(0.25 × 10⁻³)² = 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ m². Stress = 40/1.96 × 10⁻⁷ = 2.04 × 10⁸ Pa. Strain = 1.2 × 10⁻³/2.0 = 6.0 × 10⁻⁴. E = 3.4 × 10¹¹ Pa. (Higher than typical steel; worth checking the readings.)

2. A copper wire (E = 1.2 × 10¹¹ Pa) 1.5 m long with A = 3.0 × 10⁻⁷ m² supports 25 N. Find the extension.

   ΔL = FL/(AE) = 25 × 1.5 / (3.0 × 10⁻⁷ × 1.2 × 10¹¹) = 1.04 × 10⁻³ m.

### Common mistakes

- Using the diameter as the radius when finding A.
- Forgetting to convert mm to m (and mm² to m²).
- Giving strain a unit.
- Finding E from the curved part of the graph.

### Practical

**AQA required practical 4** (also Edexcel CP5, OCR A PAG2, OCR B 3.2(d)(ii), Eduqas specified practical): determine the Young modulus of a metal wire.

1. Clamp a long (2 to 3 m) thin wire at one end; run it over a pulley at the edge of the bench to a mass hanger. (Alternatively use Searle's apparatus with a reference wire and a vernier.)
2. Put a marker (a strip of tape or a pointer) on the wire near the pulley, above a fixed metre rule.
3. Measure the original length L from the clamp to the marker with a tape measure.
4. Measure the diameter with a micrometer at several points and orientations; average. Calculate A = πd²/4.
5. Add masses in equal steps (e.g. 100 g); record the marker position each time and find the extension. Also record readings while unloading, to check the elastic limit was not exceeded.
6. Plot stress against strain (or F against ΔL). E = gradient of the straight part (or gradient × L/A).

Why: a long wire gives a measurable extension; a thin wire gives a large stress for small loads. The biggest percentage uncertainty is usually in the diameter (squared) and the extension. Safety: wear goggles (the wire may snap and whip); put a box or cushion under the masses; keep feet clear.

**Force–extension of rubber, polythene and springs** (OCR A PAG2, OCR B 3.2(d)(i), Eduqas). Hang the sample from a clamp; add masses and measure length with a metre rule next to it, reading at eye level with a pointer. Measure during unloading too: rubber shows hysteresis, polythene shows large plastic deformation.

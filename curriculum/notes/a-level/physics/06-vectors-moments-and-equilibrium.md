# Scalars, vectors, moments and equilibrium

Forces have size and direction, so they add as vectors. An object is in equilibrium when the forces on it cancel and the moments about any point cancel. This file covers adding and resolving vectors, free-body diagrams, inclined planes, moments, couples, centre of mass and the conditions for equilibrium.

## Overview

## 3.4.1.1 Scalars and vectors

*Refs: AQA 3.4.1.1 · Edexcel 12, 13, 14, 16 · OCR A 2.3.1(a) to (d), 3.2.1(e), 3.2.3(e), (f) · OCR B 4.2(a)(i), (iii), (b)(i), (iii), (c)(i), (ii) · Eduqas C1.1(d), (e), (f), (k), C1.3(b). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- A scalar has size only; a vector has size and direction. Examples of each.
- Adding vectors by calculation (two at right angles) or by scale drawing (any angle).
- Resolving a vector into two perpendicular components, including along and perpendicular to an inclined plane.
- Conditions for equilibrium of two or three coplanar forces at a point; using resolved forces or a closed triangle.
- Equilibrium means at rest or moving with constant velocity.
- Free-body diagrams.

### Explained

| Scalars | Vectors |
| --- | --- |
| distance, speed, mass, time, energy, power, temperature, density, pressure, charge | displacement, velocity, acceleration, force (including weight), momentum, electric and gravitational field strength |

Weight is a vector (a force, downwards); mass is a scalar. Pressure is a scalar even though it comes from a force.

**Adding vectors.** Draw them tip to tail; the resultant goes from the start of the first to the end of the last.

- Two vectors at right angles: magnitude R = √(A² + B²); direction tan θ = B/A.
- Any angle (scale drawing): choose a scale, draw the first vector, draw the second from its tip at the correct angle, measure the resultant and its angle. (OCR A may also expect the cosine rule.)
- Subtracting a vector means adding its negative: Δv = v − u.

**Resolving.** A vector F at angle θ to the horizontal has components F cos θ (horizontal, adjacent to θ) and F sin θ (vertical). Choose axes that make the problem easy.

**Inclined plane.** For an object of weight W on a slope at angle θ to the horizontal:

- Component of weight down the slope = W sin θ.
- Component of weight perpendicular to the slope = W cos θ, balanced by the normal contact force R if there is no acceleration perpendicular to the slope.
- If the object is at rest, friction up the slope = W sin θ.

**Free-body diagrams** (Edexcel 16, OCR A 3.2.1(e), Eduqas C1.3(b)). Draw only the object, with every force acting on it as an arrow from the point where it acts, labelled with its name or symbol. Do not include forces the object exerts on other things. Common forces: weight W (from the centre of mass), normal contact force R or N (perpendicular to the surface), friction (along the surface, opposing sliding), tension T (along a string, away from the object), drag, upthrust, thrust.

**Equilibrium.** An object is in equilibrium if the resultant force on it is zero (and the resultant moment is zero, see below). It is then at rest or moving at constant velocity.

- Two forces: equal in size, opposite in direction, along the same line.
- Three coplanar forces at a point: either resolve in two perpendicular directions and set each sum to zero, or draw them tip to tail: they form a closed triangle (triangle of forces).

### Worked example

1. A boat is rowed at 2.5 m s⁻¹ due north across a river flowing east at 1.2 m s⁻¹. Find its resultant velocity.

   R = √(2.5² + 1.2²) = 2.77 m s⁻¹. tan θ = 1.2/2.5, θ = 25.6° east of north.

2. A 4.0 kg box rests on a slope at 30°. Find the normal contact force and the friction force.

   W = 39.2 N. R = W cos 30° = 34.0 N. Friction = W sin 30° = 19.6 N up the slope.

3. A 12 N picture hangs from two strings, each at 40° to the horizontal. Find the tension in each.

   Vertical: 2T sin 40° = 12, so T = 9.3 N. Horizontal components cancel.

4. A sledge is pulled with a force of 80 N at 25° above the horizontal. Find the horizontal and vertical components.

   Horizontal 80 cos 25° = 72.5 N; vertical 80 sin 25° = 33.8 N.

### Common mistakes

- Swapping sin and cos on a slope: the component down the slope is W sin θ, where θ is the slope angle.
- Including forces exerted by the object on other bodies in a free-body diagram.
- Saying an object moving at constant speed in a straight line cannot be in equilibrium.
- Adding magnitudes of vectors at an angle as if they were scalars.

## 3.4.1.2 Moments

*Refs: AQA 3.4.1.2 · Edexcel 23, 24 · OCR A 3.2.3(a) to (e) · Eduqas C1.1(h), (i), (j), (k). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The moment of a force about a point = force × perpendicular distance from the point to the line of action of the force.
- A couple is a pair of equal, opposite, coplanar forces whose lines of action do not coincide; moment (torque) of a couple = one force × perpendicular distance between the lines of action.
- The principle of moments.
- Centre of mass (and centre of gravity): for a uniform regular solid it is at the geometric centre.
- Equilibrium of an extended body: no resultant force and no resultant moment.
- (OCR A, Edexcel) Finding the centre of gravity of an irregular object experimentally.

### Explained

**Moment** = Fd, unit N m, where d is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action. If the force is at angle θ to the rod, use the component perpendicular to the rod: moment = F × l sin θ.

**Principle of moments.** For an object in equilibrium, the sum of the clockwise moments about any point equals the sum of the anticlockwise moments about that point.

**Conditions for equilibrium of an extended object.**

1. The resultant force is zero (in every direction).
2. The resultant moment about any point is zero.

Tip: take moments about the point where an unknown force acts, so that force has zero moment and drops out.

**Couples.** Two equal and opposite forces F, a perpendicular distance d apart. The resultant force is zero, so there is no linear acceleration, but there is a turning effect: torque = Fd. Examples: turning a steering wheel with both hands, twisting a tap, the forces on a coil in a motor.

**Centre of mass and centre of gravity.** The centre of mass is the point where the whole mass can be taken to act; the centre of gravity is the point where the weight appears to act. In a uniform field they are the same point. For a uniform, symmetrical object it is at the geometric centre (middle of a uniform rod, centre of a sphere). An object hung freely comes to rest with its centre of gravity directly below the pivot. An object topples when its centre of gravity moves outside its base, because the weight then has a moment that tips it further.

**Finding the centre of gravity of an irregular lamina** (OCR A 3.2.3(d)). Hang the shape from a pin through a hole near its edge so it swings freely. Hang a plumb line from the same pin and draw the vertical line on the shape. Repeat from a second (and third, as a check) hole. The centre of gravity is where the lines cross. For a rod: balance it on a knife edge and find the balance point.

**Stability.** A low centre of gravity and wide base make an object stable: it must be tilted further before its centre of gravity passes outside the base.

### Worked example

1. A uniform plank of length 4.0 m and weight 200 N rests on supports at each end. A 600 N person stands 1.0 m from the left end. Find the forces from each support.

   Moments about left end: R_right × 4.0 = 600 × 1.0 + 200 × 2.0 = 1000, so R_right = 250 N. Vertical: R_left = 800 − 250 = 550 N.

2. A spanner 0.25 m long is pushed at its end with 60 N at 70° to the handle. Find the moment about the nut.

   Perpendicular component = 60 sin 70° = 56.4 N. Moment = 56.4 × 0.25 = 14.1 N m.

3. (Eduqas practical) A metre rule balances at its 50.0 cm mark. A known 100 g mass at 10.0 cm balances an unknown mass at 75.0 cm. Find the unknown mass.

   100 × 40.0 = m × 25.0, so m = 160 g.

4. A uniform 2.0 m beam of weight 150 N is hinged to a wall and held horizontal by a cable attached at its far end at 30° above the horizontal. Find the cable tension.

   Moments about the hinge: T sin 30° × 2.0 = 150 × 1.0, so T = 150 N.

### Common mistakes

- Using the distance along the rod instead of the perpendicular distance.
- Forgetting the weight of the beam itself, acting at its centre.
- Saying a couple produces a resultant force.
- Taking moments about a point and still including the force through that point.

### Practical

**Principle of moments to find an unknown mass** (Eduqas specified practical). Balance a metre rule on a knife edge and note the balance point (its centre of gravity). Hang the unknown mass at a fixed distance on one side and move a known mass on the other side until the rule balances. Record both perpendicular distances from the pivot. Repeat for several positions; plot known-mass distance against unknown-mass distance (gradient = m_unknown/m_known). Reduce error by using thin loops of thread rather than thick hangers, reading distances at eye level, and checking balance by small taps.

## Check questions

1. Explain why a person walking at constant velocity is in equilibrium. (2 marks)
2. Two forces of 5.0 N and 12 N act at right angles. Find the resultant and its angle to the 12 N force. (3 marks)
3. A 50 kg skier stands still on a slope of 20°. Calculate the friction force on the skier. (2 marks)
4. Three forces act on a point object in equilibrium: 8.0 N east, 6.0 N north and a third force F. Find F. (2 marks)
5. Define the moment of a couple. (2 marks)
6. A uniform 3.0 m ladder of weight 250 N leans against a smooth wall at 60° to the ground. Calculate the horizontal force from the wall. (4 marks)
7. Describe how to find the centre of gravity of an irregular sheet of card. (4 marks)

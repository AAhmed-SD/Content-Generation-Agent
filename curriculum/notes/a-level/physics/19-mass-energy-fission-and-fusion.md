# Mass and energy: binding energy, fission, fusion, nuclear reactors and safety

A nucleus has less mass than the nucleons that make it. This file covers E = mc², the atomic mass unit, mass defect and binding energy, the binding energy per nucleon curve, energy released in fission and fusion, the conditions needed for fusion, chain reactions and thermal fission reactors (moderator, control rods, coolant), and the safety and waste issues of nuclear power.

## 3.8.1.6 Mass and energy

*Refs: AQA 3.8.1.6 · Edexcel 164, 165, 166, 167 · OCR A 6.4.4(a), (b), (d), (e), (f), (j), (k) · OCR B 6.2.2(a)(ii), (a)(iii), (b)(i), (b)(ii), (c)(iv) · Eduqas C3.8(a) to (e). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- E = mc² applies to all energy changes.
- Mass difference (mass defect) and binding energy.
- The atomic mass unit u; 1 u = 931.5 MeV (or 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg).
- Fission and fusion; calculating the energy released from nuclear masses.
- The graph of binding energy per nucleon against nucleon number, and the regions where fission and fusion release energy.
- (Edexcel 167) Why fusion needs very high temperatures and densities.
- Knowing the physics of nuclear energy lets society make informed decisions.

### Explained

**Mass–energy.** Energy and mass are equivalent: ΔE = Δm c². Any change in energy of a system changes its mass, though for chemical reactions the change is far too small to measure. In nuclear reactions it is large enough (about 0.1% of the mass) to calculate.

**Atomic mass unit.** 1 u = 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. Its energy equivalent: 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ × (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 1.49 × 10⁻¹⁰ J = 931.5 MeV.

**Mass defect.** The mass of a nucleus is less than the total mass of its separate protons and neutrons. Mass defect Δm = (Z m_p + N m_n) − m_nucleus.

**Binding energy.** The energy needed to separate a nucleus completely into its individual nucleons (equivalently, the energy released when the nucleus forms from them). Binding energy = Δm c². Binding energy per nucleon = binding energy ÷ A. The higher the binding energy per nucleon, the more stable the nucleus.

**The binding energy per nucleon curve.**

- Rises steeply from hydrogen (0) through deuterium (1.1 MeV), with a peak at helium-4 (7.1 MeV), to a broad maximum of about 8.8 MeV near iron-56 (A ≈ 56 to 62).
- Then falls slowly to about 7.6 MeV for uranium.

Energy is released whenever nucleons end up more tightly bound (higher on the curve):

- *Fusion* of light nuclei (left of iron) into heavier ones moves up the steep left side: large energy release per nucleon.
- *Fission* of heavy nuclei (right of iron) into two medium-sized ones moves up the gentle right side.
- Iron is at the top, so it can release energy by neither.

**Energy released** = (total mass before − total mass after) × c² = increase in total binding energy.

**Fission.** A heavy nucleus such as uranium-235 absorbs a slow neutron, becomes unstable (U-236), and splits into two smaller nuclei of roughly similar size, plus 2 or 3 neutrons and about 200 MeV of energy (mostly kinetic energy of the fragments). Example:

²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + 3¹₀n

**Fusion.** Two light nuclei join to make a heavier one, releasing energy. In the Sun, hydrogen fuses to helium (proton–proton chain). A reaction for fusion reactors: ²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n + 17.6 MeV.

**Why fusion is hard** (Edexcel 167, OCR A 6.4.4(j)). Nuclei are positive and repel. To fuse, they must get close enough (about 10⁻¹⁵ m) for the strong force to act. This needs very high kinetic energy, so temperatures of about 10⁸ K (10⁷ K in the Sun's core, helped by quantum tunnelling). At these temperatures matter is a plasma. A high density is also needed so collisions happen often enough to sustain the reaction. In stars, gravity provides the confinement; on Earth, strong magnetic fields (tokamaks) or lasers (inertial confinement).

**Society.** Understanding mass–energy allows informed decisions about nuclear power (low carbon emissions but waste and accident risks), fusion research funding and nuclear weapons.

### Worked example

1. Find the binding energy per nucleon of iron-56 (nuclear mass 55.92068 u; m_p = 1.00728 u; m_n = 1.00867 u).

   Separate nucleons: 26 × 1.00728 + 30 × 1.00867 = 26.18928 + 30.26010 = 56.44938 u. Mass defect = 0.52870 u. Binding energy = 0.52870 × 931.5 = 492.5 MeV. Per nucleon = 8.79 MeV.

2. Find the energy released in the D–T fusion reaction. Masses: ²H 2.01355 u, ³H 3.01550 u, ⁴He 4.00151 u, n 1.00867 u.

   Before: 5.02905 u. After: 5.01018 u. Δm = 0.01887 u. E = 0.01887 × 931.5 = 17.6 MeV = 2.8 × 10⁻¹² J.

3. A fission of U-235 releases 200 MeV. How much energy is released by fission of 1.0 kg of U-235?

   Nuclei = (1.0/0.235) × 6.02 × 10²³ = 2.56 × 10²⁴. E = 2.56 × 10²⁴ × 200 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 8.2 × 10¹³ J (compare about 3 × 10⁷ J from 1 kg of coal).

### Common mistakes

- Saying binding energy is energy stored in the nucleus that is released when it forms the nucleus (binding energy is the energy needed to pull it apart; a bigger binding energy means a more stable nucleus with less mass).
- Using atomic masses for one side and nuclear masses for the other.
- Saying fission of iron releases energy.
- Forgetting to multiply by 931.5 (MeV) or by c² with masses in kg (J).

## 3.8.1.7 Induced fission

*Refs: AQA 3.8.1.7 · OCR A 6.4.4(g), (h) · OCR B 6.2.2(a)(iii). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Fission induced by thermal (slow) neutrons; chain reaction; critical mass.
- The functions of the moderator, control rods and coolant in a thermal reactor.
- A simple mechanical model of moderation by elastic collisions.
- Factors affecting the choice of materials; examples of materials used.

### Explained

**Thermal neutrons.** U-235 is far more likely to absorb slow neutrons (kinetic energy about 0.025 eV, similar to the thermal energy of particles at room temperature) than the fast neutrons (about 2 MeV) produced by fission.

**Chain reaction.** Each fission releases 2 or 3 neutrons. If, on average, at least one goes on to cause another fission, the reaction sustains itself. If exactly one does, the reactor is critical (steady power). More than one: supercritical (power rises; uncontrolled in a bomb).

**Critical mass.** The minimum mass of fissile material that can sustain a chain reaction. Below it, too many neutrons escape through the surface before causing fission (a small lump has a large surface-area-to-volume ratio).

**Thermal reactor.**

| Part | Function | Material and reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fuel rods | Contain the fissile fuel | Uranium enriched to about 3% U-235 (the rest is U-238) |
| Moderator | Slows fast neutrons to thermal speeds by elastic collisions so they can cause more fission | Water, heavy water or graphite: light nuclei (close in mass to the neutron so it loses a large fraction of its energy per collision) that do not absorb neutrons much |
| Control rods | Absorb neutrons, to keep exactly one neutron per fission going on to cause another; lowered to reduce power or shut down | Boron or cadmium: they absorb neutrons readily without becoming unstable in a way that releases more |
| Coolant | Carries thermal energy from the core to a heat exchanger to make steam for turbines | Water (high specific heat capacity, cheap) or carbon dioxide gas; molten sodium in fast reactors; must not absorb neutrons strongly, must flow easily |

**Elastic collision model of moderation.** When a moving ball hits a stationary ball of the same mass head-on elastically, it stops and passes on all its kinetic energy. A neutron hitting a hydrogen nucleus (nearly equal mass) can lose most of its energy in one collision; hitting a heavy nucleus, it bounces off with nearly the same speed. For a head-on elastic collision with a nucleus of mass number A at rest, the fraction of kinetic energy kept is ((A − 1)/(A + 1))². So light nuclei are the best moderators. (Hydrogen in ordinary water absorbs some neutrons, which is why heavy water or graphite is sometimes used.)

### Worked example

1. A neutron makes a head-on elastic collision with a stationary carbon-12 nucleus. What fraction of its kinetic energy does it keep?

   ((12 − 1)/(12 + 1))² = (11/13)² = 0.716. It loses 28%. About 50 such collisions take it from 2 MeV to thermal energies (0.716⁵⁰ × 2 MeV ≈ 1 × 10⁻⁷ MeV ≈ 0.1 eV; in practice about 100 collisions, since most are not head-on).

### Common mistakes

- Saying the moderator slows the reaction down (it slows neutrons, which keeps the reaction going).
- Confusing control rods (absorb neutrons) with the moderator (slows neutrons).
- Saying critical mass is the mass that explodes.

## 3.8.1.8 Safety aspects

*Refs: AQA 3.8.1.8 · OCR A 6.4.4(i) · OCR B 6.2.2(a)(iii). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Fuel used, remote handling of fuel, shielding, emergency shut-down.
- Producing, handling and storing radioactive waste.
- The balance between risks and benefits of nuclear power.

### Explained

**In the reactor.**

- *Fuel*: enriched uranium oxide pellets in sealed metal cans; mildly radioactive before use, intensely radioactive after (fission products).
- *Remote handling*: used fuel is moved by robot arms or cranes, never by hand.
- *Shielding*: the core is inside a thick steel pressure vessel and several metres of concrete, which absorb neutrons and gamma rays.
- *Emergency shut-down*: control rods drop fully into the core (often by gravity, automatically on loss of power) to stop the chain reaction at once.

**Radioactive waste.**

- *Low-level* (gloves, clothing, tools): compacted, put in drums and buried in shallow sites.
- *Intermediate-level* (fuel cladding, reactor parts, resins): encased in concrete in steel drums; stored and later buried deeper.
- *High-level* (spent fuel and fission products): very hot and very radioactive. First cooled under water in ponds for years (still generating heat from decay), then may be reprocessed to recover uranium and plutonium; the rest is vitrified (made into glass), sealed in steel containers and stored, with plans for deep geological disposal. Some isotopes have half-lives of thousands of years, so storage must be secure for very long periods, safe from leaks into groundwater, earthquakes and theft.

**Risks and benefits.** Benefits: no carbon dioxide emitted in generation, reliable "base load" electricity, small fuel mass. Risks: accidents (Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011), long-term waste storage, cost of building and decommissioning, risk of materials being used for weapons. Society weighs these against the risks of fossil fuels (climate change, air pollution).

### Common mistakes

- Saying nuclear power stations can explode like a nuclear bomb (fuel is not enriched enough).
- Saying waste is safe after one half-life.

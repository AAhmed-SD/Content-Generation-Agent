# Fields, gravitational fields, gravitational potential and orbits

A field lets one object push or pull another without touching it. This file introduces force fields, then covers Newton's law of gravitation, gravitational field strength, gravitational potential and potential energy, equipotentials, graphs of g and V against distance, and the motion of planets and satellites (Kepler's laws, energy of orbits, escape velocity, geostationary orbits).

## Overview

A-level only for every board. Electric fields, and the side-by-side comparison of electric and gravitational fields, are in file 15. Uniform-field energy changes mgh are in file 08. Galaxy rotation, binary stars and the radial-velocity method (Eduqas C2.8(e) to (m)) are in file 21.

## 3.7.1 Fields

*Refs: AQA 3.7.1 · Edexcel 174 · OCR A 5.4.1(a), (e) · Eduqas C2.6(a), (c). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- A force field is a region in which a body experiences a non-contact force.
- A force field can be represented as a vector; its direction is found by inspection.
- Force fields arise from mass (gravitational), static charge (electric) and moving charges (magnetic).
- Similarities between gravitational and electrostatic forces: both are inverse-square laws, both use field lines, potential and equipotential surfaces. Difference: masses always attract; charges can attract or repel.

### Explained

A field describes how a force varies through space. At any point we define field strength as force per unit of the property that feels it: per kilogram for gravity, per coulomb for electric fields. Field lines show the direction of the force on a test object; their closeness shows the field strength. A uniform field has parallel, equally spaced lines; a radial field has lines pointing to or from a centre.

### Common mistakes

- Saying gravity can be repulsive.
- Drawing field lines that cross.

## 3.7.2.1 Newton's law

*Refs: AQA 3.7.2.1 · Edexcel 176 · OCR A 5.4.2(a) · OCR B 5.1.2(c)(iv) · Eduqas C2.8(b). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Gravity is a universal attractive force between all matter.
- F = Gm₁m₂/r² for point masses, where G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻².
- (OCR A, Eduqas) Spherical bodies act as point masses at their centres (outside them).

### Explained

Every pair of masses attracts with a force proportional to the product of the masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres. Double the distance: force falls to a quarter. The force on each mass is the same size (Newton's third law). G is tiny, so the force between everyday objects is negligible; gravity dominates only when at least one mass is huge.

A uniform sphere behaves, for points outside it, as if all its mass were at its centre (OCR A 5.4.1(b), Eduqas C2.6(b)), so r is measured from the centre.

### Worked example

1. Estimate the gravitational force between two 70 kg people 1.0 m apart.

   F = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 70 × 70 / 1.0² = 3.3 × 10⁻⁷ N, utterly negligible.

2. Find the force between the Earth (6.0 × 10²⁴ kg) and the Moon (7.3 × 10²² kg), 3.8 × 10⁸ m apart.

   F = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 6.0 × 10²⁴ × 7.3 × 10²² / (3.8 × 10⁸)² = 2.0 × 10²⁰ N.

### Common mistakes

- Using the distance from the surface instead of from the centre.
- Forgetting to square r.

## 3.7.2.2 Gravitational field strength

*Refs: AQA 3.7.2.2 · Edexcel 175, 177 · OCR A 5.4.1(b), (c), (d), 5.4.2(b), (c) · OCR B 5.1.2(a)(ii), (iii), (c)(iv) · Eduqas C2.6(b), (c), (e). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Gravitational field lines.
- g = F/m (force per unit mass); g = GM/r² in a radial field.
- (OCR A) The field near the Earth's surface is approximately uniform.

### Explained

**Definition.** Gravitational field strength at a point is the force per unit mass on a small test mass placed there: g = F/m, unit N kg⁻¹ (equivalent to m s⁻²). It is a vector, directed towards the mass.

**Radial field.** Dividing F = GMm/r² by m: g = GM/r². Field lines point radially inwards towards the centre of the mass.

**Uniform field.** Near the Earth's surface over small height changes, g is nearly constant (9.81 N kg⁻¹): parallel, equally spaced field lines pointing down.

**Graph of g against r** for a planet of radius R: outside, g = GM/r², falling as 1/r². (Inside a uniform planet g rises linearly from zero at the centre to g_surface at R; not required by most boards.)

**Combining fields** (Eduqas C2.6(e)). Field strength is a vector: add the contributions from each mass as vectors. Between the Earth and the Moon there is a point where their fields cancel: GM_E/x² = GM_M/(d − x)².

**Deriving the Earth's mass.** g = GM/R², so M = gR²/G = 9.81 × (6.37 × 10⁶)²/6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg.

### Worked example

1. Find g at the height of the International Space Station, 400 km above the Earth's surface (R = 6.37 × 10⁶ m, M = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg).

   r = 6.77 × 10⁶ m. g = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴/(6.77 × 10⁶)² = 8.69 N kg⁻¹. (Astronauts float because they are in free fall, not because g is zero.)

2. At what distance from the Earth's centre is g one ninth of its surface value?

   g ∝ 1/r², so r = 3R = 1.9 × 10⁷ m.

### Common mistakes

- Saying there is no gravity on the ISS.
- Treating g as a constant at large distances.

## 3.7.2.3 Gravitational potential

*Refs: AQA 3.7.2.3 · Edexcel 178 · OCR A 5.4.4(a) to (d) · OCR B 5.1.2(a)(i), (iii), (b)(i) to (iv), (c)(i), (ii), (v), (vi) · Eduqas C2.6(d), (e), (f). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Gravitational potential at a point is the work done per unit mass in bringing a small mass from infinity to that point; it is zero at infinity.
- Gravitational potential difference; work done ΔW = mΔV.
- Equipotential surfaces; no work is done moving along one.
- V = −GM/r in a radial field; the significance of the negative sign.
- Graphs of g and V against r; g = −ΔV/Δr; ΔV = area under the g–r graph.
- (Edexcel, OCR A, OCR B) Gravitational potential energy E_p = mV = −GMm/r; force–distance and energy–distance graphs.

### Explained

**Gravitational potential** V at a point: the work done per unit mass to bring a small test mass from infinity to that point. Unit J kg⁻¹.

V = −GM/r

- Zero at infinity (by definition).
- Negative everywhere else, because gravity is attractive: work is done *by* the field as a mass falls in from infinity, so energy is released, not supplied. You would have to do positive work to take it back out to infinity.
- Increases (becomes less negative) with distance.

**Potential difference.** Moving mass m between two points with potential difference ΔV needs work ΔW = mΔV. Near the Earth's surface ΔV = gΔh, giving ΔW = mgΔh (Eduqas C2.6(f), OCR B 5.1.2(c)(i)).

**Gravitational potential energy** (Edexcel, OCR A, OCR B): E_p = mV = −GMm/r. Change in E_p between r₁ and r₂: ΔE_p = GMm(1/r₁ − 1/r₂).

**Equipotentials.** Surfaces of equal potential. In a radial field they are spheres around the mass; near the Earth's surface they are horizontal planes. Equipotentials are always perpendicular to field lines. Moving along an equipotential involves no work (no change in potential, and the force is perpendicular to the motion). Equipotentials for equal steps of V get further apart as r increases.

**Field and potential.** g = −ΔV/Δr: the field strength is minus the gradient of the potential–distance graph. Conversely, the area under a g–r graph between two radii is the potential difference.

Similarly (OCR B 5.1.2(b)(ii), (iii)): the area under a force–distance graph is the change in potential energy, and force = minus the gradient of the potential energy–distance graph.

**Graphs.**

- V against r: −1/r curve, negative, approaching zero from below as r → ∞, steepest near the surface.
- g against r: 1/r² curve, positive, falling faster than V.

### Worked example

1. Find the gravitational potential at the Earth's surface and the energy needed to lift a 1000 kg satellite from the surface to a height of 2.0 × 10⁶ m.

   V_surface = −GM/R = −6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴/6.37 × 10⁶ = −6.25 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹. At r = 8.37 × 10⁶ m: V = −4.76 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹. ΔV = 1.49 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹. Energy = mΔV = 1.49 × 10¹⁰ J. (Using mgh would give 1.96 × 10¹⁰ J, too much, because g falls with height.)

2. At a point the potential changes from −5.0 × 10⁷ to −4.8 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹ over 100 km. Estimate g there.

   g = −ΔV/Δr = −(2.0 × 10⁶)/(1.0 × 10⁵), magnitude 20 N kg⁻¹ (towards decreasing r).

### Common mistakes

- Saying potential is zero at the surface of the Earth.
- Forgetting the minus sign in V = −GM/r, or thinking a more negative V is higher.
- Using mgh for large distances.
- Saying work is done moving a satellite around a circular orbit.

## 3.7.2.4 Orbits of planets and satellites

*Refs: AQA 3.7.2.4 · Edexcel 180 · OCR A 5.4.3(a) to (e), 5.4.4(e) · OCR B 5.1.2(a)(v) · Eduqas C2.8(a) to (d). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Orbital period and speed related to orbital radius; derivation of T² ∝ r³.
- Energy of an orbiting satellite: kinetic, potential and total.
- Escape velocity.
- Synchronous orbits; low orbits and geostationary orbits, including the plane and radius of a geostationary orbit.
- (OCR A, Eduqas) Kepler's three laws; using orbital data to find the mass of the central body.

### Explained

**Kepler's laws** (OCR A, Eduqas).

1. Planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus.
2. A line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times (so planets move faster when closer to the Sun).
3. The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the mean distance from the Sun: T² ∝ r³.

**Circular orbits.** Gravity provides the centripetal force:

GMm/r² = mv²/r → **v = √(GM/r)**

Closer orbits are faster. With v = 2πr/T:

GM/r² = 4π²r/T² → **T² = (4π²/GM)r³**

This is Kepler's third law, and the constant depends only on the central mass. So measuring T and r for a satellite (or moon) gives the central mass: M = 4π²r³/(GT²) (Eduqas C2.8(d)). Plotting log T against log r gives a straight line of gradient 1.5.

**Energy in orbit.**

- Kinetic: E_k = ½mv² = GMm/2r.
- Potential: E_p = −GMm/r.
- Total: E = E_k + E_p = −GMm/2r.

A satellite in a lower orbit has more kinetic energy but less (more negative) total energy. To move to a higher orbit, energy must be supplied (the total becomes less negative), but the satellite ends up slower. Atmospheric drag on a low satellite removes energy, so it spirals inwards and speeds up.

**Escape velocity.** The minimum speed an object needs at the surface to escape to infinity (with no further propulsion). At infinity E_p = 0 and E_k ≥ 0, so at launch ½mv² − GMm/R ≥ 0:

**v_esc = √(2GM/R)** (11.2 km s⁻¹ for the Earth; independent of the mass of the object).

**Synchronous and geostationary orbits.** A synchronous orbit has a period equal to the rotation period of the body it orbits. A geostationary satellite:

- orbits above the equator (equatorial plane);
- has a period of 24 hours (strictly one sidereal day, 23 h 56 min);
- moves west to east, the same direction as the Earth's rotation;
- so it stays above the same point on the Earth.

Radius: r³ = GMT²/4π² → r = 4.2 × 10⁷ m (about 36 000 km above the surface). Uses: communications and TV broadcasting (fixed dishes), some weather satellites. Disadvantages: signal delay (round trip about 0.24 s), poor coverage near the poles.

**Low (polar) orbits.** A few hundred km up, period about 90 minutes. A satellite passing over the poles sees the whole Earth's surface as the Earth turns underneath. Uses: imaging, mapping, weather forecasting, spying, GPS (medium orbits). They are closer, so give higher-resolution images, need less powerful transmitters, but each is only in view for a short time, so many are needed for continuous coverage.

### Worked example

1. Find the orbital speed and period of a satellite 800 km above the Earth.

   r = 7.17 × 10⁶ m. v = √(GM/r) = √(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴/7.17 × 10⁶) = 7.45 × 10³ m s⁻¹. T = 2πr/v = 6050 s (101 min).

2. Show that the radius of a geostationary orbit is about 4.2 × 10⁷ m.

   r³ = GMT²/4π² = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ × (86 400)²/39.48 = 7.53 × 10²² m³. r = 4.22 × 10⁷ m.

3. Jupiter's moon Io orbits at 4.22 × 10⁸ m with a period of 1.77 days. Find the mass of Jupiter.

   T = 1.53 × 10⁵ s. M = 4π²r³/(GT²) = 39.48 × 7.52 × 10²⁵ / (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 2.34 × 10¹⁰) = 1.90 × 10²⁷ kg.

4. Find the escape velocity from the Moon (M = 7.35 × 10²² kg, R = 1.74 × 10⁶ m).

   v = √(2 × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 7.35 × 10²²/1.74 × 10⁶) = 2.37 × 10³ m s⁻¹.

### Common mistakes

- Saying a satellite needs fuel to stay in a circular orbit.
- Saying a geostationary satellite can be above London.
- Using the height above the surface as r.
- Saying escape velocity depends on the mass of the rocket.

# Progressive waves, polarisation and stationary waves

A progressive wave carries energy from place to place; a stationary wave stores it in a fixed pattern. This file covers the language of waves (amplitude, phase, wavelength), transverse and longitudinal waves, the electromagnetic spectrum, polarisation, intensity, and stationary waves on strings, in air columns and with microwaves, including AQA required practical 1.

## Overview

AS & A for every board. Superposition, interference, diffraction and refraction are in file 05. Oscilloscope use for frequency and timing is also in file 32.

## 3.3.1.1 Progressive waves

*Refs: AQA 3.3.1.1 · Edexcel 59, 60, 63, 70 · OCR A 4.4.1(a) to (e), (g), 4.4.2(a), (b) · OCR B 3.1.1(c)(v) · Eduqas C3.1(a), (d) to (h). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- A progressive wave transfers energy (not matter) by oscillations of the particles of a medium, or of electric and magnetic fields.
- Amplitude, frequency, wavelength, speed, period, phase and phase difference.
- c = fλ and f = 1/T.
- Phase difference in degrees, radians or as a fraction of a cycle.
- (Edexcel, OCR A) Intensity I = P/A; intensity is proportional to amplitude².
- (OCR A) The electromagnetic spectrum and orders of magnitude of each region's wavelength.
- (Eduqas) Wavefronts and rays: all points on a wavefront are in phase, and rays are perpendicular to wavefronts.

### Explained

**Definitions.**

| Term | Meaning | Unit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Displacement x | Distance and direction of a point from its equilibrium position | m |
| Amplitude A | Maximum displacement | m |
| Wavelength λ | Distance between two adjacent points oscillating in phase (e.g. crest to crest) | m |
| Period T | Time for one complete oscillation of a point | s |
| Frequency f | Number of complete oscillations per second; f = 1/T | Hz |
| Wave speed c (or v) | Distance travelled by the wave per second | m s⁻¹ |
| Phase | Where a point is in its cycle | rad or ° |
| Phase difference | How far one point's cycle is ahead of or behind another's | rad or ° |

**The wave equation.** In one period the wave moves one wavelength, so c = λ/T = fλ.

**Phase difference.** Two points a distance d apart along a wave have phase difference Δφ = 2πd/λ radians (or 360° × d/λ). In phase: Δφ = 0, 2π, 4π... (d = λ, 2λ...). In antiphase: Δφ = π, 3π... (d = λ/2, 3λ/2...).

**Graphs.**

- Displacement–distance (a snapshot at one instant): shows wavelength and amplitude.
- Displacement–time (one point over time): shows period and amplitude.

Make sure you know which you are reading: the horizontal distance between peaks is λ on one and T on the other.

**Wavefronts and rays.** A wavefront is a line joining points that are in phase (for example, all crests). Rays show the direction of travel and are at right angles to wavefronts.

**Intensity** (Edexcel 70, OCR A 4.4.1(g)). Intensity is the power per unit area at right angles to the direction of travel: I = P/A, in W m⁻². For a point source radiating equally in all directions, I = P/(4πr²), so intensity falls as 1/r². Intensity ∝ amplitude²: doubling the amplitude gives four times the intensity.

**Electromagnetic spectrum** (OCR A 4.4.2(a), (b); Eduqas C3.4(f) also). All EM waves are transverse, travel at c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ in a vacuum, and consist of oscillating electric and magnetic fields at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel.

| Region | Typical wavelength |
| --- | --- |
| Radio | > 10⁻¹ m (up to km) |
| Microwave | 10⁻³ to 10⁻¹ m |
| Infrared | 7 × 10⁻⁷ to 10⁻³ m |
| Visible | 4 × 10⁻⁷ (violet) to 7 × 10⁻⁷ m (red) |
| Ultraviolet | 10⁻⁸ to 4 × 10⁻⁷ m |
| X-ray | 10⁻¹³ to 10⁻⁸ m |
| Gamma | < 10⁻¹⁰ m |

X-rays and gamma overlap: the difference is how they are made (X-rays from decelerating electrons, gamma from nuclei).

**Measuring frequency with an oscilloscope** (OCR A 4.4.1(b)(ii), OCR B 4.1(d)(i)). Measure the horizontal length of several complete cycles in divisions, multiply by the time-base setting (s per division) and divide by the number of cycles to get T; then f = 1/T.

### Worked example

1. A sound wave has frequency 440 Hz and speed 340 m s⁻¹. Find the wavelength and the phase difference between two points 0.20 m apart along the wave.

   λ = 340/440 = 0.773 m. Δφ = 2π × 0.20/0.773 = 1.63 rad (93°).

2. On an oscilloscope with time-base 0.5 ms div⁻¹, three complete waves span 7.5 divisions. Find f.

   3T = 7.5 × 0.5 ms = 3.75 ms, so T = 1.25 ms and f = 800 Hz.

3. A lamp emits 60 W of light uniformly. Find the intensity 2.5 m away.

   I = 60/(4π × 2.5²) = 0.76 W m⁻².

### Common mistakes

- Reading a period off a displacement–distance graph.
- Saying waves transfer matter.
- Giving phase difference in degrees when radians are asked for (or the reverse).
- Forgetting to square amplitude when comparing intensities.

## 3.3.1.2 Longitudinal and transverse waves

*Refs: AQA 3.3.1.2 · Edexcel 61, 62, 82 · OCR A 4.4.1(a), (e), (f), 4.4.2(c) · OCR B 3.1.1(a)(iv), 3.1.1(d)(ii) · Eduqas C3.1(b), (c). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- In a transverse wave the oscillations are at right angles to the direction of energy transfer; in a longitudinal wave they are parallel to it.
- Examples: sound (longitudinal), electromagnetic waves and waves on a string (transverse).
- All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum.
- (Edexcel 61) Longitudinal waves described in terms of pressure variation and the displacement of molecules.
- Polarisation is evidence that a wave is transverse.
- Uses of polarisers: Polaroid material (sunglasses, camera filters) and aligning aerials for transmitting and receiving.

### Explained

**Transverse.** Particles (or fields) oscillate perpendicular to the direction the wave travels. Examples: waves on a string, ripples on water (approximately), all EM waves, S-waves in earthquakes.

**Longitudinal.** Particles oscillate parallel to the direction of travel, forming compressions (high pressure, particles close) and rarefactions (low pressure, particles spread). Examples: sound, P-waves, a slinky pushed back and forth. Longitudinal waves need a medium; EM waves do not.

On a graph of displacement against position for a longitudinal wave, the compressions are where the displacement graph crosses zero going from positive to negative (particles on both sides moving towards that point), assuming displacement to the right is positive. Pressure is a maximum at compressions, which are a quarter of a wavelength from points of maximum displacement.

**Polarisation.** A transverse wave is plane-polarised if its oscillations are in one plane only. Unpolarised light (from a lamp or the Sun) has oscillations in all planes perpendicular to its direction.

- A polarising filter only lets through the component of oscillation along its transmission axis.
- Two filters: rotate the second (the analyser). Transmitted light falls to a minimum (zero) when the two axes are at 90° (crossed), and is maximum when they are parallel. Each full rotation gives two maxima and two minima.
- Longitudinal waves cannot be polarised, since their oscillation is always along the direction of travel. So polarisation shows that light is transverse.

**Malus's law** (Eduqas practical only; not AQA). Transmitted intensity through an analyser at angle θ to the polarised light: I = I₀cos²θ. Measure with a light sensor while rotating the analyser in 10° steps; a graph of I against cos²θ is a straight line through the origin.

**Microwaves** (OCR A 4.4.1(f)(iii), OCR B 3.1.1(d)(ii)). Microwave transmitters emit plane-polarised waves. A metal grille placed between transmitter and receiver blocks the waves when the rods are parallel to the electric field (the field drives currents in the rods, which absorb and reflect the energy), and lets them through when the rods are perpendicular. The receiver signal varies as the receiver itself is rotated.

**Applications.**

- Polaroid sunglasses: light reflected from water or roads is partly horizontally polarised; the vertical transmission axis cuts out the glare.
- TV and radio aerials: broadcast waves are polarised (vertically or horizontally). The receiving aerial rods must be aligned with the transmitter's plane of polarisation for the strongest signal. Neighbouring transmitters use different polarisations to reduce interference.
- Stress analysis: some plastics rotate the plane of polarisation by amounts that depend on stress; between crossed polarisers, coloured fringes show stress concentrations.

### Worked example

1. Unpolarised light of intensity 40 W m⁻² passes through a polariser and then an analyser at 60° to it. Find the final intensity (Eduqas).

   After the first polariser: half the intensity, 20 W m⁻². After the analyser: 20 × cos²60° = 20 × 0.25 = 5.0 W m⁻².

### Common mistakes

- Saying sound can be polarised.
- Saying a polariser "blocks horizontal waves". It transmits the component along its axis.
- Saying compressions are where displacement is greatest.

## 3.3.1.3 Principle of superposition of waves and formation of stationary waves

*Refs: AQA 3.3.1.3, RP1 · Edexcel 63, 64, 67, 68, 69, CP6, CP7 · OCR A 4.4.4(a) to (g) · OCR B 4.1(a)(i), 4.1(c)(i), 4.1(d)(iii), (v) · Eduqas C3.2(n), (o). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- A stationary wave forms when two waves of the same frequency (and similar amplitude) travel in opposite directions and superpose.
- Nodes (zero amplitude) and antinodes (maximum amplitude); adjacent nodes are λ/2 apart.
- Graphical explanation of how a stationary wave forms.
- Stationary waves on strings, with microwaves and with sound (air columns).
- Harmonics on a string; the first harmonic frequency f = (1/2l)√(T/μ).
- (Edexcel 68) Wave speed on a string v = √(T/μ).
- The differences between stationary and progressive waves.
- AQA required practical 1 and Edexcel CP7: how frequency depends on length, tension and mass per unit length. Edexcel CP6: speed of sound with an oscilloscope.

### Explained

**Superposition.** When two waves meet, the resultant displacement at any point is the vector sum of the individual displacements.

**How a stationary wave forms.** A wave travels along a string, reflects at the fixed end and travels back. The incident and reflected waves have the same frequency, speed and (nearly) amplitude. Draw both at several instants (t = 0, T/8, T/4...) and add them: at some points they always cancel (nodes); midway between, they reinforce to give oscillation with twice the amplitude (antinodes). The pattern does not travel.

**Stationary vs progressive.**

| Property | Progressive | Stationary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Energy | Transferred along the wave | Not transferred; stored |
| Amplitude | Same for all points (if no losses) | Varies from zero (nodes) to maximum (antinodes) |
| Phase | Changes continuously along a wavelength | All points between two adjacent nodes are in phase; points either side of a node are in antiphase |
| Wavelength | Distance between adjacent in-phase points | Twice the distance between adjacent nodes |
| Frequency | All points have the same frequency | All points (except nodes) have the same frequency |

**Strings fixed at both ends.** Both ends must be nodes, so a whole number of half-wavelengths fits on the string: l = nλ/2, λ = 2l/n.

- First harmonic (n = 1): one loop, λ = 2l, f₁ = v/2l.
- Second harmonic: two loops, λ = l, f₂ = 2f₁.
- nth harmonic: fₙ = nf₁.

The speed of a wave on a string is v = √(T/μ), where T is tension (N) and μ is mass per unit length (kg m⁻¹). So:

**f₁ = (1/2l)√(T/μ).**

So frequency ∝ 1/l, f ∝ √T and f ∝ 1/√μ. Thicker, heavier strings give lower notes; tightening a guitar string raises its pitch.

**Air columns** (OCR A, OCR B, Eduqas). Sound in a tube forms stationary waves. A closed end is a displacement node (air cannot move); an open end is a displacement antinode.

- Tube closed at one end: first harmonic has l = λ/4. Only odd harmonics: f, 3f, 5f... (l = λ/4, 3λ/4, 5λ/4...)
- Tube open at both ends: antinodes at both ends; l = λ/2 for the first harmonic; all harmonics: f, 2f, 3f...

(End corrections are not required by OCR B; in practice the antinode is slightly beyond the open end.)

**Microwaves.** A microwave transmitter faces a metal plate. The reflected wave superposes with the incident wave. Moving a probe receiver along the line between them shows maxima (antinodes) and minima (nodes) λ/2 apart. Measuring the distance across several nodes gives λ (about 3 cm).

**Resonance tube** (OCR A 4.4.4(e)(ii), OCR B 4.1(d)(v), Eduqas SP). A tuning fork (or speaker at known f) is held over a tube whose effective length can be changed (a tube in a water reservoir, or a sliding tube). Raise the tube until the sound is loudest: first resonance at l₁ ≈ λ/4. Continue to the second resonance at l₂ ≈ 3λ/4. Then λ = 2(l₂ − l₁), which removes the end correction. Speed v = fλ. Repeat with several frequencies and plot l against 1/f; gradient = v/4.

### Worked example

1. A guitar string is 0.65 m long, has μ = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ kg m⁻¹ and tension 70 N. Find the first harmonic frequency.

   v = √(70/4.0 × 10⁻⁴) = √(1.75 × 10⁵) = 418 m s⁻¹. f₁ = v/2l = 418/1.30 = 322 Hz.

2. The tension is doubled. Find the new first harmonic.

   f ∝ √T, so f = 322 × √2 = 455 Hz.

3. A tube closed at one end resonates first at 0.180 m and next at 0.545 m with a 470 Hz fork. Find the speed of sound.

   λ/2 = 0.545 − 0.180 = 0.365 m, so λ = 0.730 m. v = 470 × 0.730 = 343 m s⁻¹.

4. Microwave nodes: 8 nodes span 10.5 cm (7 gaps). Find f.

   λ/2 = 10.5/7 = 1.50 cm, so λ = 3.00 cm. f = 3.00 × 10⁸ / 0.0300 = 1.0 × 10¹⁰ Hz.

### Common mistakes

- Saying adjacent nodes are one wavelength apart.
- Saying all points on a stationary wave have the same amplitude.
- Saying a stationary wave transfers energy along the string.
- Forgetting to convert mass per unit length from g m⁻¹ to kg m⁻¹.
- Using l = λ/2 for a tube closed at one end.

### Practical

**AQA required practical 1** (also Edexcel CP7, OCR B 4.1(d)(iii)): investigate how the frequency of stationary waves on a string depends on length, tension and mass per unit length.

1. Fix one end of a string to a vibration generator driven by a signal generator. Run the string over a pulley to a hanging mass, so tension T = mg. Use a movable bridge near the pulley to set the vibrating length l (vibration generator to bridge).
2. Start at low frequency and increase until the first harmonic (one loop with the largest amplitude) appears. Record f from the signal generator. Check by finding the 2nd harmonic at 2f.
3. Length: vary l with T and μ fixed; plot f against 1/l (straight line through the origin, gradient = v/2).
4. Tension: vary the hanging mass; plot f against √T (or f² against T), straight line, gradient (1/2l)√(1/μ).
5. Mass per unit length: use strings of different μ (measure by weighing a known length, or calculate from diameter and density); plot f against 1/√μ.

Improving accuracy: measure l with a metre rule to the nearest mm; judge the largest-amplitude point by sweeping the frequency up and down; use a strobe to see the nodes clearly. Safety: the hanging masses can fall, so keep feet clear and use a cushion; goggles if the string might snap.

**Edexcel CP6:** speed of sound with a two-beam oscilloscope. A signal generator drives a loudspeaker and also feeds one channel of the oscilloscope. A microphone in front of the speaker feeds the other channel. Move the microphone away from the speaker until the two traces are in phase again; the distance moved is one wavelength. Measure over several wavelengths (move until the traces return into phase n times) for a smaller percentage uncertainty. v = fλ. Alternatively, plot distance against number of wavelengths; gradient = λ.

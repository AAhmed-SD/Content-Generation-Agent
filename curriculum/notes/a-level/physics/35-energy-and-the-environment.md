# Energy and the environment: the Earth's energy balance, energy resources, fuel cells and thermal conduction

This file covers the Eduqas option on energy and the environment: the balance between energy from the Sun and energy radiated by the Earth, the greenhouse effect and carbon dioxide, the solar spectrum, rising sea levels, solar, wind, tidal, hydroelectric and nuclear power, fuel cells, and thermal conduction and insulation (U-values).

## OD(a) The Earth's temperature and energy balance

*Refs: Eduqas OD(a)(i) to (iv). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Thermal equilibrium: the balance between energy arriving from the Sun and energy re-radiated by the Earth, in the context of global energy demand and CO₂ levels.
- The origin and transmission of solar energy; the shape of the Sun's power spectrum; energy converted to near infrared in the atmosphere.
- Wien's law and the Stefan–Boltzmann law in the context of solar power.
- Density and Archimedes' principle to explain sea-level rise: melting ice on land raises sea level; melting icebergs do not.

### Explained

**Energy balance.** The Earth's temperature is steady when the power it absorbs from the Sun equals the power it radiates into space. Absorbed power = (1 − albedo) × I × πR² (the Earth presents a disc of area πR² to the Sun; albedo ≈ 0.3 is the fraction reflected). Radiated power = σT⁴ × 4πR² (the whole surface radiates). Setting these equal with I = 1360 W m⁻²:

T⁴ = (1 − 0.3) × 1360/(4σ) → T ≈ 255 K (−18 °C)

The actual average surface temperature is about 288 K (15 °C). The difference is the greenhouse effect.

**Solar spectrum.** The Sun's energy comes from nuclear fusion in its core and reaches its surface (about 5800 K) by radiation and convection. It radiates like a black body peaking near 500 nm (Wien's law), mostly visible and near infrared, with some UV. This crosses space as electromagnetic radiation. Much of the short-wavelength radiation passes through the atmosphere and is absorbed by the ground, which warms up and re-emits energy at much longer wavelengths (infrared, peaking near 10 μm since T ≈ 288 K).

**Greenhouse effect.** Gases such as CO₂, water vapour and methane are transparent to most incoming visible light but absorb outgoing infrared and re-emit it in all directions, including back down. This keeps the surface warmer than it would otherwise be. Increasing CO₂ (from burning fossil fuels to meet growing global energy demand) absorbs more infrared, so the Earth must warm to a higher temperature before it radiates enough to restore balance.

**Sea levels** (OD(a)(iv)).

- A floating iceberg displaces its own weight of sea water (Archimedes). When it melts, the water it becomes has the same weight, so it fills the same volume it displaced: sea level does not change (ignoring the small difference between fresh and salt water).
- Ice on land (glaciers, Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets) is not displacing any sea water; when it melts and flows into the sea, it adds volume, raising sea level.
- Warming also expands sea water (lower density), raising levels further.

### Worked example

1. If the Earth's albedo fell from 0.30 to 0.28, estimate the new equilibrium temperature (no greenhouse effect).

   T ∝ (1 − albedo)^(1/4): 255 × (0.72/0.70)^(1/4) = 255 × 1.0071 = 256.8 K (about 2 K warmer).

## OD(b) Energy resources

*Refs: Eduqas OD(b)(i) to (iv). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Common renewable and non-renewable energy sources, and their development and use in the UK and worldwide.
- Solar: the proton–proton chain; I = P/A and the inverse-square law; photovoltaic cells and efficiency.
- Wind: power available from a flowing fluid = ½Aρv³; factors affecting turbine efficiency.
- Tidal barrages, hydroelectric power and pumped storage: energy conversion (Ep to Ek) and power calculations; comparison with wind.
- Nuclear: breeding and enrichment in fission; difficulties of fusion power and the fusion triple product.

### Explained

**Renewable** (replenished naturally): solar, wind, hydroelectric, tidal, wave, geothermal, biomass. **Non-renewable**: coal, oil, gas, nuclear fission fuels. In the UK, coal has almost disappeared; gas, wind (especially offshore) and nuclear supply most electricity, with solar growing; worldwide, coal remains large (especially in Asia) but solar and wind are growing fastest. Considerations: cost, reliability (intermittency of wind and solar needs storage or back-up), carbon emissions, land use and environmental impact.

**Solar power.**

- The Sun's main energy source is the proton–proton chain: four protons fuse (in steps) to form a helium-4 nucleus, two positrons and two neutrinos, releasing about 26.7 MeV. Main branch: p + p → ²H + e⁺ + νₑ; ²H + p → ³He + γ; ³He + ³He → ⁴He + 2p.
- Intensity at distance d from a source of power P: I = P/(4πd²) (inverse square). At the Earth, I ≈ 1360 W m⁻² above the atmosphere; about 1000 W m⁻² reaches the ground on a clear day with the Sun overhead.
- Photovoltaic (PV) cells: photons release charge carriers in a semiconductor p–n junction, producing a p.d. and current. Efficiency = electrical power output ÷ solar power falling on the cell (typically 15 to 22%).

**Wind power.** In time t, a column of air of length vt and cross-sectional area A (the swept area of the blades) passes the turbine. Its mass is ρAvt and its kinetic energy ½ρAvt × v². So power available = **½Aρv³**. Doubling the wind speed gives 8 times the power. A turbine cannot extract all of it (the air must keep moving away; the theoretical maximum is 59%, the Betz limit). Efficiency depends on blade design and angle, wind speed (turbines cut out in very strong winds), and losses in gearbox and generator. Large blades sweep a bigger area (A ∝ r²).

**Hydroelectric, tidal and pumped storage.** Water stored at a height h has gravitational potential energy mgh; it flows down through turbines, becoming kinetic energy and then electrical energy. Power = (mass per second) × g × h × efficiency = ρ × (volume flow rate) × g × h × efficiency.

- *Hydroelectric*: water from a high reservoir behind a dam.
- *Tidal barrage*: a dam across an estuary traps water at high tide; it is released through turbines as the tide falls (and sometimes on the way in). Predictable, but only at certain times and harms estuary habitats.
- *Pumped storage* (e.g. Dinorwig in Wales): spare electricity at times of low demand pumps water uphill; at peak demand it flows back down through turbines within seconds. It stores energy (about 75% efficient overall) rather than generating it.

**Nuclear.**

- *Enrichment*: natural uranium is 0.7% U-235 (fissile) and 99.3% U-238. Reactor fuel is enriched to about 3 to 5% U-235 (by separating isotopes, e.g. with centrifuges, which exploit the tiny mass difference).
- *Breeding*: U-238 absorbs a fast neutron to become U-239, which decays (by two β⁻ decays) to plutonium-239, which is fissile. A fast breeder reactor makes more fuel than it uses, extending uranium supplies many times.
- *Fusion* (file 19): needs temperatures above about 10⁸ K so nuclei overcome repulsion, and the plasma must be confined (by magnetic fields in a tokamak) without touching the walls. To produce more energy than is put in, the **triple product** n T τ_E (plasma density × temperature × energy confinement time) must exceed about 3 × 10²¹ keV s m⁻³ (Lawson criterion). Achieving high density, temperature and confinement time together is very hard; materials must survive intense neutron bombardment.

### Worked example

1. A wind turbine with 40 m blades faces a 10 m s⁻¹ wind (ρ = 1.2 kg m⁻³). Find the power in the wind and the output at 40% efficiency.

   A = π × 40² = 5030 m². P = ½ × 5030 × 1.2 × 1000 = 3.0 × 10⁶ W. Output = 1.2 MW.

2. A hydroelectric station has a head of 150 m and a flow of 40 m³ s⁻¹, with 85% efficiency. Find its output.

   P = 1000 × 40 × 9.81 × 150 × 0.85 = 5.0 × 10⁷ W (50 MW).

3. A 1.6 m² solar panel receives 800 W m⁻² and gives 220 W. Find its efficiency.

   Input = 1280 W; efficiency = 0.17.

## OD(c) Fuel cells

*Refs: Eduqas OD(c). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The principles of fuel cell operation and their benefits, particularly for greenhouse gas emissions.

### Explained

A hydrogen fuel cell converts chemical energy directly into electrical energy, without burning. Hydrogen is fed to the anode, where a catalyst (platinum) splits it into protons and electrons: H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻. The protons pass through an electrolyte membrane; the electrons cannot, so they flow through the external circuit as a current. At the cathode, oxygen combines with the protons and electrons to make water: O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O.

Benefits: the only product is water, so there are no CO₂ or other pollutant emissions at the point of use; more efficient (about 40 to 60%) than combustion engines, since there is no heat-engine limit; quiet; can run continuously while fuel is supplied. Drawbacks: hydrogen must be produced (often from natural gas, which does release CO₂, unless made by electrolysis using renewable electricity), stored and transported safely; catalysts are expensive.

## OD(d), (e) Thermal conduction and insulation

*Refs: Eduqas OD(d), (e). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The thermal conduction equation ΔQ/Δt = −AK Δθ/Δx.
- The effect of insulation on energy loss; heat loss through parallel surfaces using rate of transfer = UAΔθ, including different materials in contact.

### Explained

**Conduction.** The rate of energy flow through a slab of material of area A, thickness Δx, with a temperature difference Δθ across it:

ΔQ/Δt = −AK Δθ/Δx

where K is the thermal conductivity of the material (W m⁻¹ K⁻¹) and Δθ/Δx the temperature gradient. The minus sign shows that heat flows from hot to cold (down the temperature gradient). Good conductors (copper, K ≈ 400) have high K; insulators (air, K ≈ 0.025; polystyrene, 0.03; brick, 0.6; glass, 1.0) have low K. Trapped air (in foam, fibre or double glazing) is a good insulator because air conducts poorly and cannot convect when trapped.

**U-values.** For building elements (walls, windows, roofs), rate of energy loss = UAΔθ, where U (W m⁻² K⁻¹) is the U-value: the rate of energy transfer per square metre per kelvin difference. Lower U means better insulation. For a single layer, U = K/thickness.

**Layers in contact.** The same power flows through each layer in series (steady state), and the temperature differences across the layers add. So the "thermal resistances" (thickness/K, or 1/U) add: 1/U_total = 1/U₁ + 1/U₂ + …. For walls and windows side by side (parallel surfaces), the energy losses add: total = (U₁A₁ + U₂A₂)Δθ.

### Worked example

1. A glass window 4.0 mm thick and 1.5 m² in area has 20 °C inside and 5 °C outside surfaces. Find the rate of heat loss (K = 1.0 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹).

   P = 1.5 × 1.0 × 15/0.0040 = 5600 W. (Real windows lose far less because thin air layers at each surface insulate; the glass surfaces are not at the room and outside air temperatures.)

2. A wall of area 12 m² has U = 0.35 W m⁻² K⁻¹; a window of 2.0 m² in it has U = 2.8 W m⁻² K⁻¹. Inside 20 °C, outside 0 °C. Find the total heat loss.

   Wall: 0.35 × 10 × 20 = 70 W (wall area excluding window = 10 m²). Window: 2.8 × 2.0 × 20 = 112 W. Total 182 W.

3. A wall has 0.10 m of brick (K = 0.60) and 0.050 m of insulation (K = 0.030). Find the U-value.

   1/U = 0.10/0.60 + 0.050/0.030 = 0.167 + 1.667 = 1.83; U = 0.55 W m⁻² K⁻¹.

### Common mistakes

- Adding U-values for layers in series (add 1/U).
- Forgetting to subtract the window area from the wall area.
- Saying melting icebergs raise sea levels significantly.
- Using wind power ∝ v² instead of v³.

# Practical skills, working scientifically, and every board's required practicals

All five qualifications test practical skills in the written papers (planning, measuring, analysing and evaluating) and through the separate Practical Endorsement. This file sets out the skills, the standard techniques and instruments, how to analyse data with graphs (including logarithmic graphs), and lists each board's required, core or specified practicals with the file where each is described in full.

## Overview

AS & A for every board. Errors and uncertainties are in file 01; each practical's method is in its topic file (see the tables below).

### Board differences

- AQA: 12 required practicals (RP1 to RP6 in AS); apparatus and techniques ATa to ATk; practical skills PS1 to PS4 (independent thinking, use and application of scientific methods, numeracy and data handling, instruments and equipment).
- Edexcel: 16 core practicals (CP1 to CP8 in AS); Topic 1 statements 2 (practical skills), 5 (communication), 6 (applications, benefits and risks), 7 (the scientific community), 8 (science and decision making).
- OCR A: Module 1: 1.1 (planning, implementing, analysis, evaluation, in the written exam) and 1.2 (the Practical Endorsement skills and apparatus); 12 Practical Activity Groups PAG1 to PAG12.
- OCR B: Module 1 with the same structure (1.1.1 to 1.1.4, 1.2.1, 1.2.2) and its own PAG list; practical activities are listed in each topic's (d) points.
- Eduqas: specified practical work listed in each topic; the Practical Endorsement.
- The Practical Endorsement (CPAC: common practical assessment criteria) is the same for all boards: follow procedures, use apparatus safely, make and record observations, research and reference, and present data.

## Working scientifically and planning

*Refs: Edexcel 2, 5 to 8 · OCR A 1.1.1, 1.2.1(a), (h), (i) · OCR B 1.1.1, 1.2.1 · AQA PS1, PS2. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Designing an experiment: identifying the independent, dependent and control variables; choosing apparatus and ranges; risk assessment.
- Evaluating whether a method is valid (actually tests the hypothesis) and will give precise, accurate results.
- Communicating with correct terminology; citing sources.
- How the scientific community validates new knowledge (peer review, repeat experiments), and how society uses science to make decisions, weighing benefits and risks.

### Explained

**Variables.** Independent: the one you change. Dependent: the one you measure. Control: everything else that could affect the result, kept constant. In the stationary waves experiment (file 04), for example, length is independent, frequency dependent, and tension and mass per unit length controlled.

**A good plan states:**

1. The aim or hypothesis.
2. The apparatus (with a labelled diagram) and why each instrument was chosen (resolution, range).
3. The variables and how controls are kept constant.
4. The range and number of values of the independent variable (at least 6 to 8, evenly spread), and repeats.
5. How each measurement is made, with techniques to reduce uncertainty.
6. How the data will be analysed (which graph, what the gradient or intercept means).
7. Hazards and how risks are reduced.

**Validity.** A valid experiment measures what it claims to and controls other variables. A conclusion is valid only if supported by data with acceptable uncertainty.

**Science and society** (Edexcel 6 to 8). New results are published in journals after peer review (checking by independent experts), then accepted only when others reproduce them. Examples: the discovery of the Higgs boson (two independent detectors), the faster-than-light neutrino claim (2011), withdrawn when a faulty cable was found. Societies use scientific evidence (and its uncertainty) to decide on nuclear power, medical imaging doses, climate policy and mobile phone masts, weighing benefits against risks.

## Implementing: apparatus and techniques

*Refs: AQA ATa to ATk · OCR A 1.1.2, 1.2.1(b) to (g), (j), 1.2.2(a) to (l) · OCR B 1.1.2, 1.2.2 · Edexcel 2. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

The standard techniques that every board expects you to have used.

### Explained

| Technique | Key points |
| --- | --- |
| Metre rule, ruler | Resolution 1 mm; read at eye level to avoid parallax; use a set square or fiducial marker |
| Vernier calipers | Resolution 0.1 mm (or 0.02 mm); internal and external jaws; check zero error |
| Micrometer | Resolution 0.01 mm; use the ratchet so the same pressure is applied; check zero error; measure at several places and orientations |
| Top-pan balance | Resolution 0.01 g or 0.1 g; tare (zero) before use |
| Stopwatch | Resolution 0.01 s, but human reaction time (about 0.1 to 0.2 s) dominates; time many oscillations; use a fiducial marker |
| Light gates and data logger | Measure short times and speeds precisely (no reaction time); speed = length of card ÷ time it blocks the beam |
| Video analysis | Film with a scale in view; frame-by-frame position and time |
| Thermometer / temperature probe | Stir; wait for equilibrium; read at eye level |
| Ammeter, voltmeter, multimeter | Ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel; choose a suitable range; digital meters are nearly ideal |
| Signal generator and oscilloscope | Set frequency; use time-base and Y-gain to measure period and p.d. (file 17) |
| Lasers | Low power; do not look into the beam; beware reflections (file 05) |
| Radioactive sources and GM tube | Measure background; tongs; minimum time; shielding (file 18) |
| Circuits | Build from a diagram; check connections; switch off between readings to avoid heating |
| ICT | Data loggers, spreadsheets for calculations, graphs and modelling (iterative methods) |

**Reducing uncertainty.**

- Measure over larger quantities: time 20 swings, measure across 10 fringes, use a long wire.
- Repeat and average; discard anomalies before averaging, with a reason.
- Choose instruments with finer resolution when the quantity is small.
- Use a graph rather than a single calculation.

**Recording data.** Tables with headings showing quantity and unit (e.g. "t / s"), consistent decimal places matching the instrument's resolution, raw data and processed data in separate columns.

## Analysis

*Refs: OCR A 1.1.3(a) to (d) · OCR B 1.1.3 · AQA PS3 · Edexcel 2. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Processing data with appropriate mathematics and significant figures.
- Plotting graphs: choice and labelling of axes, scales, best-fit lines; finding gradients and intercepts.
- Linearising relationships, including logarithmic plots for exponential and power laws.

### Explained

**Graph skills.**

- Put the independent variable on the x-axis (unless the analysis needs otherwise).
- Choose scales so the data cover at least half the grid in each direction, with easy divisions (1, 2, 5, 10); avoid awkward multiples of 3.
- Plot points as small crosses accurately to half a small square.
- Draw a smooth best-fit line or curve with points scattered evenly either side; do not join dot to dot.
- For a gradient, use a large triangle (at least half the line), and read points on the line, not data points.

**Linearising.** Rearrange the equation into y = mx + c.

| Relationship | Plot | Gradient | Intercept |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T = 2π√(l/g) | T² against l | 4π²/g | 0 |
| V = ε − Ir | V against I | −r | ε |
| f = (1/2l)√(T/μ) | f against 1/l | √(T/μ)/2 | 0 |
| V = V₀e^(−t/RC) | ln V against t | −1/RC | ln V₀ |
| y = kxⁿ (power law) | ln y against ln x (or log y against log x) | n | ln k |
| I = k/x² | 1/√I against x | gradient 1/√k | gives offset |

**Logarithmic graphs.** For an exponential y = Ae^(−kx), ln y = ln A − kx (straight line). For a power law y = kxⁿ, log y = log k + n log x; the gradient gives the power n. Log scales also show data that range over many orders of magnitude (OCR B 5.1.3(b)(i)).

**Significant figures.** Give results to the same number of significant figures as the least precise data (file 01).

## Evaluation

*Refs: OCR A 1.1.4(a) to (e) · OCR B 1.1.4 · AQA PS2, PS4. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Drawing conclusions from results; identifying anomalies; limitations of the procedure; precision and accuracy; percentage error and uncertainty; suggesting improvements.

### Explained

- **Conclusion**: state what the graph shows (e.g. "T² is directly proportional to l, since the line is straight and passes through the origin within the uncertainties"), and give the derived value with its uncertainty.
- **Compare with an accepted value**: percentage difference = |experimental − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100%. If this is smaller than the percentage uncertainty, the result agrees with the accepted value.
- **Anomalies**: points far from the line; suggest a cause and repeat them.
- **Limitations and improvements**: identify the largest source of uncertainty and suggest a specific, practical improvement (e.g. "use a light gate instead of a stopwatch to remove reaction-time errors"), not "be more careful".
- **Systematic errors**: a non-zero intercept where theory predicts zero suggests one (zero error, end correction).

## Check questions

1. A student times 20 swings of a pendulum with a stopwatch. Explain why 20 swings are timed and where the fiducial marker should be placed. (3 marks)
2. Explain the difference between a valid experiment and a reliable (repeatable) one. (2 marks)
3. The relationship between two quantities is believed to be y = kxⁿ. Describe how a graph can be used to find n and k. (3 marks)
4. A student's value for g is 9.62 m s⁻² with a percentage uncertainty of 3%. Does it agree with 9.81 m s⁻²? Explain. (2 marks)
5. Identify the independent, dependent and control variables in AQA required practical 5 (resistivity of a wire). (3 marks)
6. Suggest why a micrometer, not a ruler, is used to measure the diameter of a wire. (2 marks)

## Mark scheme

1. Reduces the percentage uncertainty in the time, since reaction-time error is the same for 1 or 20 swings (1); fiducial marker at the centre / equilibrium position (1); where the bob moves fastest, so the moment of passing is clearest (1).
2. Valid: the experiment measures what it is meant to / controls other variables (1); repeatable: similar results obtained when repeated by the same method (1).
3. Plot log y against log x (1); gradient = n (1); intercept = log k, so k = 10^intercept (1).
4. Percentage difference = (9.81 − 9.62)/9.81 = 1.9% (1); less than the 3% uncertainty, so it agrees (1).
5. Independent: length of wire between contacts (1); dependent: resistance (from V and I) (1); control: same wire / diameter / material / temperature (low current) (1).
6. Diameter is very small (under 1 mm) (1); micrometer resolution 0.01 mm gives much smaller percentage uncertainty than a ruler's 1 mm (1).

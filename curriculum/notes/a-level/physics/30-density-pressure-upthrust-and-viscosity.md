# Fluids: density, pressure, upthrust, drag, viscosity and Stokes' law

Fluids (liquids and gases) push on things inside them and resist things moving through them. This file covers density and how to measure it, pressure (including pressure in a fluid, p = hρg), upthrust and Archimedes' principle, drag, viscosity, laminar and turbulent flow, Stokes' law and the falling-ball method for viscosity (Edexcel core practical 4).

## Density

*Refs: Edexcel 49 · OCR A 3.2.4(a) · Eduqas C1.1(g) · AQA 3.4.2.1. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- ρ = m/V, unit kg m⁻³.
- How to measure the density of regular and irregular solids and of liquids.

### Explained

Density is mass per unit volume: ρ = m/V. 1 g cm⁻³ = 1000 kg m⁻³. Water: 1000 kg m⁻³. Air: about 1.2 kg m⁻³. Aluminium: 2700 kg m⁻³. Iron: 7900 kg m⁻³.

### Practical

**Density of solids** (Eduqas specified practical). Regular solid: measure dimensions with vernier calipers (or a micrometer for small dimensions) at several places, calculate volume; measure mass on a top-pan balance. Irregular solid: lower it into a displacement (eureka) can full to the spout, and collect and measure the displaced water in a measuring cylinder; or use the difference in readings of a measuring cylinder before and after. Combine percentage uncertainties (file 01).

### Worked example

1. A brass cylinder has diameter 2.00 cm, length 5.00 cm and mass 133 g. Find its density.

   V = π × 1.00² × 5.00 = 15.7 cm³. ρ = 133/15.7 = 8.47 g cm⁻³ = 8470 kg m⁻³.

## Pressure and upthrust

*Refs: Edexcel 50 · OCR A 3.2.1(d), 3.2.4(b), (c). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Pressure p = F/A for solids, liquids and gases. Unit Pa (N m⁻²).
- Pressure in a fluid at depth h: p = hρg.
- Upthrust on an object in a fluid; Archimedes' principle: upthrust = weight of fluid displaced.

### Explained

**Pressure** is force per unit area, acting perpendicular to a surface. In a fluid at rest, pressure acts equally in all directions at a given depth.

**Pressure at depth.** Consider a column of fluid of height h and cross-sectional area A. Its weight is mg = ρVg = ρAhg, which acts on area A, so the pressure due to the fluid is p = hρg. This adds to any pressure at the surface (atmospheric pressure, about 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa): total pressure = p_atm + hρg. Pressure depends on depth and density only, not on the shape of the container.

**Upthrust.** An object in a fluid has greater pressure on its bottom (deeper) than on its top, so there is a net upward force: upthrust. For a block of height h and horizontal area A: upthrust = (p_bottom − p_top)A = hρ_fluid gA = ρ_fluid V g = weight of fluid displaced. This is **Archimedes' principle**: the upthrust on an object in a fluid equals the weight of fluid it displaces.

**Floating and sinking.** If the object's weight is greater than the maximum upthrust (when fully submerged), it sinks: this happens when its density is greater than the fluid's. A floating object sinks until the upthrust equals its weight: it displaces its own weight of fluid.

### Worked example

1. Find the pressure 25 m below the surface of the sea (ρ = 1030 kg m⁻³).

   Fluid pressure = 25 × 1030 × 9.81 = 2.53 × 10⁵ Pa. Total = 3.5 × 10⁵ Pa (with atmospheric pressure).

2. A 0.40 kg steel ball (ρ = 7900 kg m⁻³) hangs from a newtonmeter, fully submerged in water. Find the reading.

   V = 0.40/7900 = 5.06 × 10⁻⁵ m³. Upthrust = 1000 × 5.06 × 10⁻⁵ × 9.81 = 0.50 N. Weight = 3.92 N. Reading = 3.43 N.

3. A wooden block (ρ = 600 kg m⁻³) floats in water. What fraction is under water?

   Weight of block = weight of water displaced: ρ_wood V g = ρ_water V_sub g; V_sub/V = 0.60.

### Common mistakes

- Using the object's density in the upthrust formula.
- Forgetting atmospheric pressure when the total pressure is asked for.

## Drag, viscosity and Stokes' law

*Refs: Edexcel 51(a), 51(b), 52, CP4 · OCR A 3.2.2(a), (b) · OCR B 4.2(d)(iii) · AQA 3.12.1.4. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Drag is the frictional (resistive) force on an object moving through a fluid; factors affecting drag (OCR A).
- Viscous drag on a small sphere: F = 6πηrv (Stokes' law), where η is the viscosity.
- Stokes' law applies only to small spheres moving slowly with laminar flow (no turbulence); viscosity depends on temperature.
- Edexcel CP4: falling-ball method for viscosity.

### Explained

**Viscosity** η is a measure of a fluid's resistance to flow (its "thickness"). Unit Pa s (N s m⁻²). Water: about 1 × 10⁻³ Pa s; glycerol: about 1.4 Pa s; air: 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ Pa s. The viscosity of liquids falls sharply as temperature rises (warm honey flows more easily); for gases it rises slightly.

**Laminar and turbulent flow.** In laminar (streamline) flow, layers of fluid slide smoothly past each other; streamlines are smooth and do not cross; velocity at any point is steady. In turbulent flow, the fluid swirls in eddies; streamlines mix; drag is much larger. Flow becomes turbulent at higher speeds, around larger objects and around non-streamlined shapes.

**Drag in air** (OCR A 3.2.2(b)). Drag increases with speed (roughly ∝ v² for fast objects), with cross-sectional area, with the density of the fluid, and depends on the shape (streamlining reduces it) and surface roughness.

**Stokes' law.** For a small sphere of radius r moving at speed v through a fluid of viscosity η, with laminar flow: **F = 6πηrv**.

**Terminal velocity of a sphere in a liquid.** Three forces: weight W = (4/3)πr³ρ_s g (down), upthrust U = (4/3)πr³ρ_f g (up), viscous drag F = 6πηrv (up). At terminal velocity: W = U + F, so

6πηrv_t = (4/3)πr³(ρ_s − ρ_f)g → **v_t = 2r²(ρ_s − ρ_f)g/(9η)**

Terminal velocity increases with r² (bigger balls fall faster) and is larger for denser balls and less viscous liquids.

### Worked example

1. A steel ball (r = 1.0 mm, ρ = 7800 kg m⁻³) falls through glycerol (ρ = 1260 kg m⁻³) at a terminal speed of 1.0 × 10⁻² m s⁻¹. Find the viscosity.

   η = 2r²(ρ_s − ρ_f)g/(9v) = 2 × (1.0 × 10⁻³)² × 6540 × 9.81/(9 × 0.010) = 1.43 Pa s.

2. Find the drag on a 0.50 mm radius sphere moving at 2.0 cm s⁻¹ through water (η = 1.0 × 10⁻³ Pa s).

   F = 6π × 1.0 × 10⁻³ × 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.020 = 1.9 × 10⁻⁷ N.

### Common mistakes

- Using the diameter as r.
- Applying Stokes' law to large or fast objects (turbulent flow).
- Ignoring upthrust when finding viscosity.

### Practical

**Edexcel CP4** (also OCR B 4.2(d)(iii), OCR A PAG1): falling-ball method for the viscosity of a liquid.

1. Fill a tall measuring cylinder (or tube) with the liquid (e.g. glycerol or washing-up liquid). Put rubber bands round it at measured intervals, starting well below the surface so the ball has reached terminal velocity.
2. Measure the diameter of a small steel ball with a micrometer; find its mass on a balance (or use a known density). Measure the liquid's density (mass of a known volume).
3. Drop the ball gently at the centre of the surface. Time it between bands (using a stopwatch, or a video with a ruler in shot). Check terminal velocity has been reached: equal times for equal distances.
4. Repeat with several balls; use different radii and plot v_t against r² (straight line through the origin, gradient 2(ρ_s − ρ_f)g/(9η)).
5. Record the temperature, since η depends strongly on it.

Errors: parallax when judging when the ball passes a band (view at eye level); the walls of the tube increase drag (use a wide tube, drop at the centre); reaction time (use video analysis). Safety: clean spills (slippery), retrieve balls with a magnet.

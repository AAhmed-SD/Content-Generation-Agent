# Photons, the photoelectric effect, energy levels and wave–particle duality

Light sometimes behaves as a wave and sometimes as a stream of particles (photons); electrons sometimes behave as particles and sometimes as waves. This file covers the evidence for each: the photoelectric effect, excitation and ionisation, line spectra and energy levels, and electron diffraction with the de Broglie wavelength.

## 3.2.2.1 The photoelectric effect

*Refs: AQA 3.2.2.1 · Edexcel 90 to 95 · OCR A 4.5.1(a) to (e), 4.5.2(a) to (f) · OCR B 4.1(a)(vi), 4.1(b)(i), 4.1(c)(iv), 4.1(d)(vi) · Eduqas C3.4(a) to (g). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- When light of high enough frequency shines on a metal surface, electrons (photoelectrons) are emitted.
- There is a threshold frequency below which no electrons are emitted, however bright the light. Only the photon model explains this.
- The work function φ is the minimum energy needed to free an electron from the surface.
- Einstein's photoelectric equation: hf = φ + Ek(max).
- Stopping potential: the p.d. that just stops the most energetic photoelectrons, so eVs = Ek(max).
- (Edexcel, OCR A, Eduqas) The rate of emission is proportional to intensity; Ek(max) does not depend on intensity. A graph of Ek(max) against f is a straight line, gradient h, x-intercept f₀, y-intercept −φ.
- (OCR A, OCR B, Eduqas) Planck constant from LEDs.

### Explained

**Photons.** The energy of a photon is E = hf = hc/λ. A photon is a quantum (a discrete packet) of electromagnetic energy. Each photon interacts with one electron, all at once (one-to-one).

**Observations that the wave model cannot explain.**

1. Below a threshold frequency f₀ no electrons are emitted, whatever the intensity. Wave model: any frequency should work if you wait long enough for the energy to build up.
2. Above f₀, emission starts at once, even in very dim light. Wave model: there should be a delay.
3. The maximum kinetic energy of the electrons depends on frequency, not on intensity. Wave model: brighter light should give faster electrons.
4. The number of electrons emitted per second is proportional to intensity.

**Photon explanation.** Each electron absorbs one whole photon. If hf < φ, no single photon has enough energy to free an electron, and electrons cannot save up energy from several photons, so nothing is emitted. If hf ≥ φ, the electron escapes immediately, and its energy left over is kinetic. Electrons from deeper in the metal lose some energy on the way out, so hf − φ is the maximum kinetic energy, not every electron's kinetic energy. A brighter beam has more photons per second, so more electrons per second, but each photon still has the same energy.

**Equations.**

- hf = φ + Ek(max)
- At threshold, Ek(max) = 0, so φ = hf₀ and f₀ = φ/h.
- Ek(max) = eVs, where Vs is the stopping potential.

**The Ek(max)–f graph.** Ek(max) = hf − φ has the form y = mx + c. Gradient = h for every metal. Intercept on the f-axis = f₀. Intercept on the Ek axis (extrapolated) = −φ. Different metals give parallel lines.

**Measuring Ek(max)** (Eduqas C3.4(c)). In a vacuum photocell, light falls on a curved metal cathode; a central wire anode collects electrons. Make the anode negative relative to the cathode and increase the reverse p.d. until the current (on a sensitive meter) falls to zero. That p.d. is Vs and Ek(max) = eVs (in eV, numerically equal to Vs in volts).

**Gold-leaf electroscope demonstration.** A negatively charged zinc plate on an electroscope: UV light makes the leaf fall (electrons emitted); bright visible light does nothing, because visible photons have less energy than the work function of zinc.

**Planck constant using LEDs** (OCR A PAG6, OCR B, Eduqas). An LED only lights when the p.d. across it reaches a threshold V₀ at which each electron crossing the junction has enough energy to make one photon: eV₀ ≈ hc/λ. Measure V₀ for several LEDs of known wavelength (increase p.d. slowly until the LED just glows, viewed through a tube to block room light, or find where the I–V graph starts to rise). Plot V₀ against 1/λ: gradient = hc/e, so h = gradient × e / c.

### Worked example

1. Light of wavelength 250 nm falls on a metal with work function 3.2 eV. Find Ek(max) and the stopping potential.

   E = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(250 × 10⁻⁹) = 7.96 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 4.97 eV. Ek(max) = 4.97 − 3.2 = 1.77 eV = 2.8 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Stopping potential = 1.8 V.

2. Find the threshold wavelength for that metal.

   φ = 3.2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 5.12 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. λ₀ = hc/φ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ / 5.12 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.9 × 10⁻⁷ m (388 nm, violet).

3. An LED of wavelength 630 nm just lights at 1.95 V. Estimate h.

   h = eV₀λ/c = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1.95 × 630 × 10⁻⁹ / 3.00 × 10⁸ = 6.6 × 10⁻³⁴ J s.

4. A 2.0 mW laser emits light of wavelength 532 nm. How many photons does it emit per second?

   E per photon = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ / 532 × 10⁻⁹ = 3.74 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. N = P/E = 2.0 × 10⁻³ / 3.74 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 5.3 × 10¹⁵ s⁻¹.

### Common mistakes

- Saying brighter light gives electrons more energy.
- Saying every photoelectron has kinetic energy hf − φ; that is the maximum.
- Saying electrons absorb several photons to build up energy.
- Forgetting to convert eV to J (or the reverse) before combining with hf.
- Calling the threshold frequency "the frequency of the electrons".

## 3.2.2.2 Collisions of electrons with atoms

*Refs: AQA 3.2.2.2 · Edexcel 94 · OCR A 4.5.1(d) · Eduqas C3.4(k). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Ionisation: an electron is removed from an atom.
- Excitation: an electron moves to a higher energy level in the atom.
- How a fluorescent tube works in terms of excitation and ionisation.
- The electronvolt and how to convert between eV and J.

### Explained

**The electronvolt.** 1 eV is the energy transferred when one electron moves through a p.d. of 1 V: 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. An electron accelerated through V volts gains eV joules, or V electronvolts, of kinetic energy.

**Excitation.** A free electron (or a photon) can give energy to an orbital electron. If the energy transferred exactly matches the gap between two levels, the orbital electron moves up: the atom is excited. A colliding free electron can give only part of its energy and carry on with the rest; a photon must be absorbed whole and must have exactly the right energy.

**Ionisation.** If an orbital electron receives at least the ionisation energy, it leaves the atom completely, and the atom becomes a positive ion. The ionisation energy of hydrogen in its ground state is 13.6 eV.

**De-excitation.** Excited atoms are unstable. The electron drops back to a lower level, emitting a photon of energy equal to the difference between the levels. It may drop in one step or several.

**Fluorescent tube.**

1. A high p.d. across the tube accelerates free electrons through low-pressure mercury vapour.
2. The fast electrons collide with mercury atoms, exciting (and ionising) them. Ionisation releases more free electrons, keeping the current going.
3. The excited mercury atoms de-excite, emitting mostly ultraviolet photons.
4. The UV photons are absorbed by the phosphor coating on the inside of the glass, exciting its atoms to high levels.
5. The phosphor atoms de-excite in several smaller steps, emitting lower-energy visible photons. This is fluorescence.

### Worked example

1. An electron is accelerated through 12 V and hits a hydrogen atom in its ground state (−13.6 eV). Levels are −13.6, −3.4, −1.5 and −0.85 eV. Which excitations are possible?

   The electron has 12 eV. −13.6 → −3.4 needs 10.2 eV: possible. −13.6 → −1.5 needs 12.1 eV: not possible. So only n = 1 to n = 2. The electron leaves with 12 − 10.2 = 1.8 eV.

2. Convert 5.0 × 10⁻¹⁸ J to eV.

   5.0 × 10⁻¹⁸ / 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 31 eV.

### Common mistakes

- Saying a photon can give part of its energy to an atom (in excitation it must be absorbed whole).
- Saying mercury atoms emit visible light directly in a fluorescent tube.
- Treating the electronvolt as a unit of p.d.

## 3.2.2.3 Energy levels and photon emission

*Refs: AQA 3.2.2.3 · Edexcel 96 · OCR A 5.5.2(a) to (f) · OCR B 4.1(a)(vi), 6.2.1(a)(iii) · Eduqas C3.4(h), (i), (j), (k). Level: AS & A (OCR A 5.5.2: A-level only).*

### What you need to know

- Line spectra (such as that of atomic hydrogen) are evidence that electrons in atoms occupy discrete energy levels.
- The photon emitted or absorbed in a transition has hf = E₁ − E₂.
- Energy levels may be given in J or eV, and are negative (zero is the energy of a free electron at rest).
- (OCR A, Eduqas) Emission and absorption line spectra and continuous spectra, and how each is produced; seeing them with a diffraction grating; each element has its own pattern of lines.
- (Eduqas) Finding ionisation energy from an energy level diagram.

### Explained

**Energy level diagrams.** Electrons in an atom can only have certain energies. The lowest is the ground state (n = 1). Levels are negative because energy must be supplied to free the electron; the ionisation level is 0. Higher levels crowd together towards 0.

**Emission.** An electron dropping from E₁ to a lower E₂ emits one photon: hf = E₁ − E₂. Since only certain gaps exist, only certain frequencies appear: an emission line spectrum of bright lines on a dark background. Made by a hot, low-pressure gas, or a gas discharge tube.

**Absorption.** White light (a continuous spectrum) passes through a cool gas. Photons with exactly the right energy to lift electrons between levels are absorbed. The atoms re-emit in all directions, so those wavelengths are missing in the original direction: dark lines on a continuous spectrum. The dark lines are at the same wavelengths as the bright emission lines of the same element. Dark lines in the Sun's spectrum show which elements are in its outer layers (see file 20).

**Continuous spectrum.** A hot dense object (a filament, a star's surface) emits all wavelengths.

**Seeing spectra.** A diffraction grating spreads light into its wavelengths: each line in the source appears as a separate bright line in each order (dsin θ = nλ, file 05).

**Each element is different.** The set of levels is unique to each element, so its line spectrum is like a fingerprint.

**Hydrogen.** Transitions down to n = 2 give visible lines (the Balmer series); down to n = 1 give ultraviolet (Lyman); down to n = 3 give infrared.

**Number of lines.** With N levels available, the number of possible downward transitions is N(N − 1)/2.

**Electron standing waves** (OCR B 6.2.1(a)(iv), (b)(iii)). In a simple model, an electron confined in an atom behaves like a wave trapped in a box. Only whole numbers of half-wavelengths fit: L = nλ/2. Since λ = h/p and Ek = p²/2m, Ek = n²h²/(8mL²). Only certain energies are allowed, which explains discrete levels. A smaller box gives larger energies.

### Worked example

1. In hydrogen, n = 3 is at −1.51 eV and n = 2 at −3.40 eV. Find the wavelength of the photon emitted when an electron falls from n = 3 to n = 2.

   ΔE = 1.89 eV = 3.02 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. λ = hc/ΔE = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ / 3.02 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 6.58 × 10⁻⁷ m (656 nm, red).

2. An atom has four levels. How many emission lines can it produce?

   4 × 3 / 2 = 6.

3. (Eduqas) The ground state of an atom is −10.4 eV. What is its ionisation energy in J?

   10.4 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.66 × 10⁻¹⁸ J.

4. (OCR B) Estimate the lowest energy of an electron confined in a box of length 0.10 nm.

   E = h²/(8mL²) = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴)² / (8 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × (1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰)²) = 6.0 × 10⁻¹⁸ J ≈ 38 eV.

### Common mistakes

- Using E₁ + E₂ instead of the difference.
- Forgetting the negative signs and getting a negative photon energy; use the magnitude of the difference.
- Saying absorption lines are dark because no light reaches the detector at all; some re-emitted light does, but much less than the background.
- Saying the photon energy depends on the level the electron started in only.

## 3.2.2.4 Wave–particle duality

*Refs: AQA 3.2.2.4 · Edexcel 86, 87, 90, 95 · OCR A 4.5.3(a) to (c) · OCR B 4.1(a)(vii), (viii), 4.1(c)(v) · Eduqas C3.4(l), (m), (n). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Electron diffraction shows that particles have wave properties; the photoelectric effect shows that electromagnetic waves have particle properties.
- The de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p = h/mv.
- How and why the amount of diffraction changes when the momentum of the particles changes.
- Our understanding of the nature of matter has changed over time, and changes are checked by peer review and accepted by the scientific community.
- (OCR B) Quantum behaviour: the probability of a photon or electron arriving somewhere is found by adding phasors for every possible path.
- (Eduqas) p = h/λ applies to photons as well; radiation pressure.

### Explained

**Particles as waves.** De Broglie suggested that any particle with momentum p has a wavelength λ = h/p. For a non-relativistic particle, p = mv and Ek = p²/2m, so λ = h/√(2mEk). For an electron accelerated through V: Ek = eV, so λ = h/√(2meV).

**Electron diffraction.** In an electron diffraction tube, electrons from a hot filament are accelerated through a few kV and pass through a thin film of graphite (polycrystalline carbon). A pattern of concentric rings appears on the fluorescent screen. Rings are a diffraction pattern: the atoms in the crystal act like a grating, and the spacing between atoms (about 10⁻¹⁰ m) is similar to the electron wavelength. This shows electrons behave as waves.

- Increase the accelerating p.d.: electrons move faster, p rises, λ falls, so the rings get smaller (less diffraction).
- Diffraction is only noticeable when λ is similar to the gap or object size. A ball has a de Broglie wavelength of around 10⁻³⁴ m, far too small for any effect.

**Light as particles.** The photoelectric effect (one photon, one electron, threshold frequency) needs the photon model. Interference and diffraction need the wave model. Neither model alone explains everything; each is used where it works.

**How ideas change.** Newton's particle model of light was replaced by Young's and Maxwell's wave model, which was then shown to be incomplete by the photoelectric effect. De Broglie's idea of matter waves (1924) was confirmed by electron diffraction experiments (1927). New ideas are published, checked by other scientists, and accepted only when experiments by independent groups agree.

**Photon momentum** (Eduqas C3.4(m), (n)). A photon has no rest mass but has momentum p = h/λ = E/c. When light is absorbed by a surface, its momentum is transferred, so it exerts a force: for a beam of power P absorbed completely, F = P/c; if reflected, F = 2P/c. Radiation pressure = F/A = I/c (absorbed) or 2I/c (reflected), where I is intensity. Solar sails use this.

**Quantum behaviour and phasors** (OCR B 4.1(a)(vii)). A quantum object (photon, electron) does not travel along one path. To predict where it arrives:

1. Consider every possible path from source to detector.
2. For each path, a phasor (a rotating arrow) turns at the frequency f of the quantum; for a photon the phasor makes one full turn per wavelength travelled, so the number of turns along a path = path length ÷ λ.
3. Add the final phasors for all paths tip to tail.
4. The probability of arrival is proportional to the square of the resultant amplitude.

Paths near the straight-line (or least-time) path have phasors that point the same way and add up; paths far from it have phasors pointing in all directions that cancel. This explains why light appears to travel in straight lines, reflects at equal angles and refracts, and it explains two-slit interference: the two groups of paths through the slits add (bright) when the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths and cancel (dark) when it is a half-number. For electrons, the phasor frequency is set by the electron's energy and the wavelength by λ = h/p.

### Worked example

1. Find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through 3.0 kV.

   Ek = 3000 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 4.80 × 10⁻¹⁶ J. p = √(2mEk) = √(2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 4.80 × 10⁻¹⁶) = 2.96 × 10⁻²³ kg m s⁻¹. λ = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ / 2.96 × 10⁻²³ = 2.24 × 10⁻¹¹ m.

2. A proton and an electron have the same de Broglie wavelength. Compare their speeds.

   λ = h/mv is the same, so mv is the same. v_e/v_p = m_p/m_e = 1836. The electron is 1836 times faster.

3. Find the speed of a neutron with wavelength 0.15 nm (useful for diffracting from crystals).

   v = h/(mλ) = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ / (1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ × 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 2.6 × 10³ m s⁻¹.

4. (Eduqas) A 1.0 kW beam falls on a perfectly reflecting surface. Find the force on it.

   F = 2P/c = 2000 / 3.00 × 10⁸ = 6.7 × 10⁻⁶ N.

### Common mistakes

- Using the rest mass in place of momentum for a photon; for a photon use p = h/λ.
- Saying faster electrons give bigger rings (they give smaller rings).
- Saying electron diffraction shows electrons are only waves.
- Forgetting to square-root when finding p from Ek.

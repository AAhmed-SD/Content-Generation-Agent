# Special relativity: Michelson–Morley, Einstein's postulates, time dilation, length contraction and relativistic energy

At speeds close to the speed of light, time, length and energy behave in ways that seem strange but have been tested precisely. This file covers the Michelson–Morley experiment, inertial frames and Einstein's two postulates, proper time and time dilation (with muon evidence), length contraction, and relativistic mass and energy, including Bertozzi's experiment and the relativistic factor γ.

## 3.12.3.1 The Michelson–Morley experiment

*Refs: AQA 3.12.3.1. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The principle of the Michelson–Morley interferometer.
- The experiment as an attempt to detect absolute motion (through the "aether").
- The significance of the null result: the invariance of the speed of light.

### Explained

In the 1800s, physicists believed light waves needed a medium, the aether, filling all space and at absolute rest. The Earth moves through it at about 30 km s⁻¹, so light travelling along the Earth's motion and across it should take slightly different times.

**Interferometer.** Light from a source hits a half-silvered mirror (beam splitter), which sends half the light along one arm to a mirror and half along a perpendicular arm to another mirror. Both beams return to the beam splitter and combine in a telescope, where they produce interference fringes. If the travel time along one arm changes relative to the other, the fringes shift.

**The experiment.** The apparatus was floated on mercury so it could be rotated smoothly through 90°. If the Earth moved through the aether, rotating it would swap which arm was along the motion, and the fringes should shift by about 0.4 of a fringe (calculated for their arm length). They could detect a shift of 0.01 fringe.

**Result.** No significant shift was found at any orientation or time of year (a "null result").

**Significance.** The Earth's absolute motion through an aether could not be detected. Either the aether does not exist or something else was going on. Einstein took the result to mean that there is no absolute rest frame and the speed of light is the same for all observers: the invariance of the speed of light.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the experiment measured the speed of light.

## 3.12.3.2 Einstein's theory of special relativity

*Refs: AQA 3.12.3.2 · OCR B 5.1.3(a)(i). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The concept of an inertial frame of reference.
- The two postulates: (1) physical laws have the same form in all inertial frames; (2) the speed of light in free space is invariant.

### Explained

**Inertial frame.** A frame of reference (a set of coordinates and clocks) that is not accelerating: an object with no resultant force on it moves at constant velocity (Newton's first law holds). A train moving at constant velocity is (approximately) an inertial frame; a train braking is not.

**Postulates.**

1. The laws of physics have the same form in all inertial frames. No experiment done inside a closed, non-accelerating box can tell whether the box is "really" moving.
2. The speed of light in free space, c, is the same for all inertial observers, whatever the motion of the source or observer.

These lead to the conclusion that time intervals and lengths depend on the observer's frame. Because c is invariant, distance can be defined in terms of the time light takes to travel it (OCR B: radar ranging).

### Common mistakes

- Saying a frame moving at constant velocity is not inertial.
- Saying the speed of light depends on the speed of the source.

## 3.12.3.3 Time dilation

*Refs: AQA 3.12.3.3 · Edexcel 139 · OCR B 5.1.3(a)(ii). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Proper time, and time dilation as a consequence of special relativity.
- t = t₀/√(1 − v²/c²).
- Evidence from muon decay.
- (OCR B) The relativistic factor γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²).

### Explained

**Proper time** t₀ is the time between two events measured by an observer for whom both events happen at the same place (for example, a clock moving with a particle, measuring its lifetime).

An observer who sees that clock moving at speed v measures a longer time between the same events:

**t = t₀/√(1 − v²/c²) = γt₀**

Moving clocks run slow. γ ≥ 1, and γ ≈ 1 at everyday speeds; it becomes large as v approaches c.

**Light clock argument.** A pulse of light bounces between two mirrors a distance L apart. In the clock's frame each tick takes t₀ = 2L/c. To an observer watching the clock move sideways at v, the light travels a longer diagonal path; since c is the same for them, each tick takes longer: (ct/2)² = L² + (vt/2)², which gives t = t₀/√(1 − v²/c²).

**Evidence: muons.** Muons are made by cosmic rays hitting the upper atmosphere, about 10 km up, and travel at about 0.99c. Their half-life (at rest) is 1.5 μs. Without relativity, in 1.5 μs they travel about 450 m, so after 10 km (about 22 half-lives) only a tiny fraction (about 10⁻⁷) would survive. In fact, a large fraction reaches the ground. In the Earth's frame their half-life is dilated by γ ≈ 7 to about 10.6 μs, so far more survive. Experiments measuring muon counts at the top of a mountain and at sea level confirm the relativistic prediction. Unstable particles in accelerators also live longer by γ (Edexcel 139).

### Worked example

1. A muon (half-life at rest 1.53 μs) moves at 0.995c. Find its half-life in the Earth's frame, and the distance it travels in one half-life.

   γ = 1/√(1 − 0.995²) = 10.0. t = 15.3 μs. Distance = 0.995 × 3.0 × 10⁸ × 15.3 × 10⁻⁶ = 4.6 km.

2. Muons travel 2000 m at 0.98c. What fraction survive? (half-life at rest 1.53 μs)

   Time in Earth frame = 2000/(0.98 × 3.0 × 10⁸) = 6.80 μs. γ = 5.03; dilated half-life = 7.69 μs. Fraction = (½)^(6.80/7.69) = 0.54. (Without relativity: (½)^(4.44) = 0.046.)

### Common mistakes

- Using the dilated time as the proper time.
- Forgetting to square v/c.

## 3.12.3.4 Length contraction

*Refs: AQA 3.12.3.4. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The length of an object moving at speed v: l = l₀√(1 − v²/c²).

### Explained

The proper length l₀ of an object is its length measured in its own rest frame. An observer for whom the object moves at speed v along its length measures it shorter: l = l₀√(1 − v²/c²) = l₀/γ. Only the dimension along the motion contracts.

Muons again: in the muon's frame, it is at rest and the atmosphere rushes towards it at 0.99c, so the 10 km of atmosphere is contracted to about 1.4 km, which it can cover within its (undilated) half-life. Both frames agree on how many muons reach the ground.

### Worked example

1. A spacecraft 80 m long (proper length) passes the Earth at 0.60c. How long does it appear to Earth observers?

   l = 80 × √(1 − 0.36) = 80 × 0.80 = 64 m.

### Common mistakes

- Contracting the dimension perpendicular to the motion.

## 3.12.3.5 Mass and energy

*Refs: AQA 3.12.3.5 · OCR B 6.2.1(a)(vii), (c)(iii), (c)(iv). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Equivalence of mass and energy: E = mc²; E = m₀c²/√(1 − v²/c²).
- Graphs of how mass and kinetic energy vary with speed.
- Bertozzi's experiment as direct evidence for the variation of kinetic energy with speed.
- (OCR B) E_rest = mc² and E_total = γE_rest.

### Explained

**Rest energy.** A particle of rest mass m₀ has rest energy E₀ = m₀c².

**Total energy.** E = γm₀c² = m₀c²/√(1 − v²/c²). The relativistic mass m = γm₀ increases with speed (AQA's treatment).

**Kinetic energy.** Ek = E − E₀ = (γ − 1)m₀c². At low speeds this reduces to ½m₀v². As v → c, γ → ∞, so the energy needed to accelerate a particle to the speed of light is infinite: nothing with mass can reach c.

**Graphs.** Against v (from 0 to c): mass stays nearly m₀ at low speeds and rises steeply towards infinity as v approaches c (asymptote at v = c). Kinetic energy follows ½mv² at low speeds (Newtonian curve) but rises much more steeply near c, approaching infinity, while the Newtonian curve would reach ½m₀c² at v = c.

**Bertozzi (1964).** Electrons were accelerated by a linear accelerator to measured kinetic energies (from 0.5 to 15 MeV). Their speeds were found by timing them over a fixed distance (8.4 m) with an oscilloscope. Their kinetic energy was checked independently by the heat they produced when stopped in an aluminium target (measured with a thermocouple). Results: speed approached but never exceeded c, however much energy was given; Ek matched (γ − 1)m₀c², not ½mv². Direct evidence for relativistic kinetic energy.

### Worked example

1. An electron is accelerated to 0.90c. Find its total energy and kinetic energy in MeV.

   γ = 1/√(1 − 0.81) = 2.29. E = 2.29 × 0.511 = 1.17 MeV. Ek = 1.17 − 0.511 = 0.66 MeV.

2. (OCR B) A proton in an accelerator has total energy 10 GeV. Find γ and its speed.

   E_rest = 0.938 GeV, γ = 10.66. v = c√(1 − 1/γ²) = 0.9956c.

3. Find the speed of an electron with kinetic energy 2.0 MeV.

   E = 2.511 MeV, γ = 4.91. v = c√(1 − 1/24.1) = 0.979c.

### Common mistakes

- Using ½mv² for particles near c.
- Saying relativistic mass increase means the particle gets bigger.

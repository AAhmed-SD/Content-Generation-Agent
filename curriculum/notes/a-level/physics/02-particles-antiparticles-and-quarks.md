# Particles: the atom, antiparticles, interactions and quarks

This file builds the particle picture of matter: what atoms and nuclei are made of, why some nuclei decay, antimatter and photons, the four fundamental interactions and their exchange particles, and the classification of particles into hadrons and leptons built from quarks. It ends with the conservation laws used to check whether a reaction can happen.

## Overview

AS & A for AQA, OCR B and Eduqas; A-level only for Edexcel (Topic 8) and OCR A (Module 6). Alpha scattering and nuclear radius are in file 18; binding energy, mass–energy and fission are in file 19; particle accelerators and detectors are in file 33.

### Board differences

- AQA covers the most: exchange particles (virtual photon, W⁺, W⁻, pion), Feynman-style diagrams, strangeness, kaons and muons. No other board asks for strangeness conservation in such detail.
- Edexcel 140 adds that the symmetry of the quark–lepton model predicted the top quark; Edexcel 137 and 138 use ΔE = c²Δm and MeV/c² for creation and annihilation (AQA says E = mc² is not needed in calculations at this point).
- OCR A 6.4.2 needs u, d, s quarks and their charges, β⁻ and β⁺ decay in quark terms, and balancing quark equations. It does not need exchange particles.
- Eduqas C3.7 uses a three-generation picture but only tests the first generation (e, νₑ, u, d), and asks for the table of the four interactions with ranges. It accepts "quark number" in place of baryon number. Eduqas C3.7(k): neutrinos and quark flavour change only happen in weak interactions.
- OCR B 6.2.1 only needs a simple quark picture of protons and neutrons and the term list (gluon, hadron, lepton, lepton number).

## 3.2.1.1 Constituents of the atom

*Refs: AQA 3.2.1.1 · Edexcel 130 · OCR A 6.4.1(b), (d) · OCR B 6.2.1(b)(i). Level: AS & A (AQA, OCR B); A-level only (Edexcel, OCR A).*

### What you need to know

- The simple nuclear atom: protons and neutrons in a tiny nucleus, electrons around it.
- The charge and mass of the proton, neutron and electron in SI and relative units.
- Specific charge (charge ÷ mass) of the proton, electron, nuclei and ions.
- Proton number Z, nucleon number A, and nuclide notation ᴬ_Z X.
- What isotopes are and how isotopic data are used.

### Explained

| Particle | Charge / C | Relative charge | Mass / kg | Relative mass |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Proton | +1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ | +1 | 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ | 1 |
| Neutron | 0 | 0 | 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ | 1 |
| Electron | −1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ | −1 | 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ | 1/1836 (about 0.0005) |

- **Proton number Z** (atomic number): number of protons. It fixes the element.
- **Nucleon number A** (mass number): number of protons + neutrons.
- **Neutron number** N = A − Z.
- **Nuclide notation**: A written top left, Z bottom left of the symbol. Carbon-14 is ¹⁴₆C: 6 protons, 8 neutrons. A neutral atom also has Z electrons.
- **Isotopes**: atoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A). They have the same chemistry but different nuclear stability. Uses of isotopic data: carbon-14 dating (the fraction of ¹⁴C in once-living material falls as it decays), tracers in medicine, finding average relative atomic mass.

**Specific charge** = charge ÷ mass, in C kg⁻¹.

- Electron: 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ ÷ 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ = 1.76 × 10¹¹ C kg⁻¹ (the largest of any common particle).
- Proton: 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ ÷ 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ = 9.58 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹.
- Neutron: 0.
- For a nucleus: charge = Ze, mass ≈ A × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg (protons and neutrons only).
- For an ion: count the protons and electrons to get the net charge; include every nucleon (and, strictly, electrons) in the mass.

### Worked example

1. Find the specific charge of the ⁷₃Li nucleus.

   Charge = 3 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 4.80 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Mass ≈ (3 × 1.673 + 4 × 1.675) × 10⁻²⁷ = 1.172 × 10⁻²⁶ kg. Specific charge = 4.10 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹.

2. An ion of ²⁴₁₂Mg has lost two electrons. Find its specific charge.

   Charge = +2e = 3.20 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Mass ≈ 24 × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ = 4.01 × 10⁻²⁶ kg (the 10 electrons add only 9.1 × 10⁻³⁰ kg, under 0.03%). Specific charge = 7.98 × 10⁶ C kg⁻¹.

### Common mistakes

- Using the nucleon number for the charge of a nucleus.
- For a negative ion, forgetting the extra electrons give a negative charge.
- Saying isotopes have different numbers of protons.

## 3.2.1.2 Stable and unstable nuclei

*Refs: AQA 3.2.1.2 · OCR A 6.4.1(e) · OCR B 6.2.1(a)(vi). Level: AS & A (AQA); A-level only (OCR A).*

### What you need to know

- The strong nuclear force holds the nucleus together: attractive up to about 3 fm, repulsive at separations below about 0.5 fm.
- Unstable nuclei decay by alpha or beta emission.
- Equations for alpha decay and β⁻ decay, including the antineutrino.
- The neutrino was proposed to explain how energy is conserved in beta decay.

### Explained

Protons repel each other electrostatically. Gravity between nucleons is far too weak to stop this. The **strong nuclear force** acts between all nucleons (proton–proton, proton–neutron, neutron–neutron) and is the same for each pair.

- From about 0.5 fm to 3 fm it is strongly attractive; it is much stronger than the electrostatic repulsion at these distances.
- Beyond about 3 fm it falls to almost zero, so a nucleon only feels its nearest neighbours.
- Below about 0.5 fm it is repulsive, which stops the nucleus collapsing.

A graph of strong force against separation (label which direction is repulsion and which is attraction) starts large and repulsive at very small separation, crosses zero at about 0.5 fm, reaches a maximum attraction near 1 fm, then decays to zero by about 3 fm. The equilibrium separation of nucleons is where the force is zero.

**Why nuclei are unstable.** Large nuclei have many protons, and electrostatic repulsion acts across the whole nucleus while the strong force only acts between neighbours. Nuclei with too many protons, too many neutrons, or simply too many nucleons are unstable.

**Alpha decay.** Very large nuclei (Z above about 82) emit an alpha particle, a helium nucleus ⁴₂He:

ᴬ_Z X → ᴬ⁻⁴_{Z−2} Y + ⁴₂α. Example: ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂α.

**Beta-minus decay.** Neutron-rich nuclei: a neutron changes into a proton, emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino:

ᴬ_Z X → ᴬ_{Z+1} Y + ⁰₋₁β + ν̄ₑ. Example: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁β + ν̄ₑ.

**Why the neutrino was needed.** In alpha decay, every alpha particle from a given nuclide has the same kinetic energy. In beta decay, the electrons have a continuous range of energies up to a maximum. If only two particles were produced, conservation of energy and momentum would force a single energy. Pauli suggested a third, neutral, almost massless particle (the antineutrino) carries away the missing energy and momentum. It interacts so weakly that it was only detected about 25 years later.

### Worked example

1. Write the equation for the β⁻ decay of ⁹⁰₃₈Sr.

   ⁹⁰₃₈Sr → ⁹⁰₃₉Y + ⁰₋₁β + ν̄ₑ. Check: A: 90 = 90 + 0; Z: 38 = 39 − 1.

2. Polonium-210 emits an alpha particle. Write the equation.

   ²¹⁰₈₄Po → ²⁰⁶₈₂Pb + ⁴₂α.

### Common mistakes

- Writing a neutrino (not an antineutrino) in β⁻ decay.
- Saying the strong force acts between protons only, or that it acts on electrons.
- Saying the strong force is always attractive.

## 3.2.1.3 Particles, antiparticles and photons

*Refs: AQA 3.2.1.3 · Edexcel 137, 138, 141 · OCR A 6.4.2(a), (b), 6.4.4(c) · Eduqas C3.7(d), (e). Level: AS & A (AQA, Eduqas); A-level only (Edexcel, OCR A).*

### What you need to know

- Every particle has an antiparticle with the same mass and rest energy but opposite charge (and opposite other quantum numbers).
- The positron, antiproton, antineutron and antineutrino are the antiparticles of the electron, proton, neutron and neutrino.
- The photon model of electromagnetic radiation: E = hf = hc/λ.
- Annihilation and pair production, and the energies involved (rest energies in MeV).
- (Edexcel) Use ΔE = c²Δm, and MeV, GeV, MeV/c² and GeV/c² with conversion to SI.

### Explained

| Particle | Antiparticle | Symbol | Rest energy / MeV |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| electron e⁻ | positron | e⁺ | 0.511 |
| proton p | antiproton | p̄ | 938.3 |
| neutron n | antineutron | n̄ | 939.6 |
| neutrino νₑ | antineutrino | ν̄ₑ | about 0 |

The antiparticle has the same rest mass, opposite charge, and opposite baryon number, lepton number and strangeness. The antineutron is neutral, like the neutron, but has baryon number −1 and is made of antiquarks.

**Rest energy.** Mass is a store of energy: E = mc². The rest energy of an electron is 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 8.20 × 10⁻¹⁴ J = 0.511 MeV. Masses are often quoted as MeV/c² (electron 0.511 MeV/c²; proton 938 MeV/c²).

**Photons.** Electromagnetic radiation is emitted and absorbed in packets of energy called photons, each with energy E = hf = hc/λ, where h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s is the Planck constant. A photon has no charge and no rest mass, and travels at c.

**Annihilation.** When a particle meets its antiparticle, both are destroyed and their total energy (rest energy plus kinetic energy) becomes two photons. Two are needed so that momentum is conserved: if the pair was nearly at rest, the photons travel in opposite directions with equal energy. For a slow electron and positron, each photon has energy 0.511 MeV. Use: positron emission tomography (PET), where a tracer emits positrons that annihilate with electrons and the two gamma photons are detected on opposite sides of the patient.

**Pair production.** A photon with enough energy can turn into a particle–antiparticle pair. Minimum photon energy = 2 × rest energy of the particle. For an electron–positron pair, E_min = 1.022 MeV. Any extra energy becomes kinetic energy of the pair. It happens near a nucleus, which recoils to conserve momentum. In a cloud chamber in a magnetic field, the two particles leave spiral tracks curving in opposite directions from the same point.

### Worked example

1. Find the minimum frequency of a photon that can produce a proton–antiproton pair.

   E_min = 2 × 938.3 MeV = 1876.6 MeV = 1876.6 × 10⁶ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ J. f = E/h = 3.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ / 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = 4.53 × 10²³ Hz.

2. A slow electron and slow positron annihilate. Find the wavelength of each photon.

   E = 0.511 MeV = 8.18 × 10⁻¹⁴ J. λ = hc/E = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸) / 8.18 × 10⁻¹⁴ = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m.

3. (Edexcel) A particle has mass 1.88 × 10⁻²⁸ kg. Express its mass in MeV/c².

   E = mc² = 1.88 × 10⁻²⁸ × 9.00 × 10¹⁶ = 1.69 × 10⁻¹¹ J = 1.69 × 10⁻¹¹ / 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 106 MeV. Mass = 106 MeV/c² (a muon).

### Common mistakes

- Saying an antiparticle has opposite mass. Mass is the same.
- Saying annihilation produces one photon.
- Forgetting to double the rest energy for pair production.
- Forgetting to convert MeV to J (× 1.60 × 10⁻¹³) before using E = hf.

## 3.2.1.4 Particle interactions

*Refs: AQA 3.2.1.4 · Eduqas C3.7(i), (k). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The four fundamental interactions: gravity, electromagnetic, weak nuclear and strong nuclear.
- Forces between particles are explained by exchange particles.
- The electromagnetic force is carried by virtual photons.
- The weak interaction (in β⁻ decay, β⁺ decay, electron capture and electron–proton collisions) is carried by W⁺ and W⁻ bosons.
- Simple diagrams showing incoming and outgoing particles and the exchange particle.

### Explained

| Interaction | Acts on | Range | Exchange particle |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strong | Quarks, so all hadrons (not leptons) | About 10⁻¹⁵ m | Pions between nucleons (gluons between quarks, not tested) |
| Electromagnetic | Charged particles | Infinite | Virtual photon (γ) |
| Weak | All quarks and leptons | About 10⁻¹⁸ m | W⁺, W⁻ (and Z⁰, not tested) |
| Gravity | All particles with mass | Infinite | Graviton (hypothetical, not tested) |

Eduqas C3.7(i) adds: gravity is negligible except between very large objects; the weak interaction only shows up when the strong and electromagnetic interactions cannot act; neutral hadrons still feel the electromagnetic force because their quarks are charged.

**Exchange particles.** In particle physics, a force is the result of particles exchanging a virtual particle that carries momentum (and energy) between them. A model: two people on skateboards throwing a heavy ball to each other move apart (repulsion). Virtual particles exist only briefly; heavier exchange particles have shorter range, which is why the weak force (W mass about 80 GeV/c²) has such a short range and the electromagnetic force (massless photon) has infinite range.

**Weak interactions.** The weak interaction is the only one that can change one type (flavour) of quark into another, and it is the only one that involves neutrinos.

| Process | Equation | Exchange particle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| β⁻ decay | n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ | W⁻ (from the neutron to the electron–antineutrino pair) |
| β⁺ decay | p → n + e⁺ + νₑ | W⁺ |
| Electron capture | p + e⁻ → n + νₑ | W⁺ (from proton to electron) or W⁻ (from electron to proton); both are accepted pictures |
| Electron–proton collision | e⁻ + p → n + νₑ | W⁻ (from electron to proton) |
| Neutrino–neutron | νₑ + n → p + e⁻ | W⁻ (from neutron to neutrino) or W⁺ the other way |

**Drawing interaction diagrams.** Time runs up the page. Draw each incoming particle as a line coming in from the bottom, each outgoing particle as a line leaving the top, and the exchange particle as a wavy line between the two vertices. Check charge is conserved at each vertex. For β⁻ decay: n comes in, p goes out at the same vertex, a W⁻ leaves that vertex (charge 0 → +1 + (−1)), and the W⁻ decays into e⁻ and ν̄ₑ at the second vertex. Electron–electron repulsion: two electrons in, two electrons out, a wavy line labelled γ between them.

### Worked example

1. Show that charge is conserved at both vertices of a β⁺ decay diagram.

   Vertex 1: p (+1) → n (0) + W⁺ (+1). Vertex 2: W⁺ (+1) → e⁺ (+1) + νₑ (0). Both balance.

2. Which exchange particle is involved when an electron is captured by a proton, if the W comes from the proton?

   p (+1) → n (0) + W⁺; then W⁺ + e⁻ → νₑ. So W⁺.

### Common mistakes

- Saying leptons feel the strong force.
- Saying the photon is the exchange particle of the weak interaction.
- Getting the W charge wrong: work out charge at the vertex rather than guessing.
- Drawing the W as a straight line, or showing particles appearing from nowhere.

## 3.2.1.5 Classification of particles

*Refs: AQA 3.2.1.5 · Edexcel 140 · OCR A 6.4.2(c), (d) · Eduqas C3.7(c), (f). Level: AS & A (AQA, Eduqas); A-level only (Edexcel, OCR A).*

### What you need to know

- Hadrons feel the strong interaction. They are baryons (proton, neutron) and antibaryons, or mesons (pion, kaon).
- Baryon number is a quantum number that is always conserved.
- The proton is the only stable baryon; all others eventually decay into it.
- The pion is the exchange particle of the strong force between nucleons. The kaon can decay into pions.
- Leptons (electron, muon, electron neutrino, muon neutrino and their antiparticles) do not feel the strong force.
- Lepton number is conserved separately for electron leptons and muon leptons.
- The muon decays into an electron.
- Strange particles are produced by the strong interaction and decay by the weak interaction. Strangeness is conserved in strong interactions and can change by 0 or ±1 in weak interactions. Strange particles are produced in pairs.
- Particle physics depends on large, international teams to test and validate new results.

### Explained

```
                    particles
          ┌────────────┴─────────────┐
       hadrons                    leptons
   (feel strong force)       (do not feel strong force)
     ┌──────┴──────┐          e⁻, μ⁻, νₑ, ν_μ
  baryons       mesons        and antiparticles
  (3 quarks)   (quark + antiquark)
  p, n, ...    π, K
```

**Baryons.** Proton (B = +1), neutron (B = +1). Antibaryons have B = −1. Mesons and leptons have B = 0. Baryon number is always conserved. The free neutron is unstable (half-life about 10 minutes) and decays to a proton; the proton has never been seen to decay.

**Mesons.**

| Meson | Charges | Rest energy | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pion | π⁺, π⁰, π⁻ | About 140 MeV (π⁰ 135) | Exchange particle of the strong force between nucleons; found in cosmic ray showers |
| Kaon | K⁺, K⁰, K⁻ | About 494 MeV (K⁰ 498) | Strange; decays into pions (or muons) |

**Leptons.** Fundamental particles (not made of anything smaller).

| Lepton | L_e | L_μ | Charge |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| e⁻, νₑ | +1 | 0 | −1, 0 |
| e⁺, ν̄ₑ | −1 | 0 | +1, 0 |
| μ⁻, ν_μ | 0 | +1 | −1, 0 |
| μ⁺, ν̄_μ | 0 | −1 | +1, 0 |

The muon is a heavy version of the electron (rest energy 106 MeV) and decays: μ⁻ → e⁻ + ν̄ₑ + ν_μ. Check: L_μ: 1 = 0 + 0 + 1; L_e: 0 = 1 − 1 + 0.

**Strangeness.** Kaons were puzzling: they were produced quickly (by the strong interaction) but lived a long time (about 10⁻⁸ s, typical of a weak decay). Strangeness S explains this. It is conserved in strong interactions, so strange particles must be made in pairs with opposite strangeness (S = +1 and S = −1). In decay, they can only use the weak interaction, which allows S to change by 0 or ±1, and so they decay slowly.

| Particle | S |
| --- | --- |
| K⁺, K⁰ | +1 |
| K⁻, K̄⁰ | −1 |
| s quark | −1 |
| s̄ antiquark | +1 |
| Non-strange particles | 0 |

**Big science.** Particle accelerators like those at CERN cost billions and need thousands of physicists and engineers from many countries. Results are only accepted after independent teams and detectors confirm them and the work is peer reviewed (for example, the Higgs boson was confirmed by two separate detector teams, ATLAS and CMS).

### Worked example

1. Classify each particle: K⁰, μ⁺, antineutron, π⁻.

   K⁰: meson (hadron). μ⁺: lepton. Antineutron: antibaryon (hadron). π⁻: meson.

2. Explain why the reaction p + p → p + p + K⁺ cannot happen, but p + p → p + p + K⁺ + K⁻ can.

   It would be a strong interaction (hadrons colliding), so strangeness must be conserved. First: S = 0 → 0 + 0 + 1, not conserved. Second: 0 → 0 + 0 + 1 − 1 = 0, conserved. (Charge: +2 → +2 + 1 − 1 = +2. Baryon number: 2 → 2.)

### Common mistakes

- Calling a muon a meson (it is a lepton, despite the old name "mu meson").
- Mixing electron lepton number and muon lepton number; each is conserved separately.
- Saying strangeness is conserved in weak interactions.
- Saying the neutron is stable outside the nucleus.

## 3.2.1.6 Quarks and antiquarks

*Refs: AQA 3.2.1.6 · Edexcel 140, 141 · OCR A 6.4.2(e), (f), (g), (h) · OCR B 6.2.1(a)(v) · Eduqas C3.7(c), (e), (f), (g), (h). Level: AS & A (AQA, OCR B, Eduqas); A-level only (Edexcel, OCR A).*

### What you need to know

- Charge, baryon number and strangeness of up, down and strange quarks and their antiquarks.
- Quark make-up of the proton, neutron, antiproton, antineutron, pions and kaons.
- The decay of the neutron.
- Quarks are never seen on their own (they are confined in hadrons).

### Explained

| Quark | Charge (e) | Baryon number | Strangeness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| up u | +2/3 | +1/3 | 0 |
| down d | −1/3 | +1/3 | 0 |
| strange s | −1/3 | +1/3 | −1 |
| anti-up ū | −2/3 | −1/3 | 0 |
| anti-down d̄ | +1/3 | −1/3 | 0 |
| anti-strange s̄ | +1/3 | −1/3 | +1 |

| Hadron | Quarks | Check of charge |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proton | uud | 2/3 + 2/3 − 1/3 = +1 |
| Neutron | udd | 2/3 − 1/3 − 1/3 = 0 |
| Antiproton | ūūd̄ | −1 |
| Antineutron | ūd̄d̄ | 0 |
| π⁺ | ud̄ | 2/3 + 1/3 = +1 |
| π⁻ | dū | −1 |
| π⁰ | uū or dd̄ | 0 |
| K⁺ | us̄ | 2/3 + 1/3 = +1 (S = +1) |
| K⁻ | sū | −1 (S = −1) |
| K⁰ | ds̄ | 0 (S = +1) |
| K̄⁰ | sd̄ | 0 (S = −1) |

Baryons are three quarks; antibaryons are three antiquarks; mesons are one quark and one antiquark. Quarks are always bound in hadrons; trying to pull one out supplies enough energy to make a new quark–antiquark pair, so a meson forms instead (confinement).

**(Eduqas C3.7(h))** Other first-generation baryons can be worked out from charge: a baryon of charge +2 must be uuu; one of charge −1 must be ddd.

**(Edexcel 140)** The quark–lepton model is symmetric: quarks and leptons come in matching pairs (generations). After the bottom quark was found, the symmetry predicted a partner, the top quark, which was found in 1995.

**Neutron decay.** n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ. In quark terms, one down quark becomes an up quark: udd → uud, so d → u + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ (via W⁻).

### Worked example

1. A particle is made of a strange quark and an anti-down quark. Find its charge, baryon number and strangeness, and name it.

   Charge −1/3 + 1/3 = 0. B = 1/3 − 1/3 = 0 (a meson). S = −1. It is the K̄⁰ (anti-K-zero).

2. The Λ⁰ baryon has strangeness −1 and charge 0. Suggest its quark content.

   Three quarks, one of which is s (S = −1). Remaining charge must be +1/3 from two quarks: u + d (2/3 − 1/3). So uds.

### Common mistakes

- Giving the antiquark of the down quark a charge of −1/3 (it is +1/3).
- Forgetting the s quark has S = −1, not +1.
- Writing mesons as two quarks (they are a quark and an antiquark).

## 3.2.1.7 Applications of conservation laws

*Refs: AQA 3.2.1.7 · Edexcel 135, 142, 143 · OCR A 6.4.2(i), (j), (k), (l) · OCR B 6.2.1(a)(vi) · Eduqas C3.7(j), (k). Level: AS & A (AQA, OCR B, Eduqas); A-level only (Edexcel, OCR A).*

### What you need to know

- How quarks change in β⁻ and β⁺ decay.
- How to use conservation of charge, baryon number, lepton number and strangeness to decide whether an interaction can happen (data for unfamiliar particles will be given).
- Energy and momentum are conserved in all interactions.

### Explained

**Quark changes in beta decay.**

- β⁻: d → u + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ (a neutron becomes a proton).
- β⁺: u → d + e⁺ + νₑ (a proton in a proton-rich nucleus becomes a neutron). This cannot happen to a free proton, because the neutron is heavier; the energy comes from the nucleus.

**Checklist for any reaction.**

| Quantity | Strong | Electromagnetic | Weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Charge | Conserved | Conserved | Conserved |
| Baryon number | Conserved | Conserved | Conserved |
| Lepton numbers L_e and L_μ (separately) | Conserved | Conserved | Conserved |
| Strangeness | Conserved | Conserved | Can change by 0, ±1 |
| Energy and momentum | Conserved | Conserved | Conserved |

If any always-conserved quantity fails, the reaction cannot happen. If only strangeness fails by one, the reaction can only happen by the weak interaction. In addition, the total rest energy of the products cannot exceed the total energy available.

**Which interaction?** If a neutrino is involved, or a quark changes flavour, it is weak. If only charged particles and photons are involved, it is electromagnetic. If only hadrons are involved and strangeness is conserved, it is probably strong.

**(Edexcel 135)** Particle tracks in detectors: charged particles leave tracks; neutral ones do not, but their decay products do (a "V" appearing from nowhere shows a neutral particle decaying into two oppositely charged ones). Curvature in a magnetic field shows sign of charge and momentum (see file 33).

### Worked example

1. Can the reaction p + ν̄ₑ → n + e⁺ happen? If so, by which interaction?

   Charge: +1 + 0 = 0 + 1 ✓. B: 1 = 1 ✓. L_e: 0 − 1 = 0 − 1 ✓. S: 0 = 0 ✓. Allowed. A neutrino is involved, so weak.

2. Can π⁻ + p → K⁰ + n happen?

   Charge: −1 + 1 = 0 + 0 ✓. B: 0 + 1 = 0 + 1 ✓. S: 0 → +1 + 0 ✗. A collision of hadrons that makes a strange particle is a strong interaction, and strangeness must be conserved, so this cannot happen. (π⁻ + p → K⁰ + Λ⁰ is allowed: S goes 0 → +1 − 1 = 0, so strange particles appear in pairs.)

3. Show that μ⁻ → e⁻ + γ is not observed.

   L_μ: +1 → 0 ✗; L_e: 0 → +1 ✗. Forbidden.

### Common mistakes

- Checking only charge and stopping.
- Treating L_e and L_μ as one lepton number.
- Saying a reaction is "possible" when strangeness changes by 2.
- Forgetting that an antilepton has lepton number −1.

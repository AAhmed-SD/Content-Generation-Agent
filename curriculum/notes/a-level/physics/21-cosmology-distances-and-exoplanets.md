## 3.9.3.1 Doppler effect

*Refs: AQA 3.9.3.1 · Edexcel 161, 162 · OCR A 5.5.3(e), (f) · OCR B 5.1.3(a)(iii), (c)(ii) · Eduqas C2.8(g) to (j). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Δf/f = v/c and z = Δλ/λ = −Δf/f ≈ v/c for v ≪ c, for optical and radio frequencies.
- Calculations on binary stars viewed in the plane of their orbit.
- Doppler shifts of galaxies and quasars.
- (Eduqas) Centre of mass of two bodies and their mutual orbital period; radial velocity; using radial velocity curves to find masses.
- (OCR B) Radar measurement of distance and relative velocity.

### Explained

**Doppler effect.** When a source of waves moves relative to an observer, the observed frequency and wavelength change. Moving away: wavelength longer, frequency lower (for light, shifted towards the red: **redshift**). Moving towards: shorter wavelength (**blueshift**). Only the component of velocity along the line of sight (the radial velocity) causes a shift.

For speeds much less than c:

z = Δλ/λ ≈ v/c and Δf/f ≈ −v/c (frequency falls for a receding source)

where λ is the emitted (rest) wavelength. z is the redshift (positive for recession). Astronomers measure Δλ by comparing the positions of absorption lines in a star's or galaxy's spectrum with the same lines measured in a lab.

**Binary stars.** Two stars orbit their common centre of mass. Viewed edge-on (in the plane of the orbit), each star alternately approaches and recedes, so its spectral lines shift back and forth periodically. A graph of radial velocity against time is a sine curve. From it:

- Period T = time for one cycle.
- Orbital speed v = amplitude of the velocity curve.
- Orbital radius r = vT/2π.

**Centre of mass** (Eduqas C2.8(g)). For two masses m₁ and m₂ a distance d apart, the centre of mass is at distances r₁ = m₂d/(m₁ + m₂) from m₁ and r₂ = m₁d/(m₁ + m₂) from m₂ (so m₁r₁ = m₂r₂). Both stars have the same period: T² = 4π²d³/(G(m₁ + m₂)). The heavier star moves in the smaller circle, more slowly.

**Radar** (OCR B 5.1.3(a)(i), (iii)). A radio pulse is sent to a planet or asteroid; the echo returns after time t. Distance = ct/2 (assuming the speed of light is the same for all observers, so distance can be defined in terms of light travel time). Relative velocity from two pulses: if the echo times are t₁ and t₂ for pulses sent Δt apart, the change in distance is c(t₂ − t₁)/2 in time Δt. Or from the Doppler shift of the echo: Δf/f = 2v/c (the shift happens twice, on the way and back).

### Worked example

1. A hydrogen line with rest wavelength 656.3 nm is observed at 662.8 nm in a galaxy. Find its recession speed.

   z = 6.5/656.3 = 9.90 × 10⁻³. v = zc = 2.97 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹.

2. A spectroscopic binary seen edge-on shows a line from one star varying by ±0.042 nm about 500.0 nm, with a period of 4.0 days. Find the star's orbital speed and radius.

   v = (0.042/500.0) × 3.0 × 10⁸ = 2.5 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹. r = vT/2π = 2.5 × 10⁴ × 3.46 × 10⁵/(2π) = 1.4 × 10⁹ m.

3. (Eduqas) Two stars of 2.0 and 1.0 solar masses (M_Sun = 2.0 × 10³⁰ kg) are 3.0 × 10¹¹ m apart. Find the position of the centre of mass and the period.

   From the heavier star: r₁ = 1.0 × 3.0 × 10¹¹/3.0 = 1.0 × 10¹¹ m. T = 2π√(d³/(G(m₁ + m₂))) = 2π√(2.7 × 10³⁴/(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 6.0 × 10³⁰)) = 2π√(6.75 × 10¹³) = 5.2 × 10⁷ s (1.6 years).

4. (OCR B) A radar pulse returns from Venus 276 s after it is sent. Find the distance.

   d = 3.0 × 10⁸ × 276/2 = 4.1 × 10¹⁰ m.

### Common mistakes

- Using the observed wavelength instead of the rest wavelength as λ.
- Saying redshift means the object looks red.
- Forgetting that only motion along the line of sight causes a shift.

## Distances: parallax and standard candles

*Refs: Edexcel 157, 158 · OCR A 5.5.3(a), (b), (c) · OCR B 5.1.3(c)(i). Level: A-level only. (AQA 3.9.2.2 defines the parsec; see file 20.)*

### What you need to know

- The astronomical unit, the light year and the parsec.
- Stellar parallax and p = 1/d (p in arcseconds, d in parsecs).
- (Edexcel) Standard candles: objects of known luminosity; d from I = L/(4πd²).

### Explained

**Units.** 1 AU = mean Earth–Sun distance = 1.50 × 10¹¹ m. 1 ly = 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m. 1 pc = 3.09 × 10¹⁶ m = 3.26 ly = 206 265 AU.

**Parallax.** As the Earth orbits the Sun, a nearby star appears to move against the background of very distant stars. Measure its position six months apart; the baseline is 2 AU. The parallax angle p is half the total angular shift (the angle subtended by 1 AU at the star). For small angles, tan p ≈ p (radians) = 1 AU/d. Defining the parsec as the distance where p = 1 arcsecond: **d (pc) = 1/p (arcsec)**. Parallax works up to a few hundred parsecs from the ground (angles below about 0.01 arcsec are hard to measure because of atmospheric blurring), and to thousands of parsecs from space (Gaia).

**Standard candles** (Edexcel 158). An object whose luminosity L is known (from its type). Measure its intensity (flux) I at the Earth, then d = √(L/(4πI)). Examples: Cepheid variable stars (their period of pulsation is related to their luminosity), type Ia supernovae (file 20). They extend distance measurements to other galaxies.

### Worked example

1. A star has a parallax of 0.25 arcsec. Find its distance in pc, ly and m.

   d = 4.0 pc = 13 ly = 1.24 × 10¹⁷ m.

2. A Cepheid with L = 3.8 × 10³⁰ W gives I = 4.5 × 10⁻¹⁵ W m⁻² at the Earth. Find its distance.

   d = √(3.8 × 10³⁰/(4π × 4.5 × 10⁻¹⁵)) = √(6.72 × 10⁴³) = 8.2 × 10²¹ m (about 270 kpc).

### Common mistakes

- Using the full angular shift over six months as p.
- Using p in degrees or radians in d = 1/p.

## 3.9.3.2 Hubble's law

*Refs: AQA 3.9.3.2 · Edexcel 162, 163 · OCR A 5.5.3(d), (g) to (o) · OCR B 5.1.3(a)(iv) · Eduqas C2.8(e), (f), (k), (l), (m). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Redshift and Hubble's law v = Hd.
- Interpretation as expansion of the universe; estimating the age of the universe (assuming H is constant).
- Big Bang theory, with evidence from the cosmic microwave background radiation and the relative abundance of hydrogen and helium.
- (OCR A, Edexcel, Eduqas) The cosmological principle; H₀ in km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ and in s⁻¹; dark matter (galaxy rotation curves) and dark energy; the uncertain value of H₀ and the fate of the universe; critical density.

### Explained

**Hubble's law.** The recession velocity of a distant galaxy is proportional to its distance: v = H₀d. H₀ ≈ 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹. In SI units: 70 × 10³ m s⁻¹ / 3.09 × 10²² m = 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹. (Measurements disagree between about 67 and 74 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹; this is the "Hubble tension".)

**Interpretation.** Almost all galaxies are moving away from us, and further ones faster. This does not mean we are at the centre: the whole of space is expanding, so every galaxy sees every other receding (like dots on an inflating balloon). The redshift of distant galaxies is really caused by space stretching the light as it travels (cosmological redshift).

**Cosmological principle** (OCR A 5.5.3(d)). On large scales the universe is homogeneous (the same everywhere) and isotropic (the same in every direction), and the laws of physics are universal.

**Age of the universe.** If expansion has always been at the same rate, all galaxies were together at t = 0. Time for a galaxy at distance d to get there at speed v: t = d/v = 1/H₀. With H₀ = 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹: t = 4.4 × 10¹⁷ s ≈ 14 billion years. (The current best estimate is 13.8 billion years.)

**The Big Bang theory.** The universe began about 14 billion years ago from an extremely hot, dense state and has been expanding and cooling since. The Big Bang created space and time themselves, so it did not happen at a point within space (OCR A 5.5.3(l)). Evolution (OCR A 5.5.3(n)): within the first fraction of a second, quarks and leptons formed; quarks combined into protons and neutrons; within a few minutes, some hydrogen fused to helium; after about 380 000 years, the universe cooled enough for atoms to form and become transparent; after about 200 million years the first stars and galaxies formed.

**Evidence.**

1. *Redshift of galaxies* (Hubble's law): the universe is expanding.
2. *Cosmic microwave background radiation (CMB)*: microwaves arriving equally from all directions, with a black-body spectrum at 2.7 K. It is the radiation released when atoms first formed (when the universe was about 3000 K), stretched into microwaves by the expansion since. Its uniformity, with tiny variations, fits the Big Bang.
3. *Abundance of hydrogen and helium*: about 75% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass. The Big Bang model predicts that, in the few minutes when the universe was hot enough for fusion, about this much helium formed before expansion cooled it too much. Stars alone cannot have made this much helium.

**Dark matter** (Eduqas C2.8(e), Edexcel 163, OCR A 5.5.3(o)). In a spiral galaxy, if most mass were in the bright centre, orbital speeds of stars should fall with distance from the centre (like planets: v = √(GM/r)). Instead, measured speeds (from Doppler shifts) stay roughly constant far out. This means there is much more mass than we can see, spread out in a halo: dark matter, which does not emit or absorb light. It is thought to make up about 27% of the universe's energy content. (Eduqas C2.8(f): the Higgs boson gives particles mass; some theories link dark matter to particles that interact with the Higgs field; this is uncertain.)

**Dark energy and the fate of the universe** (AQA, Edexcel 163, OCR A 5.5.3(o)). Type Ia supernovae show the expansion is accelerating, explained by "dark energy" (about 68% of the universe; ordinary matter about 5%). The fate of the universe depends on the balance of its density with the expansion:

- *Critical density* (Eduqas C2.8(m)): ρc = 3H₀²/(8πG) ≈ 9 × 10⁻²⁷ kg m⁻³ (a few hydrogen atoms per cubic metre). Denser than this, gravity would eventually stop and reverse expansion (Big Crunch); less dense, it expands for ever; equal, it slows towards zero.
- Controversy (Edexcel 163): the age depends on H₀, which is hard to measure accurately (distances to far galaxies are uncertain). The fate depends on the amount of dark matter and dark energy, neither of which has been detected directly.

### Worked example

1. A galaxy has redshift z = 0.020. Find its distance using H₀ = 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹.

   v = 0.020 × 3.0 × 10⁵ km s⁻¹ = 6000 km s⁻¹. d = 6000/70 = 86 Mpc.

2. Estimate the age of the universe if H₀ = 67 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹.

   H₀ = 67 × 10³/3.09 × 10²² = 2.17 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹. t = 4.6 × 10¹⁷ s = 14.6 billion years.

3. (Eduqas) Calculate the critical density for H₀ = 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹.

   ρc = 3 × (2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸)²/(8π × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹) = 9.5 × 10⁻²⁷ kg m⁻³.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the Big Bang was an explosion at a point in space.
- Forgetting to convert Mpc to m for SI units of H₀.
- Saying the CMB comes from stars.
- Saying the age of the universe is exactly 1/H₀ (it assumes constant expansion).

## 3.9.3.3 Quasars

*Refs: AQA 3.9.3.3. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Quasars are the most distant measurable objects.
- They were discovered as bright radio sources.
- They have large optical redshifts; estimates of distance and power output.
- They form from active supermassive black holes.

### Explained

Quasars (quasi-stellar radio sources) were found in the 1960s as strong radio sources that looked like faint stars in optical telescopes. Their spectra have very large redshifts (z often above 1, up to about 7), so by Hubble's law they are extremely far away, billions of light years. To appear as bright as they do from so far, they must have enormous power outputs, around 10³⁹ to 10⁴¹ W, more than a thousand galaxies. Their brightness changes over days, so they must be small (light must cross them in that time, so less than a few light days across).

Explanation: a quasar is the active core of a young galaxy, powered by a supermassive black hole. Matter spiralling into it forms a hot accretion disc that radiates intensely before falling in, and jets of particles stream out along the poles. We see them as they were long ago (light has taken billions of years to arrive), when galaxies were young and their black holes were feeding.

### Worked example

1. A quasar has z = 0.16. Estimate its distance (H₀ = 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹), and its power if its intensity at Earth is 2.0 × 10⁻¹² W m⁻².

   v = 0.16c = 4.8 × 10⁴ km s⁻¹; d = 690 Mpc = 2.1 × 10²⁵ m. P = 4πd²I = 4π × (2.1 × 10²⁵)² × 2.0 × 10⁻¹² = 1.1 × 10⁴⁰ W.

(For large z, v ≈ zc is only approximate; relativistic formulae are not needed.)

### Common mistakes

- Saying quasars are stars.

## 3.9.3.4 Detection of exoplanets

*Refs: AQA 3.9.3.4 · Eduqas C2.8(j). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Why exoplanets are hard to detect directly.
- The radial velocity (Doppler shift) method and the transit method.
- A typical transit light curve.

### Explained

**Difficulties.** Planets are very small and faint and are very close to their star (in angle) compared with the star's glare; the star is billions of times brighter, so the planet is lost in its light, and the angle between them is below the telescope's resolution.

**Radial velocity method.** A star and planet orbit their common centre of mass. The star wobbles in a small orbit, so its spectral lines are Doppler shifted back and forth periodically (for an orbit not face-on). The period gives the planet's orbital period; the size of the star's velocity variation, with the star's mass, gives a minimum mass for the planet. Best for massive planets close to their stars (large, fast wobbles). Very precise spectrometers detect speeds of about 1 m s⁻¹.

**Transit method.** If the orbit is nearly edge-on to us, the planet passes in front of the star once per orbit, blocking a small fraction of its light. The light curve (intensity against time) is flat, then drops slightly (by about (r_planet/r_star)²: 1% for a Jupiter-size planet in front of a Sun-like star), stays low with a flat bottom while the planet is fully in front, then rises again; this repeats every orbit. The dip depth gives the planet's size; the time between dips gives its period (and so orbital radius by Kepler's third law). Used by the Kepler and TESS space telescopes. Only works for the small fraction of systems aligned edge-on.

### Worked example

1. A transit reduces a star's intensity by 0.80%. The star's radius is 7.0 × 10⁸ m. Estimate the planet's radius.

   (r/R)² = 0.0080; r = 7.0 × 10⁸ × 0.089 = 6.3 × 10⁷ m (similar to Jupiter).

### Common mistakes

- Saying the radial velocity method sees the planet's own spectrum.
- Saying the transit light curve drops to zero.

# Turning points: the discovery of the electron, and wave–particle duality

Two great shifts in physics: the discovery that atoms contain electrons, and the discovery that light and matter behave as both waves and particles. This file covers cathode rays, thermionic emission, measuring the specific charge of the electron, Millikan's oil-drop experiment, Newton's and Huygens' theories of light, Young's experiment, Maxwell's electromagnetic waves, Hertz and Fizeau, the ultraviolet catastrophe and Planck's quanta, Einstein's photon explanation, de Broglie waves, and electron microscopes (TEM and STM).

## 3.12.1.1 Cathode rays

*Refs: AQA 3.12.1.1. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- How cathode rays are produced in a discharge tube.

### Explained

A discharge tube is a glass tube of gas at low pressure with a metal electrode at each end, connected to a high p.d. (several kV). The few ions present in the gas are accelerated by the field; positive ions hit the cathode and knock out electrons; electrons collide with gas atoms, ionising more (an avalanche) and exciting them so the gas glows. Some electrons stream from the cathode towards the anode: these were called cathode rays. Experiments showed cathode rays travel in straight lines (casting sharp shadows), carry negative charge, can turn a paddle wheel (they have momentum) and are deflected by electric and magnetic fields. Thomson (1897) showed they are particles with a fixed charge-to-mass ratio: electrons.

### Common mistakes

- Saying cathode rays are electromagnetic waves.

## 3.12.1.2 Thermionic emission of electrons

*Refs: AQA 3.12.1.2 · Edexcel 132 · OCR B 5.2.2(a)(ii). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The principle of thermionic emission.
- The work done on an electron accelerated through a p.d. V: ½mv² = eV.

### Explained

When a metal filament is heated (by passing a current through it), some free electrons gain enough kinetic energy to escape from the surface (they need more energy than the work function). This is thermionic emission. The number escaping rises very steeply with temperature (OCR B: a Boltzmann-factor process). In a vacuum tube, an anode at a high positive potential relative to the filament (cathode) accelerates the emitted electrons. Each gains kinetic energy eV (the electrons start with negligible speed):

½mv² = eV, so v = √(2eV/m)

A hole in the anode lets a narrow beam of electrons through (an electron gun), used in old CRT televisions, electron diffraction tubes, X-ray tubes and electron microscopes. The tube must be evacuated so electrons do not collide with gas molecules.

### Worked example

1. Find the speed of an electron accelerated through 2500 V.

   v = √(2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2500/9.11 × 10⁻³¹) = 2.96 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹.

### Common mistakes

- Saying electrons are emitted because the filament is negative (they are emitted because it is hot; the anode p.d. only accelerates them).

## 3.12.1.3 Specific charge of the electron

*Refs: AQA 3.12.1.3 · Edexcel 132. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- One method of determining e/m.
- The significance of Thomson's measurement.
- Comparison with the specific charge of the hydrogen ion.

### Explained

**Method: fine-beam tube.** An electron gun in a glass bulb with low-pressure gas (so the beam path glows) is placed between a pair of Helmholtz coils that give a uniform magnetic field B perpendicular to the beam. The electrons move in a circle of radius r (measured with a scale).

- Magnetic force provides centripetal force: Bev = mv²/r, so v = Ber/m.
- Energy from the gun: ½mv² = eV.
- Combining: **e/m = 2V/(B²r²)**.

B is found from the coil current (calibrated, or with a Hall probe). Measure r for several V or B.

**Alternative: crossed fields (Thomson's method).** In a deflection tube, adjust an electric field E (between plates) and a magnetic field B so the beam goes straight: eE = Bev, giving v = E/B. Then switch off E and measure the radius in B alone (or use ½mv² = eV): e/m = v/(Br) = E/(B²r).

**Significance.** Thomson found e/m = 1.76 × 10¹¹ C kg⁻¹, the same whatever cathode metal or gas was used. So electrons are a component of all atoms: atoms are not indivisible. The value was about 1800 times larger than the specific charge of the hydrogen ion (9.6 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹, the largest then known). Assuming the electron's charge is similar in size to the hydrogen ion's, the electron must be about 1800 times lighter: a subatomic particle.

### Worked example

1. In a fine-beam tube, electrons accelerated through 250 V move in a circle of radius 0.050 m in a field of 1.07 mT. Find e/m.

   e/m = 2 × 250/((1.07 × 10⁻³)² × 0.050²) = 500/(1.145 × 10⁻⁶ × 2.5 × 10⁻³) = 1.75 × 10¹¹ C kg⁻¹.

### Common mistakes

- Saying Thomson measured e and m separately.

## 3.12.1.4 Principle of Millikan's determination of the electronic charge, e

*Refs: AQA 3.12.1.4 · OCR B 6.1.2(a)(iv). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The condition for holding a charged oil drop stationary between oppositely charged plates: QV/d = mg.
- The motion of a falling drop with and without an electric field; using terminal speed to find the drop's mass and charge.
- Stokes' law, F = 6πηrv, to find the drop's radius.
- The significance of Millikan's results: quantisation of charge.

### Explained

Fine oil drops are sprayed between two horizontal metal plates a distance d apart; some become charged by friction (or by X-rays ionising the air). The drops are viewed through a microscope.

**Step 1: field off.** A drop falls and quickly reaches terminal speed v₁, where its weight equals the viscous drag (ignoring upthrust): mg = 6πηrv₁. With m = (4/3)πr³ρ:

(4/3)πr³ρg = 6πηrv₁ → r = √(9ηv₁/(2ρg))

Measuring v₁ (time to fall between two marks on the eyepiece scale) gives r and so m.

**Step 2: field on.** A p.d. V is applied so the electric force QV/d acts upwards. Adjust V until the drop is held stationary: QV/d = mg, so Q = mgd/V. (Alternatively measure the new terminal speed with the field on.)

**Results.** Every charge measured was a whole-number multiple of 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. So charge is quantised: it comes in units of e, the charge of the electron. Combined with Thomson's e/m, this gave the mass of the electron.

### Worked example

1. An oil drop (ρ = 880 kg m⁻³) falls at terminal speed 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹ in air (η = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ Pa s). Find its radius and mass. It is held still between plates 6.0 mm apart by 405 V. Find its charge.

   r = √(9 × 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ × 1.2 × 10⁻⁴/(2 × 880 × 9.81)) = √(1.13 × 10⁻¹²) = 1.06 × 10⁻⁶ m. m = (4/3)π(1.06 × 10⁻⁶)³ × 880 = 4.39 × 10⁻¹⁵ kg. Q = mgd/V = 4.39 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 9.81 × 6.0 × 10⁻³/405 = 6.4 × 10⁻¹⁹ C = 4e.

### Common mistakes

- Forgetting that the drop must be at terminal speed for Stokes' law to give its radius.

## 3.12.2.1 Newton's corpuscular theory of light

*Refs: AQA 3.12.2.1 · Edexcel 90. Level: A-level only (Edexcel AS & A).*

### What you need to know

- Newton's corpuscular theory compared with Huygens' wave theory in general terms.
- Why Newton's theory was preferred.

### Explained

**Newton (corpuscles).** Light is a stream of tiny particles. Reflection: particles bounce elastically off a surface (angle of incidence = angle of reflection). Refraction: particles are attracted towards a denser medium as they reach the surface, so their velocity component perpendicular to the surface increases; they bend towards the normal and travel *faster* in the denser medium.

**Huygens (waves).** Light is a wave; each point on a wavefront is a source of secondary wavelets. Refraction happens because light travels *slower* in the denser medium, so wavefronts bend. Huygens's theory also predicted diffraction and interference.

**Why Newton's was preferred** (for about 100 years): Newton's great reputation (from his work on gravity and mechanics); light seemed to travel in straight lines with sharp shadows (diffraction of light is very small and hard to observe because λ is tiny); the speed of light in water could not yet be measured, so the two predictions could not be tested; the wave theory needed a medium (the "aether") to carry the waves through space; and Huygens's theory did not explain polarisation well at the time.

## 3.12.2.2 Significance of Young's double slits experiment

*Refs: AQA 3.12.2.2 · Eduqas C3.2(e). Level: A-level only (Eduqas AS & A).*

### What you need to know

- Explanation of the fringes in general terms (no calculations).
- The delayed acceptance of Huygens' wave theory.

### Explained

Young (1801) passed light from a single source through two narrow slits. Where light from the two slits overlapped, there were bright and dark fringes. Bright fringes: waves arrive in phase (constructive interference). Dark fringes: waves arrive in antiphase and cancel (destructive). Particles from two slits would simply add, giving two bright patches, never darkness where light from both arrives. Interference is a property of waves, so the experiment strongly supported the wave theory.

Acceptance was slow: Newton's authority, criticism of Young's work, and the lack of a measurement of the speed of light in water. It was only after Fresnel's mathematical wave theory explained diffraction (1810s) and Foucault and Fizeau showed (1850) that light is slower in water than in air, as the wave theory predicted, that the wave theory was fully accepted.

## 3.12.2.3 Electromagnetic waves

*Refs: AQA 3.12.2.3. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The nature of electromagnetic waves.
- Maxwell's formula c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀); μ₀ relates to the magnetic flux density from a current, ε₀ to the electric field of a charge.
- Hertz's discovery of radio waves, including measuring their speed.
- Fizeau's determination of the speed of light and its implications.

### Explained

**Maxwell (1860s).** A changing electric field produces a magnetic field and a changing magnetic field produces an electric field. These can sustain each other and travel through space as an electromagnetic wave: oscillating electric and magnetic fields, perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel (transverse). Maxwell calculated their speed:

c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) = 1/√(4π × 10⁻⁷ × 8.85 × 10⁻¹²) = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹

This matched the measured speed of light, so he concluded that light is an electromagnetic wave. ε₀ (permittivity of free space) sets the strength of electric fields from charges (Coulomb's law); μ₀ (permeability of free space) sets the strength of magnetic fields from currents.

**Hertz (1887).** Produced radio waves with a spark gap connected to an induction coil (high-voltage sparks oscillating at high frequency) and detected them with a loop of wire with a small gap, in which sparks appeared. He showed they reflect, refract, can be polarised (the detector only worked at certain orientations) and form stationary waves when reflected from a metal sheet. Measuring the node spacing gave λ; with the known frequency, v = fλ came out equal to c, supporting Maxwell.

**Fizeau (1849).** Light passed through a gap between the teeth of a rapidly rotating toothed wheel, travelled about 8.6 km to a mirror and back. At a certain speed of rotation, the returning light was blocked by the next tooth. The travel time 2d/c equals the time for the wheel to turn from a gap to a tooth: 1/(2Nf) for N teeth rotating at f revolutions per second. So c = 4dNf. He found c ≈ 3.1 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, close to Maxwell's prediction, which confirmed that light is an electromagnetic wave.

### Worked example

1. Fizeau's wheel had 720 teeth and the mirror was 8.63 km away. Light was first blocked at 12.6 revolutions per second. Find c.

   c = 4dNf = 4 × 8630 × 720 × 12.6 = 3.13 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

## 3.12.2.4 The discovery of photoelectricity

*Refs: AQA 3.12.2.4 · Edexcel 90. Level: A-level only (Edexcel AS & A).*

### What you need to know

- The ultraviolet catastrophe and black-body radiation.
- Planck's interpretation in terms of quanta.
- The failure of classical wave theory to explain photoelectricity.
- Einstein's explanation and its significance for the nature of electromagnetic radiation.

### Explained

**Ultraviolet catastrophe.** Classical wave theory predicted that a black body should emit more and more intensity at shorter wavelengths, without limit: an infinite total energy, concentrated in the ultraviolet. Experiments showed the intensity rises to a peak and then falls to zero at short wavelengths (file 20).

**Planck (1900).** He assumed that the energy of the oscillators emitting the radiation could only take values in steps (quanta) of E = hf. At high frequency, each quantum is large, so few oscillators have enough energy to emit, and the intensity drops. His formula matched experiment exactly. Planck saw this as a mathematical device, not a statement about light itself.

**Photoelectric effect** (file 03). Wave theory predicted that any frequency of light would eventually release electrons, and that brighter light would give faster electrons. Experiments showed a threshold frequency, instant emission, and kinetic energy depending only on frequency.

**Einstein (1905).** Light itself consists of quanta (photons), each of energy hf, and each photon is absorbed by one electron: hf = φ + Ek(max). This explained all the observations. Significance: electromagnetic radiation has a particle nature as well as a wave nature. Millikan's careful experiments (1916) confirmed Einstein's equation, and Einstein received the Nobel Prize for it.

## 3.12.2.5 Wave–particle duality

*Refs: AQA 3.12.2.5. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- De Broglie's hypothesis p = h/λ; λ = h/√(2meV) for an electron accelerated through V.
- Low-energy electron diffraction; qualitative effect of electron speed on the pattern.

### Explained

De Broglie (1924) proposed that if light waves have particle properties, particles should have wave properties, with λ = h/p. For an electron accelerated from rest through V: p = √(2meV), so λ = h/√(2meV).

Davisson and Germer (1927) fired low-energy electrons (tens of eV) at a nickel crystal and found strong reflection only at certain angles: a diffraction pattern, with the crystal lattice acting as a grating. Thomson (G.P.) passed electrons through thin metal foil and saw rings. At higher speeds (higher V), λ is shorter, so the diffraction angles are smaller and the pattern shrinks.

### Worked example

1. Find the de Broglie wavelength of electrons accelerated through 54 V (as in Davisson and Germer).

   λ = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴/√(2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 54) = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴/3.97 × 10⁻²⁴ = 1.67 × 10⁻¹⁰ m, similar to atomic spacing.

## 3.12.2.6 Electron microscopes

*Refs: AQA 3.12.2.6. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Estimating the anode voltage needed for wavelengths of the order of atomic size.
- The principle of the transmission electron microscope (TEM).
- The principle of the scanning tunnelling microscope (STM).

### Explained

The resolution of any microscope is limited by diffraction to about the wavelength used. Light (λ ≈ 500 nm) cannot resolve details smaller than about 200 nm. Electrons with a wavelength of about 0.1 nm could resolve atoms. From λ = h/√(2meV), λ = 0.1 nm needs V ≈ 150 V; in practice TEMs use 50 to 200 kV (much shorter λ, to penetrate the sample and reduce other limits).

**TEM.** An electron gun produces a beam in a vacuum. Magnetic lenses (coils whose fields bend electron paths) focus the beam:

1. A condenser lens forms a parallel beam on the thin sample.
2. Electrons pass through the sample; denser parts scatter more electrons, so fewer pass through.
3. An objective lens forms a magnified image, and a projector lens magnifies it further onto a fluorescent screen or detector.

Image detail is limited by: the sample thickness (electrons slow down in it, increasing λ), aberrations of the magnetic lenses, and variations in electron speed (so they are not all focused at the same point).

**STM.** A very sharp conducting tip (ending in one atom) is held about 1 nm above a conducting surface, with a small p.d. between them. Electrons cannot classically cross the gap, but because of their wave nature they can "tunnel" through it. The tunnelling current is extremely sensitive to the gap width (it falls exponentially as the gap grows). The tip is scanned across the surface by piezoelectric controls.

- *Constant height mode*: the tip stays at a fixed height; changes in current map the surface.
- *Constant current mode*: a feedback system moves the tip up and down to keep the current constant; the tip's height traces the surface.

It can image individual atoms (and move them).

### Common mistakes

- Saying a TEM uses glass lenses.
- Saying STM tunnelling depends on electrons hitting the surface.

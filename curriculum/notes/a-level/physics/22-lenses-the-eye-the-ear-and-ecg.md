# Lenses, the eye and its defects, the ear and hearing, and the ECG

This file covers converging and diverging lenses (focal length, power, the lens equation, magnification, ray diagrams), the eye as an optical system, rods and cones, defects of vision and their correction, the structure of the ear, intensity level in decibels, equal loudness curves, hearing loss, and the electrocardiogram.

## Overview

AQA option 3.10 (A-level only). The lens physics is also core for Edexcel (75 to 81) and OCR B (3.1.1), both at AS. OCR A and Eduqas do not examine lenses. Medical imaging and radionuclides are in file 23. The astronomical telescope is in file 20.

### Board differences

- AQA 3.10.1 to 3.10.3 · Edexcel 75 to 81 · OCR B 3.1.1(a)(i), (b)(i), (ii), (c)(ii) to (iv), (d)(i).
- Sign conventions differ. AQA and Edexcel use **real is positive**: 1/u + 1/v = 1/f, with distances to real objects and images positive, virtual negative; a diverging lens has a negative f. OCR B uses the **Cartesian** convention: 1/v = 1/u + 1/f, with distances measured from the lens and those to the left (object side) negative, and is limited to thin converging lenses forming real images. Both give the same physical answers.
- OCR B 3.1.1(a)(i), (c)(ii): a lens changes the curvature of wavefronts; power = 1/f = change in curvature.
- Edexcel 78: powers of thin lenses in contact add: P = P₁ + P₂ + ….
- The eye, ear and ECG are AQA only.

## 3.10.1.1 Physics of vision

*Refs: AQA 3.10.1.1. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The eye as an optical refracting system, with ray diagrams of image formation.
- Sensitivity of the eye; its spectral response as a photodetector.
- Spatial resolution of the eye, explained by the behaviour of rods and cones.

### Explained

**Optical system.** Light is refracted at the cornea (most of the refraction, about 40 D, because of the large change in refractive index from air), then by the lens (fine adjustment), and focused onto the retina, forming a real, inverted, diminished image. The ciliary muscles change the shape (thickness) of the lens to focus on objects at different distances (accommodation): relaxed muscles give a thin lens for distant objects; contracted muscles let the lens get fatter (more powerful) for near objects. The iris controls the pupil size and so the amount of light.

- Far point: the furthest point that can be seen clearly (infinity for a normal eye).
- Near point: the closest point that can be focused (about 25 cm for a normal adult).

**Rods and cones.**

| | Rods | Cones |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number | About 120 million | About 6 million |
| Where | Mostly outside the fovea (periphery) | Concentrated in the fovea (centre) |
| Sensitivity | Very sensitive; work in dim light | Need bright light |
| Colour | No; one type, peak sensitivity about 500 nm | Three types (peaks in blue ~ 440 nm, green ~ 540 nm, red ~ 570 nm), giving colour vision |
| Resolution | Poor: many rods share one nerve fibre | Good in the fovea: each cone has its own nerve fibre |

**Spectral response.** The eye detects 400 to 700 nm. In bright light (cones) the overall sensitivity peaks at about 555 nm (yellow-green). In dim light (rods) the peak shifts to about 510 nm, and we see no colour.

**Spatial resolution.** Two points can be seen as separate only if their images fall on two receptors with at least one unstimulated receptor between them, each connected to separate nerve fibres. In the fovea, cones are about 2 to 3 μm apart and each has its own nerve, so resolution is best there (about 1 arcminute, 3 × 10⁻⁴ rad). In dim light, rods are used, and many rods connect to one nerve fibre, so resolution is poorer (but sensitivity is higher, since signals from many rods add up).

### Common mistakes

- Saying the lens does most of the focusing (the cornea does).
- Saying rods detect colour.

## 3.10.1.2 Defects of vision and their correction using lenses

*Refs: AQA 3.10.1.2 · Edexcel 75 to 81 · OCR B 3.1.1(a)(i), (b)(i), (b)(ii), (c)(ii), (c)(iii), (c)(iv), (d)(i). Level: A-level only (AQA); AS & A (Edexcel, OCR B).*

### What you need to know

- Properties of converging and diverging lenses: principal focus, focal length and power.
- Power = 1/f (dioptres, D = m⁻¹); 1/u + 1/v = 1/f; magnification m = v/u (= image height ÷ object height).
- Real and virtual images (Edexcel 79).
- Powers of lenses in contact add (Edexcel 78).
- Myopia, hypermetropia and astigmatism; ray diagrams and calculations of the power of correcting lenses for myopia and hypermetropia; the format of prescriptions for astigmatism.

### Explained

**Converging lens** (convex): parallel rays converge to a real principal focus F on the far side. **Diverging lens** (concave): parallel rays spread out as if from a virtual principal focus on the near side. Focal length f = distance from the centre of the lens to F.

**Power** P = 1/f, in dioptres (D) when f is in metres. Converging lenses have positive power; diverging lenses negative power (real is positive). A fatter lens (more curved surfaces) has more power. Thin lenses in contact: P = P₁ + P₂ + … (Edexcel 78).

**Wavefronts** (OCR B). A lens changes the curvature of wavefronts. Curvature = 1/(radius of the wavefront). A converging lens of power P adds curvature P to every wavefront: plane wavefronts (curvature 0) leave with curvature 1/f, converging to the focus. So the lens equation can be read as: curvature out = curvature in + power of the lens. In the Cartesian form, 1/v = 1/u + 1/f.

**Ray diagrams.** Use two of these rays from the top of the object:

1. A ray parallel to the axis refracts through the principal focus (converging) or appears to come from the near focus (diverging).
2. A ray through the optical centre goes straight on.
3. A ray through the near focus (converging) emerges parallel to the axis.

Where the rays (or their backward extensions) meet is the top of the image.

**Real and virtual images.** A real image is formed where rays actually meet; it can be shown on a screen. A virtual image is where rays only appear to come from; it cannot be shown on a screen.

| Object position (converging lens) | Image |
| --- | --- |
| Beyond 2F | Real, inverted, diminished, between F and 2F (camera, eye) |
| At 2F | Real, inverted, same size, at 2F |
| Between F and 2F | Real, inverted, magnified, beyond 2F (projector) |
| At F | Image at infinity |
| Inside F | Virtual, upright, magnified, same side (magnifying glass) |

A diverging lens always gives a virtual, upright, diminished image.

**Lens equation** (real is positive): 1/u + 1/v = 1/f. u = object distance, v = image distance. A negative v means a virtual image. Magnification m = v/u = image height/object height.

**Defects of vision.**

*Myopia (short sight).* The eye is too powerful (or the eyeball too long), so distant objects focus in front of the retina. The far point is closer than infinity. Correction: a diverging lens (negative power) that makes parallel light appear to come from the far point, so f = −(far point distance).

*Hypermetropia (long sight).* The eye is not powerful enough (or the eyeball too short), so near objects focus behind the retina. The near point is further than 25 cm. Correction: a converging lens that forms a virtual image, at the person's near point, of an object at 25 cm: 1/0.25 + 1/(−near point) = 1/f.

*Astigmatism.* The cornea (or lens) is not spherical: it has different curvatures in different planes (like a rugby ball), so lines in one direction are in focus while lines at right angles are blurred. Correction: a cylindrical lens that adds power in one plane only, oriented at the correct angle.

**Prescriptions.** A prescription for each eye gives: the spherical power (D), the cylindrical power (D), and the axis (the angle, 0° to 180°, measured anticlockwise from the horizontal as seen by the optician facing the patient) of the cylindrical lens. For example: −2.00 / +1.25 × 90 means spherical −2.00 D, cylindrical +1.25 D, cylinder axis at 90°.

### Worked example

1. An object 0.30 m from a converging lens of focal length 0.20 m. Find the image position and magnification.

   1/v = 1/0.20 − 1/0.30 = 5 − 3.33 = 1.67; v = 0.60 m (real, on the far side). m = 0.60/0.30 = 2.0 (inverted, magnified).

2. An object 0.10 m from the same lens.

   1/v = 5 − 10 = −5; v = −0.20 m (virtual, same side as the object). m = 2.0 (upright).

3. A short-sighted person has a far point of 2.5 m. Find the power of the correcting lens.

   f = −2.5 m, P = −0.40 D.

4. A long-sighted person has a near point of 1.0 m. Find the power of the lens needed to read at 25 cm.

   u = 0.25 m, v = −1.0 m (virtual image at the near point). 1/f = 4 − 1 = 3; P = +3.0 D.

5. (Edexcel 78) A +4.0 D lens is in contact with a −1.5 D lens. Find the combined focal length.

   P = +2.5 D; f = 0.40 m.

6. (OCR B, Cartesian) An object is 0.30 m to the left of a lens of f = 0.20 m. Find v.

   u = −0.30 m. 1/v = 1/u + 1/f = −3.33 + 5 = 1.67; v = +0.60 m (to the right: real image). Same answer as example 1.

### Common mistakes

- Mixing sign conventions in one calculation.
- Correcting myopia with a converging lens.
- Giving power in D with f in cm.
- Saying a virtual image is upside down.

### Practical

**Focal length of a converging lens** (OCR B 3.1.1(d)(i), suggested by Edexcel). Use an illuminated object (cross-wires), the lens and a screen on a metre rule. For several object distances u, move the screen to find the sharpest image and measure v. Plot 1/v against 1/u: a straight line of gradient −1 with both intercepts equal to 1/f (real is positive). Quick estimate: focus a distant window onto a screen; the distance from the lens is f.

## 3.10.2.1 Ear as a sound detection system

*Refs: AQA 3.10.2.1. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The simple structure of the ear and how sound is transmitted.

### Explained

1. **Outer ear.** The pinna collects sound and funnels it along the auditory canal to the eardrum (tympanic membrane), which vibrates.
2. **Middle ear.** An air-filled cavity. Three tiny bones (ossicles: hammer/malleus, anvil/incus, stirrup/stapes) pass the vibrations to the oval window. They act as a lever (force multiplied about 1.5 times), and the eardrum's area is about 15 to 20 times the oval window's area, so the pressure is increased about 20 to 30 times. This matches the low acoustic impedance of air to the high impedance of the fluid in the inner ear (otherwise most sound would be reflected). The Eustachian tube equalises air pressure on both sides of the eardrum.
3. **Inner ear.** The oval window sets the fluid in the cochlea vibrating. The basilar membrane inside the cochlea has hair cells along it. Different positions resonate at different frequencies (high frequencies near the oval window, low frequencies at the far end). Moving hair cells generate nerve impulses sent along the auditory nerve to the brain.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the ossicles increase the amplitude of vibration (they increase force and pressure).

## 3.10.2.2 Sensitivity and frequency response

*Refs: AQA 3.10.2.2. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Producing and interpreting equal loudness curves.
- Human perception of relative intensity needs a logarithmic scale.
- Definition of intensity; intensity level = 10 log(I/I₀), with threshold of hearing I₀ = 1.0 × 10⁻¹² W m⁻².
- Measuring sound intensity levels; dB and dBA scales; relative intensity levels.

### Explained

**Intensity** is power per unit area: I = P/A (W m⁻²).

**Logarithmic response.** The ear can detect intensities from 10⁻¹² W m⁻² to about 1 W m⁻² (painful): a range of 10¹². Equal ratios of intensity sound like equal steps in loudness (doubling the intensity always sounds like a similar increase). So a log scale is used.

**Intensity level** (in decibels, dB) = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀). I₀ = 1.0 × 10⁻¹² W m⁻² is the threshold of hearing at 1 kHz (0 dB).

- An increase of 10 dB = 10 times the intensity; 3 dB ≈ 2 times; 20 dB = 100 times.
- Typical: whisper 30 dB; conversation 60 dB; busy traffic 80 dB; rock concert 110 dB; pain threshold 120 dB.

**Frequency response.** The ear hears about 20 Hz to 20 kHz, and is most sensitive at about 2 to 5 kHz (partly from resonance in the ear canal). Sounds of the same intensity at different frequencies do not sound equally loud.

**Equal loudness curves.** Each curve joins (frequency, intensity level) points that a listener judges as equally loud as a reference 1 kHz tone. The lowest curve is the threshold of hearing: it is lowest (most sensitive) around 3 kHz and rises steeply at low and high frequencies. Loudness is measured in phons (equal to the dB value at 1 kHz). Produced by playing a 1 kHz tone, then tones of other frequencies, and adjusting their intensity until the listener judges them equally loud.

**dBA scale.** A sound level meter with an A-weighting filter reduces its response at low and high frequencies to match the ear's sensitivity (based on the 40-phon curve). dBA readings indicate how loud sounds seem to people and their risk of damage, so they are used for noise regulations.

### Worked example

1. Find the intensity level of a sound of intensity 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ W m⁻².

   10 log(3.2 × 10⁻⁵/10⁻¹²) = 10 log(3.2 × 10⁷) = 75 dB.

2. One machine gives 80 dB. What level do four identical machines give?

   Intensity × 4: 80 + 10 log 4 = 86 dB.

### Common mistakes

- Adding decibels directly (two 80 dB sources give 83 dB, not 160 dB).
- Using I₀ as the threshold at every frequency.

## 3.10.2.3 Defects of hearing

*Refs: AQA 3.10.2.3. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- How equal loudness curves change with hearing loss from injury due to excessive noise, or from ageing.

### Explained

- **Age** (presbycusis): hearing loss is greatest at high frequencies and gets gradually worse. The threshold curve rises most at high frequencies; the upper frequency limit falls (from about 20 kHz to perhaps 10 kHz or less).
- **Noise exposure**: prolonged loud noise damages hair cells, especially those responding to the frequencies of the noise. The threshold curve shows a dip in sensitivity (a peak in the threshold) at those frequencies, often around 4 kHz for industrial noise, and the person needs a higher intensity to hear those frequencies. Sudden very loud sounds can also rupture the eardrum.

Hearing is tested by audiometry: the threshold is measured at each frequency and compared with a normal threshold curve; hearing loss is the difference in dB.

### Common mistakes

- Saying ageing causes hearing loss mainly at low frequencies.

## 3.10.3.1 Simple ECG machines and the normal ECG waveform

*Refs: AQA 3.10.3.1. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- How an ECG waveform is obtained.
- The characteristic shape of a normal ECG and what causes each part.

### Explained

**Principle.** Heart muscle contracts when its cells depolarise (ions move across cell membranes). These electrical changes spread through the body and cause small potential differences (about 1 mV) at the skin.

**Obtaining it.**

- Electrodes are placed on the skin (often on the arms, a leg and the chest). The skin is cleaned and shaved, and a conducting gel is used, to give good contact with low resistance (reducing the large skin impedance) and to exclude air.
- The patient lies still and relaxed, so signals from skeletal muscles do not interfere.
- The p.d.s between pairs of electrodes are amplified (high input impedance amplifier, so very little current is drawn) and displayed or recorded against time.

**The normal waveform** (one heartbeat, about 0.8 s at rest).

| Part | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| P wave | Small bump: the atria depolarise and contract |
| QRS complex | Large, sharp spike (Q small dip, R tall peak, S dip): the ventricles depolarise and contract (large muscle mass, so large signal); atrial repolarisation is hidden in it |
| T wave | Rounded bump: the ventricles repolarise (relax) |

The time between R peaks gives the heart rate (beats per minute = 60/R–R interval in s). Abnormal shapes or rhythms indicate heart problems.

### Worked example

1. On an ECG, R peaks are 21 mm apart and the chart moves at 25 mm s⁻¹. Find the heart rate.

   Period = 21/25 = 0.84 s; rate = 60/0.84 = 71 beats per minute.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the T wave is the ventricles contracting.
- Saying the electrodes detect the heart's sound.

# Cell recognition and the immune system

The immune system tells "self" from "non-self" and destroys pathogens. This file covers antigens, phagocytes, T and B lymphocytes, antibodies, vaccination, monoclonal antibodies, and HIV.

## 3.2.4 Cell recognition and the immune system

*Refs: AQA 3.2.4 · Edexcel A 6.7, 6.8, 6.9, 6.12 · Edexcel B 6.7 i to vii · OCR A 4.1.1(d) to (l) · OCR B 3.2.2(a) to (e), (g), (h), 3.2.3(a), (b), (d), 4.2.1(h) · Eduqas OA.3(a) to (j). Level: AS & A (AQA, OCR A, OCR B); A-level only (Edexcel A, Edexcel B, Eduqas).*

### What you need to know

- Each type of cell has specific molecules on its surface that identify it, including proteins that let the immune system identify pathogens, cells from other organisms of the same species, abnormal body cells and toxins.
- Definition of antigen; antigenic variability.
- Phagocytosis of pathogens; the subsequent destruction of ingested pathogens by lysozymes.
- The response of T lymphocytes to a foreign antigen (the cellular response): the role of antigen-presenting cells; helper T cells (TH) stimulating cytotoxic T cells (TC), B cells and phagocytes.
- The response of B lymphocytes (the humoral response): clonal selection; plasma cells releasing monoclonal antibodies; memory cells.
- The structure of an antibody; the formation of an antigen–antibody complex leading to destruction of the antigen, limited to agglutination and phagocytosis of bacterial cells.
- Primary and secondary immune responses.
- Active and passive immunity; vaccination; herd immunity; ethics of vaccines.
- Monoclonal antibodies: targeting medication to specific cell types; medical diagnosis; ELISA; ethics of their use.
- The structure of HIV and its replication in helper T cells; how HIV causes AIDS; why antibiotics do not work on viruses.

### Explained

**Antigens.** An antigen is a molecule, usually a protein or glycoprotein on a cell surface, that triggers an immune response. The immune system learns to ignore the body's own antigens (self) and responds to others (non-self). It responds to:

- pathogens (bacteria, viruses, fungi)
- cells from other individuals of the same species (transplant rejection)
- abnormal body cells (cancer cells, virus-infected cells displaying viral antigens)
- toxins.

**Non-specific defences first** (OCR A, OCR B, Edexcel A, Eduqas). Skin (keratin, a physical barrier with acidic oils); mucous membranes and cilia trapping and moving pathogens; stomach acid; lysozyme in tears; blood clotting sealing wounds; inflammation (histamine from mast cells widens vessels and makes them leaky, so more phagocytes and fluid reach the site); fever (higher temperature slows pathogen reproduction and speeds the immune response).

**Phagocytosis.**

1. Chemicals released by pathogens attract the phagocyte (chemotaxis).
2. Receptors on the phagocyte bind to the pathogen's antigens (helped when antibodies or opsonins coat the pathogen).
3. The phagocyte engulfs the pathogen, enclosing it in a vesicle called a phagosome.
4. Lysosomes fuse with the phagosome and release lysozymes, which hydrolyse the pathogen.
5. The phagocyte displays the pathogen's antigens on its surface. It is now an antigen-presenting cell.

Neutrophils are short-lived, lobed-nucleus phagocytes that arrive first. Macrophages are larger, live longer, and are the main antigen-presenting cells.

**The cellular (cell-mediated) response.** T lymphocytes have receptors that bind one specific antigen when it is presented on another cell.

1. A helper T cell with the complementary receptor binds to the antigen on an antigen-presenting cell.
2. The helper T cell is activated and divides by mitosis to make a clone.
3. Clones of helper T cells: release cytokines that stimulate cytotoxic T cells to divide; stimulate B cells to divide and make antibodies; stimulate phagocytes to engulf pathogens; some become memory T cells.
4. Cytotoxic (killer) T cells bind to infected body cells showing the antigen and release perforin, a protein that makes holes in the cell membrane, so the cell dies.

**The humoral response.** B lymphocytes each have antibody molecules on their surface that bind one specific antigen.

1. A B cell's antibody binds to its complementary antigen; the B cell takes the antigen in and presents it.
2. A helper T cell binds to the presented antigen and activates the B cell.
3. The B cell divides by mitosis (clonal selection and expansion).
4. Most daughter cells become plasma cells, which secrete large amounts of one antibody (monoclonal). Some become memory B cells.

Plasma cells live only a few days; memory cells last for decades.

**Antibody structure.** Four polypeptide chains (two heavy, two light) held by disulfide bridges in a Y shape. Each tip has a variable region whose shape is complementary to one antigen: the antigen-binding site. The rest is the constant region, the same for all antibodies of one class, which binds to receptors on phagocytes. A hinge region lets the arms move to bind two antigens at once.

**What antibodies do.**

- Agglutination: each antibody binds two pathogens, clumping them together so phagocytes find and engulf them more easily.
- Marking for phagocytosis: antibodies bound to a pathogen act as markers that phagocytes recognise. (OCR A: antibodies that do this are opsonins.)
- Neutralising toxins (antitoxins, OCR A) and blocking viral attachment proteins.

**Primary and secondary responses.**

| | Primary | Secondary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When | First exposure | Later exposure to the same antigen |
| Speed | Slow: few B and T cells match the antigen; they must be selected and cloned | Fast: many memory cells divide quickly |
| Antibody level | Low | Much higher, made sooner |
| Symptoms | Usually | Usually none: pathogen destroyed before it causes disease |

**Antigenic variability.** Some pathogens (influenza virus, HIV) mutate often, changing the shape of their antigens. Memory cells from an earlier infection no longer match, so a new primary response is needed and people catch the disease again. This is why a new flu vaccine is needed each year.

**Active and passive immunity.**

| | Active | Passive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How | Body makes its own antibodies after exposure to an antigen | Antibodies given from outside |
| Natural example | Catching a disease | Antibodies across the placenta; in breast milk |
| Artificial example | Vaccination | Injection of antibodies (antitetanus, antivenom) |
| Memory cells | Yes, long-lasting | No; antibodies broken down in weeks |
| Speed | Slow to develop | Immediate |

**Vaccination.** A vaccine contains antigens (dead or weakened pathogens, isolated antigens, or mRNA that makes cells produce the antigen). It causes a primary response, so memory cells form without the disease. On real infection, the secondary response is fast.

**Herd immunity.** When a large proportion of a population is immune, the pathogen cannot spread easily, so people who cannot be vaccinated (babies, people with weak immune systems) are protected too.

**Ethics of vaccines.** Testing on animals; testing on human volunteers who may suffer side effects; deciding who gets limited supplies; compulsory versus voluntary vaccination; the risk to the whole population when many people refuse (Edexcel B 6.7 vii, Eduqas OA.3(j)).

**Monoclonal antibodies** are many identical antibodies, all with the same binding site.

- *Targeted drugs*: a drug attached to an antibody that binds an antigen found only on cancer cells reaches those cells and harms fewer healthy cells. Some antibodies block a receptor on cancer cells directly.
- *Diagnosis*: pregnancy tests detect hCG (OCR B 4.2.1(h)). The test strip has mobile antibodies with coloured beads that bind hCG; the complex moves up the strip and is caught by fixed antibodies, forming a coloured line. A control line shows the test worked.
- *ELISA* (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) detects an antigen or antibody in a sample:
  1. The antigen (for example from HIV) is fixed to the bottom of a well.
  2. The patient's blood is added. If it contains antibodies to the antigen, they bind.
  3. The well is washed to remove unbound antibodies.
  4. A second antibody, with an enzyme attached, that binds to human antibodies is added; the well is washed again.
  5. A substrate is added. If the enzyme is present, it turns the substrate into a coloured product.
  Colour means the patient had the antibodies. Washing matters: unbound enzyme-linked antibody would give a colour even with no patient antibody (a false positive).
- *Ethics*: making monoclonal antibodies uses mice; trials of new monoclonal drugs carry risk to volunteers.

**HIV.** HIV has: an RNA genome (two strands) and the enzyme reverse transcriptase inside a protein capsid; a lipid envelope from the host cell; and attachment proteins (gp120) in the envelope.

Replication:

1. The attachment protein binds to the CD4 receptor on a helper T cell.
2. The capsid is released into the cell; it uncoats, releasing RNA and reverse transcriptase.
3. Reverse transcriptase makes a DNA copy of the viral RNA.
4. The DNA is inserted into the host cell's DNA.
5. The cell transcribes the viral DNA and makes viral proteins; new viruses assemble and bud off, taking some of the cell membrane as an envelope.

AIDS: HIV destroys helper T cells. With few helper T cells, B cells and cytotoxic T cells are not activated, so the body cannot fight other infections (opportunistic infections such as TB and pneumonia), and these cause death.

**Antibiotics** work by targeting bacterial structures and enzymes (murein cell walls, 70S ribosomes). Viruses have none of these and replicate inside host cells, so antibiotics do not affect them.

**Autoimmune disease** (OCR A). The immune system attacks the body's own antigens. Examples: type 1 diabetes (attacks β cells of the pancreas), rheumatoid arthritis (joints), lupus. Treated with immunosuppressant drugs.

**Allergies** (OCR B). An exaggerated immune response to a harmless antigen (allergen), such as pollen. B cells make IgE antibodies that attach to mast cells; on the next exposure the allergen binds, and mast cells release histamine, causing inflammation. Anaphylaxis is a severe, body-wide response treated with adrenaline.

### Worked example

A vaccine is 90% effective. A population of 50 000 people has 80% vaccine uptake. How many people would you expect to be protected by the vaccine?

1. Vaccinated = 0.80 × 50 000 = 40 000.
2. Protected = 0.90 × 40 000 = 36 000.

Answer: 36 000, which is 72% of the population.

### Common mistakes

- Saying antibodies "kill" pathogens. They bind and cause agglutination or mark them for phagocytes.
- Saying memory cells "remember the disease". They have receptors complementary to the antigen and divide rapidly on re-exposure.
- Saying the antigen and antibody are "the same shape".
- Saying vaccines contain antibodies (that is passive immunity).
- Saying HIV "is AIDS". HIV is the virus; AIDS is the condition that results.
- Saying antibiotics can kill viruses.
- Writing "T cells make antibodies". Plasma cells (from B cells) do.

# Cell membranes and transport across them

Every cell is wrapped in a membrane that controls what enters and leaves. This file covers the fluid mosaic model and the five ways substances cross membranes, with AQA required practicals 3 and 4.

## 3.2.3 Transport across cell membranes

*Refs: AQA 3.2.3, RP3, RP4 · Edexcel A 2.2, 2.3, 2.4, CP3 · Edexcel B 4.2 i to v, CP5, CP6 · OCR A 2.1.5(a) to (e) · OCR B 2.1.1(j) to (n), 2.1.2(g), (h) · Eduqas CC3(a) to (c). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The basic structure of all cell membranes, including cell-surface membranes and the membranes around organelles, is the same.
- The arrangement of phospholipids, proteins, glycoproteins and glycolipids in the fluid mosaic model. Cholesterol may also be present.
- Movement across membranes by simple diffusion (the limits set by the phospholipid bilayer), facilitated diffusion (channel and carrier proteins), osmosis (water potential), active transport (carrier proteins and ATP) and co-transport (sodium ions and glucose).
- How surface area, number of channel or carrier proteins and differences in gradients of concentration or water potential affect the rate of movement.
- How cells may be adapted for rapid transport across their internal or external membranes by more surface area or more channel and carrier proteins.
- Explain the adaptations of specialised cells for rapid transport; use the fluid mosaic model to explain the properties of membranes.

### Explained

**The fluid mosaic model.**

- A phospholipid bilayer about 7 nm thick: hydrophilic heads outward, hydrophobic tails inward.
- Fluid: phospholipids and many proteins move about within their layer.
- Mosaic: proteins scattered through it like tiles. Intrinsic proteins span the bilayer (channels, carriers); extrinsic proteins sit on one surface (receptors, enzymes).
- Glycoproteins and glycolipids (with carbohydrate chains on the outside) act as receptors and antigens for cell recognition, and help cells stick together.
- Cholesterol sits between phospholipids. It binds to the tails, reducing their movement, which makes the membrane less fluid at high temperatures and stops it becoming too rigid at low temperatures. It also reduces leakage of water and ions.

**How the model was developed** (Edexcel A). In the 1930s the Davson–Danielli model proposed a lipid bilayer sandwiched between two continuous layers of protein. Electron micrographs showed a "railway track" appearance that seemed to fit. Later evidence did not: membranes from different cells had different protein amounts; freeze-fracture images showed proteins embedded within the bilayer; and labelled proteins on fused cells spread across the whole new membrane in minutes, showing they move. Singer and Nicolson proposed the fluid mosaic model in 1972 to fit this evidence. This is an example of a model being changed as new evidence appears.

**Why the bilayer is a barrier.** Small, non-polar molecules (oxygen, carbon dioxide, steroid hormones) dissolve in the hydrophobic core and diffuse straight through. Ions and large polar molecules (glucose, amino acids) cannot, because the hydrophobic tails repel them. They need proteins.

**The five mechanisms.**

| Mechanism | Needs ATP? | Proteins? | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Simple diffusion | No | No | Down the concentration gradient |
| Facilitated diffusion | No | Channel or carrier | Down the gradient |
| Osmosis | No | Often aquaporins | Water from higher to lower water potential |
| Active transport | Yes | Carrier proteins | Against the gradient |
| Co-transport | Indirectly | Co-transporter | One substance down its gradient drives another against its gradient |

*Channel proteins* form water-filled pores for specific ions; some are gated (open only in response to voltage or a chemical). *Carrier proteins* bind a specific molecule and change shape to carry it across.

**Water potential (ψ)** is the tendency of water to move out of a solution. Pure water has ψ = 0 kPa, the highest possible. Adding solutes lowers ψ, so solutions have negative values. Water moves by osmosis from a higher (less negative) to a lower (more negative) water potential across a partially permeable membrane.

- Animal cells in a solution of higher ψ gain water and may burst (haemolysis in red blood cells). In lower ψ they lose water and shrink (crenate).
- Plant cells in higher ψ gain water and become turgid, but the wall stops them bursting. In lower ψ they lose water; the protoplast pulls away from the wall (plasmolysis).

**Active transport.** A molecule binds to the carrier on one side; ATP is hydrolysed and the phosphate attaches to the carrier, changing its shape; the molecule is released on the other side. Example: the sodium–potassium pump moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per ATP.

**Co-transport in the ileum.** Sodium ions are actively pumped out of the epithelial cell into the blood, keeping the Na⁺ concentration inside the cell low. Na⁺ then diffuses into the cell from the gut lumen through a co-transporter protein, carrying glucose (or an amino acid) with it, even against glucose's own gradient. Glucose then leaves the other side of the cell by facilitated diffusion into the blood.

**Bulk transport** (OCR, Edexcel, Eduqas). Endocytosis takes in large particles or fluids by folding the membrane around them into a vesicle (phagocytosis for solids; pinocytosis for liquids). Exocytosis releases substances when a vesicle fuses with the membrane. Both need ATP.

**Factors affecting rate.**

- Simple diffusion: steeper gradient, larger surface area, thinner membrane, higher temperature → faster (Fick's law: rate ∝ surface area × difference in concentration ÷ thickness).
- Facilitated diffusion and active transport: also limited by the number of channel or carrier proteins. When all carriers are in use, raising the concentration has no further effect.
- Adaptations: microvilli on epithelial cells of the ileum and proximal tubule give a large surface area; these cells also have many carrier proteins and many mitochondria for active transport.

**Effect of temperature and solvents on permeability.** Above about 40 °C the phospholipids move more and membrane proteins denature, leaving gaps; the membrane becomes much more permeable. Ethanol and other organic solvents dissolve the lipids, also increasing permeability.

### Worked example

1. Cell X has ψ = −400 kPa and cell Y has ψ = −650 kPa. Which way does water move?

From X to Y, because X has the higher (less negative) water potential.

2. Potato cylinders were put in sucrose solutions. Percentage change in mass: 0.0 mol dm⁻³: +14%; 0.2: +6%; 0.4: −3%; 0.6: −11%. Estimate the concentration with no change in mass and explain what it shows.

Plot % change against concentration and read where the line crosses zero: between 0.2 and 0.4, roughly 0.33 mol dm⁻³ (by linear interpolation: 0.2 + 0.2 × 6/9 = 0.33). At this concentration the solution has the same water potential as the potato cells. Its value can then be read from a table that converts sucrose concentration to water potential.

3. Percentage change: start mass 2.50 g, end mass 2.85 g. Change = 0.35 g; 0.35 ÷ 2.50 × 100 = +14%. Use percentage change because cylinders start at slightly different masses.

### Common mistakes

- Saying water moves "from a high to a low concentration". Use water potential.
- Saying water potential "increases" when more solute is added. It decreases (becomes more negative).
- Saying plant cells burst in pure water.
- Saying active transport uses "energy from ATP" with no mention of carrier proteins changing shape.
- Saying facilitated diffusion needs ATP.
- Saying co-transport moves sodium against its gradient. Sodium moves down its gradient; glucose may move against its gradient.

### Practical

**AQA required practical 3** (also Edexcel B CP6, OCR A PAG8 / 2.1.5(e), OCR B 2.1.2(h)): produce a dilution series of a solute to find the water potential of a plant tissue.

1. Make a dilution series of sucrose (or salt) from a 1.0 mol dm⁻³ stock: for 10 cm³ of 0.4 mol dm⁻³ use 4 cm³ stock + 6 cm³ distilled water.
2. Cut equal cylinders of potato with a cork borer, blot dry, weigh each.
3. Put one cylinder in each concentration for the same time (for example 30 minutes or overnight) at the same temperature.
4. Remove, blot gently the same way, reweigh. Calculate percentage change in mass.
5. Plot percentage change against concentration; the x-intercept is the concentration with the same ψ as the cells.

Control variables: size and surface area of the cylinders, same potato (same variety), time, temperature, volume of solution, blotting method. Repeat at each concentration.

**AQA required practical 4** (also Edexcel A CP3, Edexcel B CP5, OCR A PAG8 / 2.1.5(c), OCR B 2.1.1(m)): investigate the effect of a named variable on the permeability of cell-surface membranes.

1. Cut equal pieces of beetroot and rinse them to remove pigment from damaged cells.
2. Put each piece in the same volume of water at a different temperature (or in different ethanol concentrations) for the same time.
3. Remove the beetroot. Measure the absorbance of the coloured liquid with a colorimeter (blue-green filter, zeroed with distilled water).
4. Higher absorbance means more betalain pigment has leaked out of the vacuoles, so the tonoplast and cell-surface membrane are more permeable.

Explanation of results: above a threshold temperature proteins denature and phospholipids move more, so pigment leaks out; ethanol dissolves lipids. Risks: ethanol is flammable; hot water; sharp knives.

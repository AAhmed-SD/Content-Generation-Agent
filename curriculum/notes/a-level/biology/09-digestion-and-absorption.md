# Digestion and absorption

Large food molecules must be hydrolysed into small ones before they can be absorbed into the blood. This file covers the enzymes of mammalian digestion and how the products cross the ileum wall.

## 3.3.3 Digestion and absorption

*Refs: AQA 3.3.3 · Eduqas C3.3(g), (h). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- During digestion, large biological molecules are hydrolysed to smaller molecules that can be absorbed across cell membranes.
- Digestion in mammals of:
  - carbohydrates by amylases and membrane-bound disaccharidases
  - lipids by lipase, including the action of bile salts
  - proteins by endopeptidases, exopeptidases and membrane-bound dipeptidases.
- Mechanisms for the absorption of the products of digestion by cells lining the ileum of mammals, including co-transport mechanisms for monosaccharides and amino acids, and the role of micelles in the absorption of lipids.

### Explained

**Carbohydrates.**

1. Salivary amylase in the mouth begins hydrolysing starch to maltose (breaking α-1,4 glycosidic bonds).
2. Amylase stops working in the acidic stomach.
3. Pancreatic amylase, secreted into the duodenum, continues hydrolysing starch to maltose.
4. Membrane-bound disaccharidases on the epithelial cells of the ileum hydrolyse disaccharides:
   - maltase: maltose → glucose + glucose
   - sucrase: sucrose → glucose + fructose
   - lactase: lactose → glucose + galactose.

Because these enzymes are fixed in the membrane, the monosaccharides are released right next to the transport proteins that absorb them.

**Lipids.**

1. Bile, made in the liver and stored in the gall bladder, is released into the duodenum. Bile salts emulsify lipids: they break large fat droplets into many small droplets, giving a much larger surface area for lipase to act on. Bile salts do not break bonds.
2. Pancreatic lipase hydrolyses the ester bonds in triglycerides, producing monoglycerides and fatty acids.
3. Bile salts, monoglycerides and fatty acids form tiny clusters called micelles.

**Proteins.**

- Endopeptidases hydrolyse peptide bonds within a polypeptide, making many shorter chains with more ends. Example: pepsin in the stomach (works at about pH 2); trypsin from the pancreas.
- Exopeptidases hydrolyse peptide bonds at the ends of polypeptides, removing single amino acids.
- Membrane-bound dipeptidases on the ileum epithelium hydrolyse dipeptides into two amino acids.

Working together, endopeptidases increase the number of ends for exopeptidases to act on, so digestion is faster.

**Absorption in the ileum.** The ileum is adapted with folds, villi and microvilli (a huge surface area), a thin epithelium one cell thick, a good blood supply in each villus (keeping glucose and amino acid concentrations low in the blood) and a lacteal (lymph vessel) in each villus for lipids. Epithelial cells have many mitochondria for active transport.

- *Glucose and galactose* (and amino acids): co-transport with sodium ions. Na⁺ is actively pumped out of the epithelial cell into the blood by the sodium–potassium pump. This keeps Na⁺ inside the cell low. Na⁺ diffuses from the lumen into the cell through a co-transporter protein, which carries glucose in at the same time, against its concentration gradient. Glucose moves out of the cell into the blood by facilitated diffusion. Amino acids are absorbed by a similar Na⁺-dependent co-transport.
- *Fructose* is absorbed by facilitated diffusion.
- *Lipids:* micelles carry monoglycerides and fatty acids to the epithelial cell surface. Micelles constantly break up and re-form, releasing monoglycerides and fatty acids, which are non-polar and diffuse across the membrane. Inside the cell they move to the smooth ER, where they are recombined into triglycerides. In the Golgi apparatus, triglycerides are combined with cholesterol and lipoproteins to form chylomicrons. Chylomicrons leave the cell by exocytosis and enter the lacteals, travelling in the lymph before reaching the blood.

**Gut wall and other regions** (Eduqas). The gut wall has four layers: mucosa (epithelium, with glands); submucosa (blood and lymph vessels); muscularis externa (circular and longitudinal muscle for peristalsis); serosa (outer connective tissue). The stomach makes hydrochloric acid (kills microorganisms, gives the low pH for pepsin) and pepsin; mucus protects the stomach wall. In the duodenum, pancreatic juice (containing amylase, lipase, trypsin and hydrogencarbonate ions that neutralise acid) and bile are added. The colon absorbs water.

### Worked example

Lactase activity was measured in four people. A sample of lactose solution was incubated with each person's intestinal enzyme for 10 minutes, and glucose was measured: A 2.4, B 0.3, C 2.1, D 0.2 mmol dm⁻³. Interpret.

B and D produced very little glucose, so they make little lactase. They would hydrolyse little lactose; it would stay in the gut lumen, lowering the water potential there and drawing water into the gut, and gut bacteria would ferment it. These are the symptoms of lactose intolerance.

### Common mistakes

- Saying bile salts or bile "digest fats". They emulsify; lipase hydrolyses.
- Saying fatty acids enter cells by co-transport. They are lipid-soluble and diffuse.
- Calling disaccharidases "secreted into the lumen". They are bound to the membrane of the epithelial cells.
- Saying endopeptidases make "amino acids". They make shorter polypeptides.
- Saying glucose is absorbed into the lacteal. Monosaccharides and amino acids go into the blood capillaries; lipids go into the lacteals.

## Check questions

1. Name the products of the hydrolysis of lactose. (1 mark)
2. Explain the role of bile salts in the digestion and absorption of lipids. (3 marks)
3. Explain why the combined action of endopeptidases and exopeptidases is faster than exopeptidases alone. (2 marks)
4. Describe how amino acids are absorbed by co-transport into an ileum epithelial cell. (4 marks)
5. Describe what happens to monoglycerides and fatty acids after they enter an epithelial cell. (3 marks)
6. A drug inhibits the sodium–potassium pump in ileum epithelial cells. Explain how it would affect glucose absorption. (3 marks)
7. Explain why disaccharidases are bound to the cell-surface membranes of ileum epithelial cells. (2 marks)

## Mark scheme

1. Glucose and galactose (1).
2. Emulsify lipids into smaller droplets, increasing surface area for lipase (1); form micelles with monoglycerides and fatty acids (1); carry them to the epithelium / keep them in solution near the surface (1).
3. Endopeptidases hydrolyse bonds within polypeptides, making more ends (1); more ends for exopeptidases to act on (1).
4. Sodium ions actively transported out of cell into blood (1); lower Na⁺ concentration inside cell than in lumen (1); Na⁺ and amino acid bind to co-transporter (1); Na⁺ diffuses in, carrying amino acid against its gradient (1).
5. Move to smooth ER and recombined into triglycerides (1); combined with protein / cholesterol in the Golgi to form chylomicrons (1); leave by exocytosis into lacteal / lymph (1).
6. Na⁺ not pumped out, so Na⁺ concentration in cell rises (1); no Na⁺ gradient from lumen into cell (1); less glucose enters by co-transport (1).
7. Monosaccharides released close to the co-transporter / transport proteins (1); so maintains concentration gradient / fast absorption (1).

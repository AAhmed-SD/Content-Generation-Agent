# Genetic disorders, chromosome mutations, screening and antenatal care

This file covers cystic fibrosis as a model genetic disorder, chromosome mutations (non-disjunction and translocation) and karyotypes, care and screening during pregnancy (diet, alcohol, smoking, ultrasound, amniocentesis, chorionic villus sampling), genetic screening and counselling, and the ethical issues they raise.

## Cystic fibrosis

*Refs: Edexcel A 2.14. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- How the expression of a gene mutation in people with cystic fibrosis impairs the functioning of the gaseous exchange, digestive and reproductive systems.

### Explained

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is caused by recessive alleles of the **CFTR** gene, which codes for a chloride ion channel protein in the cell-surface membranes of epithelial cells. The most common mutation deletes three bases, removing one amino acid (phenylalanine); the protein misfolds and is broken down, so few channels reach the membrane. Inheritance is autosomal recessive: affected people are homozygous (ff); carriers (Ff) are healthy.

**Normal CFTR:** in airway epithelium, CFTR lets chloride ions out of cells onto the surface; sodium follows; this lowers the water potential of the surface fluid, so water moves out of the cells by osmosis. The mucus stays thin and runny and cilia move it away.

**In CF:** chloride ions are not secreted and sodium is absorbed more, so water moves into the cells by osmosis. Mucus becomes thick and sticky.

| System | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Gas exchange | Cilia cannot move the thick mucus; it blocks bronchioles; less air reaches alveoli; bacteria trapped in mucus cause repeated infections, which damage lung tissue; less surface area and longer diffusion distance |
| Digestive | Mucus blocks the pancreatic duct, so less pancreatic enzyme reaches the duodenum; food is poorly digested; mucus on the gut lining reduces absorption; poor growth. The pancreas may become damaged (CF-related diabetes) |
| Reproductive | Men: sperm ducts blocked or absent, so most are infertile. Women: thick cervical mucus reduces fertility |

**Treatment:** physiotherapy to loosen mucus; antibiotics; mucus-thinning drugs; enzyme capsules with meals; modulator drugs that help faulty CFTR reach the membrane and open; lung transplants; gene therapy is under research.

## Chromosome mutations and karyotypes

*Refs: Edexcel B 2.3 vi, vii · OCR B 3.1.2(i), 5.1.1(f) · Eduqas C2.5(f). Level: AS & A (Edexcel B, OCR B); A-level only (Eduqas).*

### What you need to know

- Chromosome mutations, as illustrated by translocations.
- How non-disjunction leads to polysomy (an extra chromosome), including Down's syndrome.
- The production and use of karyotypes.

### Explained

**Chromosome mutations** change the structure or number of chromosomes.

- **Translocation:** part of one chromosome breaks off and attaches to another (often a non-homologous chromosome). In a *balanced* translocation all the genetic material is present and the person is usually healthy, but their gametes may have too much or too little of a chromosome, leading to miscarriage or disorders in children. Some cancers (such as a form of leukaemia, where parts of chromosomes 9 and 22 swap, making a fused oncogene) are caused by translocations in body cells.
- **Non-disjunction:** homologous chromosomes (meiosis I) or sister chromatids (meiosis II) fail to separate, so a gamete has one extra or one missing chromosome (file 13).
  - **Down's syndrome:** trisomy 21 (three copies of chromosome 21, 47 chromosomes). Effects include learning disability, characteristic facial features, heart defects. The risk rises with the mother's age, because oocytes have been paused in meiosis I for decades.
  - **Turner's syndrome:** 45, X (one X chromosome, no second sex chromosome) in females: short stature, infertile.
  - **Klinefelter's syndrome:** 47, XXY in males.
  - **Polysomy** means extra copies of a chromosome; **polyploidy** (whole extra sets) is common in plants.

**Karyotypes.** A karyotype is an image of a person's chromosomes arranged in pairs by size, banding pattern and centromere position.

1. Obtain cells that are dividing (white blood cells, or fetal cells from amniocentesis or CVS; cultured to increase numbers).
2. Add colchicine, which stops spindle formation, so cells stop at metaphase, when chromosomes are most condensed.
3. Put cells in a hypotonic (dilute) solution so they swell and chromosomes spread out; fix and stain (giving bands); photograph.
4. Arrange chromosomes into 22 pairs of autosomes plus the sex chromosomes.

Karyotypes show extra or missing chromosomes, large deletions and translocations, and the sex of the fetus. They cannot show single gene mutations.

## Antenatal care and screening in pregnancy

*Refs: OCR B 3.1.2(d) to (h) · Edexcel A 2.15. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The programme of antenatal care in the United Kingdom.
- Dietary changes recommended during pregnancy.
- The effects of alcohol consumption and smoking during pregnancy.
- Monitoring fetal growth; screening tests.
- Amniocentesis and chorionic villus sampling: methods, advantages and disadvantages.

### Explained

**Antenatal care in the UK** (OCR B). Regular appointments with midwives and doctors: checks of the mother's blood pressure (high blood pressure can signal pre-eclampsia), urine (protein, glucose for gestational diabetes), weight; blood tests (blood group and rhesus factor, anaemia, infections such as HIV, hepatitis B and syphilis); ultrasound scans (dating at about 12 weeks; anomaly scan at about 20 weeks); screening for Down's syndrome and other conditions; advice on diet, smoking, alcohol and exercise; offered vaccines (whooping cough, flu).

**Diet in pregnancy.**

- **Folic acid** supplements before conception and for the first 12 weeks: lowers the risk of neural tube defects such as spina bifida.
- **Vitamin D** supplements (bones; calcium absorption).
- Enough iron (more blood is made; anaemia is common) and calcium (fetal skeleton).
- Avoid: too much vitamin A (liver; can harm the fetus); unpasteurised dairy and undercooked meat (*Listeria*, *Toxoplasma*); certain fish high in mercury; limit caffeine.
- Only a small increase in energy intake is needed, mostly in the last 3 months.

**Alcohol.** Alcohol crosses the placenta, and the fetal liver cannot break it down well. It can cause miscarriage, low birth weight and **fetal alcohol spectrum disorders** (FASD): learning difficulties, behavioural problems, distinctive facial features, poor growth. UK advice: no alcohol in pregnancy.

**Smoking.** Carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin, reducing oxygen supply to the fetus; nicotine narrows blood vessels, reducing blood flow through the placenta. Effects: low birth weight, premature birth, miscarriage, stillbirth, higher risk of sudden infant death. Stopping smoking at any stage helps.

**Monitoring fetal growth.** Ultrasound measurements (head circumference, abdominal circumference, femur length) compared with growth charts; measuring the height of the uterus (fundal height) at appointments. Poor growth may show placenta problems.

**Screening and diagnostic tests.** *Screening* tests give a risk; *diagnostic* tests give a definite answer.

- **Combined test** (screening, 10 to 14 weeks): ultrasound measurement of fluid at the back of the fetal neck (nuchal translucency) plus blood tests for hormones (hCG and a placental protein), combined with the mother's age to give a risk of Down's syndrome and some other trisomies.
- **Non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT):** analyses fragments of fetal DNA in the mother's blood; more accurate screening, no risk to the fetus.
- If the risk is high, a diagnostic test is offered:

| | Amniocentesis | Chorionic villus sampling (CVS) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When | 15 to 20 weeks | 11 to 14 weeks |
| Method | Needle through the abdomen, guided by ultrasound, into the amniotic sac; a sample of amniotic fluid containing fetal cells is withdrawn | Small sample of placental tissue (chorionic villi) taken through the abdomen or the cervix |
| Advantages | Slightly lower miscarriage risk (about 0.5%) | Earlier, so decisions can be made sooner |
| Disadvantages | Later in pregnancy; results take up to 2 to 3 weeks if cells are cultured | Slightly higher miscarriage risk (about 1%); placental cells occasionally differ from fetal cells |

Both give cells for karyotyping or DNA analysis (CF, sickle cell, and others). Both carry a risk of miscarriage and infection.

**Pre-implantation genetic diagnosis (PGD)** (Edexcel A 2.15). During IVF, one cell is removed from an early embryo and tested; only embryos without the disorder are implanted. Avoids later termination, but requires IVF, is expensive, and has ethical questions about selecting embryos.

**Carrier testing.** Testing relatives of affected people (or whole groups with a high frequency of an allele) to see if they are heterozygous carriers, so couples know the chance of an affected child.

## Genetic screening ethics and counselling

*Refs: Edexcel A 2.15, 2.16 · OCR B 5.1.1(g). Level: AS & A (Edexcel A); A-level only (OCR B).*

### What you need to know

- The social and ethical issues of genetic screening, from different viewpoints.
- The role of the genetic counsellor and the ethical considerations of genetic testing.

### Explained

**Ethical frameworks.** Decisions can be judged by:

- *Rights and duties:* people have a right to know (or not to know) their genetic information; there may be a duty to inform relatives.
- *Utilitarianism:* the right choice gives the greatest good for the greatest number.
- *Autonomy:* respecting people's informed choices.
- *Virtue ethics:* what a person of good character would do.

**Issues.**

- Results may cause anxiety; false positives may lead to unnecessary decisions; false negatives give false reassurance.
- Prenatal diagnosis may lead to a decision about whether to continue a pregnancy; some people and religious views object to terminations, especially for conditions that can be managed; others value the information to prepare.
- Who should be tested, and who decides; consent for children.
- Confidentiality: could insurers or employers use results? Should relatives be told?
- Concerns about "designer babies" and attitudes to disability.
- Cost and access across the population.

**The genetic counsellor** (OCR B). A trained professional who: explains how a condition is inherited and the chance of passing it on; explains what tests are available, how accurate they are and their risks; helps people understand results; discusses options (prenatal testing, PGD, adoption, using donor gametes, or accepting the risk) without directing a choice (non-directive counselling); offers emotional support and links to support groups; considers implications for other family members.

### Worked example

Two carriers of cystic fibrosis (Ff × Ff) plan a family. What is the probability that their first child has CF? That a child with no symptoms is a carrier?

1. Offspring: FF : Ff : ff = 1 : 2 : 1. P(CF) = 1/4.
2. Among unaffected children (FF, Ff, Ff), 2 of 3 are carriers: probability 2/3.

### Common mistakes

- Saying CF is caused by too much chloride in mucus.
- Saying a karyotype can detect CF (it cannot; CF is a gene mutation).
- Saying screening tests give a diagnosis.
- Saying Down's syndrome is inherited from a parent with Down's syndrome (it usually arises by non-disjunction during gamete formation).

# Respiration

Respiration releases energy from organic molecules to make ATP. This file covers glycolysis, anaerobic respiration, the link reaction, the Krebs cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, other respiratory substrates, the respiratory quotient, and AQA required practical 9.

## Overview

## 3.5.2 Respiration

*Refs: AQA 3.5.2, RP9 · Edexcel A 7.3 to 7.7, CP16 · Edexcel B 5.1 to 5.5, CP9 · OCR A 5.2.2(a) to (l) · OCR B 4.1.1(a) to (g) · Eduqas C1.1(a) to (d), C1.3(a) to (f). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Respiration produces ATP.
- Glycolysis is the first stage of anaerobic and aerobic respiration. It occurs in the cytoplasm and is an anaerobic process: phosphorylation of glucose to glucose phosphate, using ATP; production of triose phosphate; oxidation of triose phosphate to pyruvate with a net gain of ATP and reduced NAD.
- If respiration is only anaerobic, pyruvate can be converted to ethanol or lactate using reduced NAD. The oxidised NAD produced in this way can be used in further glycolysis.
- If respiration is aerobic, pyruvate from glycolysis enters the mitochondrial matrix by active transport. Pyruvate is oxidised to acetate, with the production of reduced NAD; acetate combines with coenzyme A in the link reaction to produce acetylcoenzyme A; acetylcoenzyme A reacts with a four-carbon molecule, releasing coenzyme A and producing a six-carbon molecule that enters the Krebs cycle; in a series of oxidation-reduction reactions, the Krebs cycle generates reduced coenzymes and ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation, and carbon dioxide is lost.
- Synthesis of ATP by oxidative phosphorylation is associated with the transfer of electrons down the electron transfer chain and passage of protons across inner mitochondrial membranes, and is catalysed by ATP synthase embedded in these membranes (chemiosmotic theory).
- Other respiratory substrates include the breakdown products of lipids and amino acids, which enter the Krebs cycle.

### Explained

**The mitochondrion** (OCR A, Edexcel B). Outer membrane; inner membrane folded into cristae (large surface area for the electron transfer chain and ATP synthase); intermembrane space (where protons build up); matrix containing Krebs cycle and link reaction enzymes, mitochondrial DNA and ribosomes.

**Coenzymes.** NAD and FAD carry hydrogen (protons and electrons) from the earlier stages to the electron transfer chain. Coenzyme A carries the acetyl group into the Krebs cycle.

**Glycolysis** (cytoplasm; no oxygen needed).

1. Glucose is phosphorylated twice using 2 ATP, forming hexose bisphosphate. This makes it more reactive and traps it in the cell.
2. It splits into two triose phosphate (TP) molecules.
3. Each TP is oxidised (hydrogen removed) to pyruvate. The hydrogen reduces NAD; 2 ATP are made per TP by substrate-level phosphorylation.

Per glucose: 4 ATP made − 2 ATP used = **net 2 ATP**, plus **2 reduced NAD** and **2 pyruvate**.

**Anaerobic respiration.** Without oxygen, the electron transfer chain stops, so reduced NAD cannot be reoxidised there. Glycolysis would stop when NAD ran out. Instead pyruvate accepts the hydrogen from reduced NAD, regenerating NAD so glycolysis continues and makes 2 ATP per glucose.

- Animals (and some bacteria): pyruvate + reduced NAD → lactate + NAD (lactate dehydrogenase). Lactate is carried to the liver, where it is converted back to pyruvate or glucose when oxygen is available.
- Plants and yeast: pyruvate → ethanal + CO₂; ethanal + reduced NAD → ethanol + NAD. This cannot be reversed.

Only 2 ATP per glucose are made anaerobically, against about 32 aerobically, because most of the energy is still in lactate or ethanol.

**The link reaction** (matrix). Pyruvate is actively transported into the matrix.

1. Pyruvate (3C) is decarboxylated (CO₂ removed) and oxidised (hydrogen removed, reducing NAD), forming acetate (2C).
2. Acetate combines with coenzyme A to form acetylcoenzyme A.

Per pyruvate: 1 CO₂, 1 reduced NAD. Per glucose: double.

**The Krebs cycle** (matrix).

1. Acetylcoenzyme A (2C) combines with a 4C molecule (oxaloacetate) to form a 6C molecule (citrate); coenzyme A is released to collect more acetate.
2. The 6C molecule is decarboxylated and oxidised to a 5C molecule, then to the 4C molecule, which is regenerated.
3. Per turn: 2 CO₂, 3 reduced NAD, 1 reduced FAD and 1 ATP (by substrate-level phosphorylation).

The cycle turns twice per glucose.

**Oxidative phosphorylation** (inner mitochondrial membrane, the cristae).

1. Reduced NAD and reduced FAD release hydrogen atoms, which split into protons and electrons.
2. Electrons pass along the electron transfer chain, a series of carrier proteins, in a series of oxidation-reduction reactions, losing energy.
3. The energy is used to pump protons from the matrix into the intermembrane space, creating an electrochemical gradient.
4. Protons diffuse back into the matrix through ATP synthase, which makes ATP from ADP and Pi (chemiosmosis).
5. At the end of the chain, oxygen is the final electron acceptor: it combines with electrons and protons to form water.

Without oxygen, electrons cannot leave the chain, so the whole process stops, and NAD and FAD stay reduced, stopping the Krebs cycle and link reaction too.

Reduced NAD gives about 2.5 ATP; reduced FAD about 1.5 (FAD's electrons enter the chain further along, so fewer protons are pumped).

**ATP tally per glucose (approximate).**

| Stage | ATP directly | Reduced NAD | Reduced FAD |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Glycolysis | 2 | 2 | 0 |
| Link reaction | 0 | 2 | 0 |
| Krebs cycle | 2 | 6 | 2 |
| Total | 4 | 10 | 2 |

From oxidative phosphorylation: 10 × 2.5 + 2 × 1.5 = 28. Total about 32. (Older texts give 38; the real yield is lower because some energy is used to move pyruvate and ADP into the mitochondrion and protons leak through the membrane.)

**Comparing mitochondria and chloroplasts** (Eduqas C1.1). Both make ATP by chemiosmosis: an electron transfer chain in a membrane pumps protons across it, and protons flow back through ATP synthase. In mitochondria protons are pumped into the intermembrane space; in chloroplasts into the thylakoid space. The energy source differs: excited electrons from light in chloroplasts; electrons from reduced NAD and FAD in mitochondria.

**Other respiratory substrates.**

- *Lipids:* triglycerides are hydrolysed to glycerol and fatty acids. Glycerol is phosphorylated and converted to triose phosphate, which enters glycolysis. Fatty acids are split into many 2C fragments, which become acetylcoenzyme A and enter the Krebs cycle, producing a lot of reduced NAD and FAD.
- *Proteins:* hydrolysed to amino acids; the amino group is removed in the liver (deamination, file 30) and the rest enters the Krebs cycle or link reaction as pyruvate, acetylcoenzyme A or a Krebs intermediate.

**Energy values** (OCR A, OCR B). Lipids about 39 kJ g⁻¹; proteins about 17 kJ g⁻¹; carbohydrates about 16 kJ g⁻¹. Lipids yield the most because they contain the most hydrogen atoms per gram, so they supply the most protons and electrons for oxidative phosphorylation.

**Respiratory quotient** (OCR A, OCR B). RQ = CO₂ produced ÷ O₂ used. Carbohydrate 1.0; protein about 0.9; lipid about 0.7. An RQ above 1 suggests some anaerobic respiration (CO₂ made without oxygen use).

### Worked example

1. Calculate the RQ for oleic acid: C₁₈H₃₄O₂ + 25.5O₂ → 18CO₂ + 17H₂O.

RQ = 18 ÷ 25.5 = 0.71.

2. In a respirometer, the coloured liquid in the capillary moved 30 mm in 10 minutes towards the organism. The capillary has a radius of 0.5 mm. Calculate the rate of oxygen uptake in mm³ min⁻¹.

Volume = π × 0.5² × 30 = 23.6 mm³. Rate = 23.6 ÷ 10 = 2.4 mm³ min⁻¹.

3. How many molecules of CO₂ are produced from one glucose in aerobic respiration, and where? 2 in the link reaction, 4 in the Krebs cycle: 6 in total.

### Common mistakes

- Saying glycolysis happens in the mitochondria.
- Saying anaerobic respiration "produces lactate to make ATP". Lactate is made to regenerate NAD so glycolysis can continue.
- Saying oxygen "makes ATP" or "combines with ADP". Oxygen is the final electron acceptor.
- Saying the Krebs cycle produces most of the ATP. Oxidative phosphorylation does.
- Saying "energy is made". Energy is released or transferred.
- Saying protons are pumped into the matrix.

### Practical

**AQA required practical 9** (also Edexcel A CP16, Edexcel B CP9, OCR A 5.2.2(i), (l) and PAG10/11, OCR B 4.1.1(g)): investigation into the effect of a named variable on the rate of respiration of cultures of single-celled organisms.

*Method 1: respirometer.* Put small organisms (yeast culture, germinating seeds or woodlice) in one tube with soda lime (or potassium hydroxide) to absorb CO₂. A second tube has glass beads of the same volume (control, to account for temperature and pressure changes). As oxygen is used, the pressure in the tube falls and the coloured liquid moves towards the organisms. Measure the distance moved in a set time and convert to volume. Change temperature with a water bath.

*Method 2: redox indicator.* Methylene blue (or TTC) accepts hydrogen from dehydrogenase enzymes in respiring yeast and turns colourless (TTC turns red). Time how long it takes at each temperature or glucose concentration.

*Method 3: CO₂ production.* Count bubbles or measure gas collected from yeast in glucose solution; or measure pH change with a datalogger.

Control variables: volume and concentration of yeast; glucose concentration; temperature (water bath); time allowed to equilibrate. Ethics: invertebrates must be returned unharmed; keep them away from soda lime (corrosive).

2. No oxygen to accept electrons, so electron transfer chain stops / NAD not regenerated (1); pyruvate reduced to lactate using reduced NAD (1); NAD regenerated so glycolysis continues / some ATP made (1).
3. Pyruvate decarboxylated / CO₂ removed (1); oxidised, reducing NAD (1); acetate combines with coenzyme A to form acetylcoenzyme A (1).
4. Reduced NAD / FAD release hydrogen / electrons (1); electrons pass along carriers losing energy (1); energy used to pump protons into intermembrane space (1); protons diffuse back through ATP synthase (1); oxygen is final electron acceptor forming water (1).
5. Oxygen is final electron acceptor; without it electrons cannot leave the chain (1); reduced NAD / FAD not oxidised (1); no NAD / FAD for Krebs cycle (1).
6. More hydrogen atoms per gram (1); more protons / electrons for oxidative phosphorylation, so more ATP (1).

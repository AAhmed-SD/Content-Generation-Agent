# Surface area to volume ratio and gas exchange

Large organisms cannot rely on diffusion across their body surface. This file covers surface area to volume ratio and how insects, fish, plant leaves and mammals exchange gases, with AQA required practical 5.

## 3.3.1 Surface area to volume ratio

*Refs: AQA 3.3.1 · Edexcel B 4.1 i, ii · OCR A 3.1.1(a), (b) · Eduqas C3.1(a). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The relationship between the size of an organism or structure and its surface area to volume ratio.
- Changes to body shape and the development of systems in larger organisms as adaptations that make exchange easier as the ratio falls.
- The relationship between surface area to volume ratio and metabolic rate.
- Calculate surface area to volume ratios.

### Explained

As an object gets bigger, its volume grows faster than its surface area. A 1 cm cube has SA 6 cm² and volume 1 cm³ (ratio 6:1); a 3 cm cube has SA 54 cm² and volume 27 cm³ (ratio 2:1).

Small organisms (single cells, flatworms) have a large ratio, so diffusion across the body surface supplies enough oxygen and removes enough waste. In large organisms:

- the surface is too small for the volume of cells needing supplies
- the distance from surface to the middle is too long for diffusion.

So large organisms have specialised exchange surfaces (lungs, gills, villi, leaves) and mass transport systems. Flattened or long thin shapes also increase the ratio (flatworms, leaves).

**Metabolic rate.** Small mammals lose heat fast because of their large surface area relative to volume. They have a high metabolic rate (per gram) to make heat, so need more oxygen per gram. Adaptations of small animals in cold places: compact shape, thick fur, hibernation. Large animals in hot places may have large ears (elephants) to lose heat.

**Features of efficient exchange surfaces** (OCR A, Edexcel A): large surface area; thin (short diffusion distance); steep concentration gradient kept up by ventilation and a good blood supply. **Fick's law** sums this up: rate of diffusion ∝ (surface area × difference in concentration) ÷ thickness of the surface.

### Worked example

A cylindrical cell is 20 µm long and 4 µm in diameter. Calculate its surface area to volume ratio.

1. r = 2 µm.
2. SA = 2πr² + 2πrh = 2π(4) + 2π(2)(20) = 8π + 80π = 88π ≈ 276.5 µm².
3. V = πr²h = π(4)(20) = 80π ≈ 251.3 µm³.
4. Ratio = 88π ÷ 80π = 1.1 : 1.

### Common mistakes

- Saying "large organisms have a small surface area". They have a large surface area but a small surface area to volume ratio.
- Leaving out units or writing the ratio upside down.

## 3.3.2 Gas exchange

*Refs: AQA 3.3.2, RP5 · Edexcel A 2.1 · Edexcel B 4.3 i, ii, CP7 · OCR A 3.1.1(b) to (h) · OCR B 2.2.3(a) to (c), (e), (f) · Eduqas C3.1(d) to (l). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Adaptations of gas exchange surfaces: across the body surface of a single-celled organism; in the tracheal system of an insect (tracheae, tracheoles and spiracles); across the gills of fish (gill lamellae and filaments, including countercurrent flow); by the leaves of dicotyledonous plants (mesophyll and stomata).
- Structural and functional compromises between efficient gas exchange and limiting water loss, in terrestrial insects and xerophytic plants.
- The gross structure of the human gas exchange system: alveoli, bronchioles, bronchi, trachea and lungs.
- The essential features of the alveolar epithelium as a surface over which gas exchange takes place.
- Ventilation and the exchange of gases in the lungs: the mechanism of breathing, including the roles of the diaphragm and the antagonistic interaction between external and internal intercostal muscles in bringing about pressure changes in the thoracic cavity.
- Interpret information on how lung disease affects lung function; interpret data on risk factors and lung disease; evaluate how experimental data led to statutory restrictions on sources of risk factors; analyse and interpret data on pulmonary ventilation rate.

### Explained

**Single-celled organisms.** Gases diffuse straight across the cell-surface membrane. The large surface area to volume ratio and short distance make this enough.

**Insects.**

- Air enters through spiracles (openings on the sides of the body that a valve can close).
- Tracheae (tubes stiffened by rings of chitin) branch into tracheoles, which reach right into the tissues; oxygen diffuses straight to the cells, with no blood involved.
- Movement of gases: diffusion down a concentration gradient (cells use oxygen); mass flow from ventilation movements of the abdomen; at the ends of the tracheoles, water is drawn into muscle cells during activity (by lowering the water potential with lactate), so air moves further down the tracheoles and gases diffuse faster.
- Limiting water loss: spiracles close when possible; a waxy, waterproof exoskeleton; hairs around spiracles trap moist air. The compromise: open spiracles let oxygen in but water out.

**Fish.**

- Water enters the mouth and passes over the gills, leaving under the operculum.
- Each gill has many gill filaments; each filament is covered in gill lamellae, giving a large surface area; lamellae have a thin epithelium and many capillaries.
- **Countercurrent flow:** blood flows through the lamellae in the opposite direction to the water. Blood always meets water with a higher oxygen concentration, so a diffusion gradient is maintained along the whole length of the lamella. About 80% of the oxygen in the water can be absorbed. In parallel flow (same direction) the concentrations would even out halfway, and only about 50% would be absorbed.

**Leaves of dicotyledonous plants.**

- Many stomata, mostly on the lower surface, so no cell is far from one.
- Air spaces in the spongy mesophyll, so gases diffuse quickly to and from cells; large internal surface area of mesophyll cells.
- Guard cells open the stomata in light (for CO₂ uptake) and close them when water is short. The compromise: open stomata let CO₂ in but water vapour out.
- (Eduqas) Leaf structure for photosynthesis: thin and broad (large surface for light, short CO₂ path); transparent cuticle and epidermis; palisade mesophyll packed with chloroplasts near the upper surface; veins of xylem bring water and phloem remove sugars. Stomata open when guard cells take up K⁺ ions actively, lowering their water potential; water enters by osmosis, the cells become turgid and bow apart because their inner walls are thicker.

**Xerophytes** (plants adapted to dry places, such as marram grass and cacti):

- thick waxy cuticle
- rolled leaves, sunken stomata and hairs that trap moist air, reducing the water potential gradient
- fewer stomata; small leaves or spines (smaller surface area)
- (more detail in file 11).

**The human gas exchange system.** Air passes down the trachea, which divides into two bronchi (one per lung), which branch into bronchioles, ending in alveoli. The trachea and bronchi have C-shaped cartilage rings to keep them open, ciliated epithelium and goblet cells that make mucus to trap particles; bronchioles have smooth muscle that can narrow them.

**The alveolar epithelium.**

- About 300 million alveoli: very large surface area.
- The alveolar wall is one layer of thin, flattened (squamous) cells, and the capillary wall is also one cell thick: diffusion path about 1 µm or less.
- A dense network of capillaries; blood flow carries oxygen away and brings carbon dioxide, maintaining the gradients.
- Ventilation replaces alveolar air, maintaining the gradients.
- Red blood cells are pressed flat against capillary walls, slowing flow and shortening the distance.
- Elastic fibres let alveoli stretch and recoil; surfactant stops them collapsing.

**Ventilation.**

| | Inspiration (inhalation) | Expiration (exhalation, at rest) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| External intercostal muscles | Contract: ribcage moves up and out | Relax |
| Internal intercostal muscles | Relax | Relax at rest; contract in forced expiration, pulling ribs down and in |
| Diaphragm | Contracts and flattens | Relaxes and domes up |
| Thoracic volume | Increases | Decreases |
| Pressure in lungs | Falls below atmospheric | Rises above atmospheric |
| Air | Moves in | Moves out |

Inspiration is active (needs muscle contraction). Normal expiration is mostly passive (elastic recoil of the lungs). The external and internal intercostals are antagonistic.

**Lung volumes** (OCR A, OCR B). A spirometer records a trace of volume against time.

- Tidal volume: volume of air breathed in or out in one normal breath (about 0.5 dm³).
- Vital capacity: the largest volume that can be breathed out after the deepest breath in.
- Residual volume: air left in the lungs after the most forceful exhalation; cannot be measured with a spirometer.
- Pulmonary ventilation rate (dm³ min⁻¹) = tidal volume (dm³) × breathing rate (breaths min⁻¹).
- Oxygen uptake: in a spirometer with soda lime absorbing CO₂, the trace falls over time; the gradient gives the rate of oxygen use.

**Lung disease** (data-handling skills). Diseases reduce gas exchange or ventilation:

- Pulmonary fibrosis: scar tissue thickens the alveolar walls (longer diffusion distance) and makes lungs less elastic (lower tidal volume and vital capacity).
- Asthma: airways inflamed; smooth muscle contracts and extra mucus is made, so airways narrow and less air flows (lower FEV₁, the volume breathed out forcefully in the first second).
- Emphysema: elastic fibres destroyed and alveolar walls break down; fewer, larger air spaces mean a much smaller surface area; lungs do not recoil, so air is trapped.

**Risk factors and evidence.** When interpreting data, separate correlation from cause. Large studies of smokers and non-smokers showed a strong correlation between smoking and lung cancer; animal experiments and identification of carcinogens in tar gave a mechanism. Together these led to laws restricting advertising and smoking in public places. Air pollution and occupational dusts (asbestos) led to similar laws.

### Worked example

1. A person's tidal volume is 0.45 dm³ and their breathing rate is 16 breaths per minute. Calculate their pulmonary ventilation.

0.45 × 16 = 7.2 dm³ min⁻¹.

2. After exercise their pulmonary ventilation is 36 dm³ min⁻¹ and their breathing rate is 24 min⁻¹. Calculate the new tidal volume.

36 ÷ 24 = 1.5 dm³.

### Common mistakes

- Saying insects carry oxygen in their blood.
- Saying countercurrent flow means "water and blood flow in opposite directions" without explaining the gradient along the whole lamella.
- Saying the lungs inflate "because air rushes in". Volume increases first, so pressure falls, and then air moves in.
- Saying the internal intercostals contract during normal breathing out.
- Saying the alveolar wall is "one cell thick so it's thin" without linking it to a short diffusion distance.
- Saying stomata are open "to let oxygen in" during photosynthesis.

### Practical

**AQA required practical 5** (also Edexcel B CP7, OCR A 3.1.1(g) and PAG2): dissection of animal or plant gas exchange or mass transport system or of an organ within such a system. (Heart dissection is described in file 10.)

*Fish gills.* Cut away the operculum with scissors to expose the gills; cut out a gill arch; put it in water and look with a hand lens or under a microscope at the filaments and lamellae, which float apart in water and stick together in air.

*Insect tracheal system* (locust or cockroach). Cut along the dorsal surface and pin the body open under water or saline in a dissecting dish; tracheae appear silvery because of the air inside. A section mounted on a slide shows the chitin rings.

*Lungs.* Identify trachea, bronchi and cartilage; inflate through the trachea using a tube connected to a pump (never by mouth).

Safety and ethics: sharp tools cut away from the body; wear gloves; wash hands; dispose of tissue properly; use material from animals killed for food where possible. Record findings as labelled drawings.

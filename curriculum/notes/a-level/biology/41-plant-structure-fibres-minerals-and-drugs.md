# Plant structure, fibres, minerals and drugs from plants

Plants give us fibres, starch and medicines. This file covers the plant cell wall and fibres, sclerenchyma, xylem and phloem in stems, the mineral ions plants need, how drugs from plants are discovered and tested, and the sustainable use of plant products, with Edexcel A core practicals 6 to 9.

## Plant fibres and stem structure

*Refs: Edexcel A 4.7, 4.10, 4.11, CP6, CP8. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- How the arrangement of cellulose microfibrils and secondary thickening in plant cell walls contributes to the physical properties of plant fibres, which can be exploited by humans.
- The similarities and differences between the structures, position in the stem and function of sclerenchyma fibres (support), xylem vessels (support and transport of water and mineral ions) and phloem (translocation of organic solutes).

### Explained

**The cell wall.** Cellulose molecules (β-glucose chains, file 01) are held in bundles called **microfibrils** by hydrogen bonds. Microfibrils are laid down in layers in a matrix of hemicellulose and pectin. In each layer microfibrils lie at an angle to those in the next layer, like plywood, which makes the wall strong in all directions. This is the primary wall.

**Secondary thickening.** Some cells, once they stop growing, lay down a thick secondary wall inside the primary wall, with more layers of cellulose and **lignin**. Lignin is a hard, waterproof polymer that makes the wall rigid and strong and resistant to compression and decay.

**Plant fibres** (such as flax, hemp, jute, cotton) are long cells or bundles of cells with thick walls. Their strength comes from many microfibrils at angles, and lignin. Uses: rope, textiles, paper, building materials, composites. Fibres are strong, light, cheap, renewable and biodegradable.

**Tissues in a dicot stem.** Vascular bundles are arranged in a ring near the outside.

| | Sclerenchyma fibres | Xylem vessels | Phloem sieve tubes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Living? | Dead at maturity | Dead at maturity | Living (with companion cells) |
| Wall | Very thick, heavily lignified, with pits | Lignified in rings, spirals or networks; pits | Cellulose only, no lignin |
| Ends | Tapered, overlapping, end walls present | End walls broken down: continuous tubes | Sieve plates (end walls with pores) |
| Position in stem | Outside the phloem in each bundle (fibre cap) | Inner side of the vascular bundle | Outer side of the vascular bundle, between xylem and fibres |
| Function | Support | Transport of water and mineral ions; support | Translocation of sucrose and amino acids |

### Practical

**CP6: identify sclerenchyma fibres, phloem sieve tubes and xylem vessels and their location within stems** (light microscope).

1. Cut thin transverse sections of a celery or sunflower stem with a sharp blade (cutting towards a board, not the hand), or use prepared slides.
2. Stain with toluidine blue O (TBO): lignified walls (xylem, sclerenchyma) turn blue-green; cellulose walls (phloem, parenchyma) turn pinkish-purple. Phloroglucinol with hydrochloric acid stains lignin red.
3. Mount in water; view under low, then high power. Draw a low-power plan showing the position of the tissues, and labelled high-power drawings of individual cells.

**CP8: determine the tensile strength of plant fibres.**

1. Extract fibres from a plant (for example nettle or celery stems soaked in water, or by pulling vascular strands from celery).
2. Clamp one end of a fibre of a fixed length to a stand; hang a mass hanger from the other end.
3. Add masses one at a time until the fibre breaks; record the mass at which it breaks. Repeat with fibres of the same length and similar thickness.
4. Tensile strength is the force (mass × 9.81 N kg⁻¹) per cross-sectional area at breaking; compare species or treatments.

Safety: wear eye protection (fibres may whip back); a tray of sand under the masses. Control variables: fibre length, diameter, age, water content.

## Water and mineral ions in plants

*Refs: Edexcel A 4.12, CP7 · Edexcel B 1.6 i · Eduqas C1.2(l). Level: AS & A (Edexcel); A-level only (Eduqas).*

### What you need to know

- The importance of water and inorganic ions (nitrate, calcium ions and magnesium ions) to plants. (Edexcel B and Eduqas also name phosphate.)
- Practical: investigating plant mineral deficiencies.

### Explained

| Ion | Use | Deficiency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nitrate (NO₃⁻) | Making amino acids, proteins, nucleotides (DNA, RNA, ATP) and chlorophyll | Stunted growth; yellow older leaves (chlorosis), because nitrogen is moved to young leaves |
| Phosphate (PO₄³⁻) | Nucleotides, ATP, phospholipids | Poor root growth; purple-tinged leaves |
| Magnesium (Mg²⁺) | Part of the chlorophyll molecule; activates some enzymes | Yellowing between leaf veins (chlorosis); less photosynthesis |
| Calcium (Ca²⁺) | Calcium pectate in the middle lamella binds cells together; membrane function | Weak growing tips; poor growth of new leaves and roots |
| Potassium (K⁺) (some boards) | Opening stomata; enzyme activation | Yellow leaf edges |

Water is needed for photosynthesis (photolysis), as a solvent for transport, for turgor (support), and to cool the plant by transpiration.

### Practical

**CP7 (Edexcel A), and the Eduqas specified practical: investigate plant mineral deficiencies.**

1. Grow seedlings of the same species and age (for example mung beans, or duckweed) in culture solutions: a complete nutrient solution (control), and solutions each lacking one ion (no nitrate, no magnesium, no calcium) and distilled water.
2. Keep all other conditions the same: light, temperature, volume of solution, aeration.
3. After two to four weeks, measure: height, number and colour of leaves, root length, dry mass, or (for duckweed) number of fronds.
4. Compare with the control.

## Drugs from plants and drug testing

*Refs: Edexcel A 4.13, CP9 · OCR B 3.3.2(e). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The development of drug testing from historic to contemporary protocols, including William Withering's digitalis soup, double blind trials, placebos, and three-phase testing.
- Plants as potential sources of medicines (OCR B).
- Practical: investigating the antimicrobial properties of plants (Edexcel A CP9).

### Explained

**Plants as sources of medicines** (OCR B 3.3.2(e)). Many drugs were first found in plants, often used in traditional medicine: digitalis from foxglove (heart failure); aspirin from willow bark (pain, blood thinning); quinine from cinchona bark (malaria); morphine from opium poppies (pain); taxol from yew (cancer); artemisinin from sweet wormwood (malaria). Plants make these chemicals to defend themselves. Many plants are untested, so conserving biodiversity, especially in tropical forests, keeps possible future medicines.

**Historic testing: William Withering (1775).** Withering learned of a herbal remedy for "dropsy" (swelling from fluid build-up, caused by heart failure). He identified foxglove as the active ingredient and tried different preparations and doses on his patients, recording the results carefully over about 10 years. Too much caused poisoning, too little had no effect. He found the dose that worked and published his findings. This was a step forward (careful observation, recording and dose-finding) but would not be acceptable now: no controls, no consent in the modern sense, no standard purity of the drug, and patients were harmed.

**Contemporary drug testing.**

1. *Pre-clinical:* testing on cells and tissues in the laboratory, computer models, and then animals, to find toxicity and likely doses.
2. *Phase 1:* a small group (20 to 100) of healthy volunteers. Tests safety, side effects and how the body handles the drug, starting with very low doses.
3. *Phase 2:* a few hundred patients with the condition. Tests whether the drug works and finds the best dose; may be compared with a placebo or existing treatment.
4. *Phase 3:* thousands of patients, often in many countries. Compares the new drug with the best existing treatment (or placebo), to confirm it works and to detect rarer side effects.
5. If approved by regulators, the drug is licensed; *phase 4* monitoring continues after release.

Key features:

- **Placebo:** a dummy treatment that looks the same but contains no active drug. It controls for the **placebo effect** (patients improving because they expect to). Using a placebo is not ethical if an effective treatment already exists; then the new drug is compared with that.
- **Double blind:** neither the patients nor the doctors know who has the drug or the placebo, so expectations do not bias results or how they are recorded.
- **Randomised:** patients are allocated to groups at random, so groups are similar.
- Large samples; informed consent; ethics committee approval.

### Practical

**CP9: investigate the antimicrobial properties of plants, including aseptic techniques for the safe handling of bacteria.**

1. Crush plant material (garlic, mint, ginger) with a pestle and mortar in ethanol to extract antimicrobial chemicals; filter.
2. Soak sterile paper discs in each extract; allow the ethanol to evaporate. Soak a control disc in ethanol only and let it evaporate.
3. Spread a culture of a safe bacterium (such as *Bacillus subtilis*) over an agar plate using aseptic technique (file 28).
4. Place the discs on the agar; tape the lid (not sealed); incubate at 25 °C for 48 hours upside down.
5. Measure the diameter of the clear zone around each disc; calculate area.

## Plant products and sustainability

*Refs: Edexcel A 4.15. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- How the uses of plant fibres and starch may contribute to sustainability, including plant-based products to replace oil-based plastics.

### Explained

A resource is **sustainable** if it can be used without running out and without harming the environment for future generations.

- **Plant fibres** replace synthetic fibres and materials made from oil: hemp and flax in car panels and insulation; cotton and bamboo textiles; paper instead of plastic packaging. They are renewable, biodegradable and often need less energy to produce; growing plants absorbs CO₂.
- **Starch** makes **bioplastics** (for example starch-based packaging and food containers), which biodegrade, and is fermented to make **bioethanol** (biofuel).
- Drawbacks: land used for fibres or biofuel crops may compete with food production or lead to deforestation; fertilisers and pesticides; some bioplastics need industrial composting; may be less durable or more expensive.

### Common mistakes

- Saying lignin is made of cellulose.
- Saying phloem has lignified walls.
- Saying a placebo is the new drug at a low dose.
- Saying "double blind" means two control groups.

## Mark scheme

1. Cellulose microfibrils held by hydrogen bonds (1); layers at different angles (1); secondary thickening with lignin (1).
2. Any two: sclerenchyma dead vs phloem living; lignified vs cellulose walls; no sieve plates / tapered ends vs sieve plates; support vs translocation.
3. Vascular bundles in a ring (1); xylem on the inside of each bundle (1); phloem outside the xylem with a cap of sclerenchyma outside the phloem (1).
4. Magnesium is part of chlorophyll (1); less chlorophyll, so leaves yellow (1); less light absorbed / less photosynthesis, so less growth (1).
5. Complete nutrient solution as control (1); solution lacking only nitrate (1); same light / temperature / volume / starting number of fronds (1); count fronds / measure dry mass after several weeks and compare (1).
6. Any two: no control group / placebo; no informed consent; not double blind / randomised; dose / purity not standardised; small, uncontrolled sample.
7. Phase 1: small number of healthy volunteers; safety and side effects (1); phase 2: small number of patients; effectiveness and dose (1); phase 3: large number of patients; compared with existing treatment / placebo (1).
8. Neither patients nor doctors know who receives the drug (1); avoids bias in reporting / recording results (1); placebo controls for the placebo effect / psychological improvement (1).

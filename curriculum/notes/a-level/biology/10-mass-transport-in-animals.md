## 3.3.4.1 Mass transport in animals

*Refs: AQA 3.3.4.1, RP5 · Edexcel A 1.1, 1.3, 1.4, 7.8 · Edexcel B 4.4 i to iii, v, vii, 4.5 i to iv, 4.6 i, ii · OCR A 3.1.2(a) to (f), (i), (j) · OCR B 2.2.1(a) to (c), 2.2.2(a) to (d), 4.1.2(g) to (i) · Eduqas C3.2(b) to (h). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The haemoglobins are a group of chemically similar molecules found in many different organisms. Haemoglobin is a protein with a quaternary structure.
- The role of haemoglobin and red blood cells in transporting oxygen; loading, transport and unloading of oxygen; the oxygen dissociation curve; the cooperative nature of oxygen binding; the effects of carbon dioxide concentration (Bohr effect).
- Many animals are adapted to their environment by having haemoglobin with different affinities for oxygen.
- The general pattern of blood circulation in a mammal, limited to: the coronary arteries; the blood vessels entering and leaving the heart, lungs and kidneys.
- The gross structure of the human heart; pressure and volume changes and associated valve movements during the cardiac cycle.
- The structure of arteries, arterioles and veins in relation to their function; the structure of capillaries and the importance of capillary beds as exchange surfaces.
- The formation of tissue fluid and its return to the circulatory system.
- Analyse and interpret data on the cardiac cycle; calculate cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate.

### Explained

**Haemoglobin.** Four polypeptide chains (two α and two β), each with a haem group containing Fe²⁺. Each haem binds one O₂ molecule, so one haemoglobin carries up to four O₂. Oxygen plus haemoglobin forms oxyhaemoglobin. Red blood cells have no nucleus and few organelles, leaving more room for haemoglobin, and a biconcave shape for a large surface area.

- *Loading* (association): in the lungs, where the partial pressure of oxygen (pO₂) is high, haemoglobin has a high affinity and becomes nearly saturated.
- *Unloading* (dissociation): in respiring tissues, where pO₂ is low, haemoglobin releases oxygen.

**The oxygen dissociation curve** plots percentage saturation of haemoglobin against pO₂. It is S-shaped (sigmoid):

1. At low pO₂ the first O₂ molecule binds with difficulty, because the haem groups are buried inside the molecule.
2. Binding of the first O₂ changes the shape (quaternary structure) of the haemoglobin, uncovering the other haem groups. The second and third bind much more easily. This is **cooperative binding**, and explains the steep middle part.
3. Near saturation it gets harder for the fourth O₂ to find a site, so the curve flattens.

The steep middle matters: a small fall in pO₂ in tissues releases a lot of oxygen.

**The Bohr effect.** At high CO₂ concentration (respiring tissues), haemoglobin has a lower affinity for oxygen. CO₂ dissolves to form carbonic acid; the H⁺ ions lower pH and change haemoglobin's shape. The curve shifts to the right. So more oxygen is unloaded where it is most needed. In the lungs, CO₂ is low, the curve is to the left, and loading is easy.

**Haemoglobins of different affinities.**

| Animal | Curve | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lugworm, llama, animals at altitude | Left of human | Live where pO₂ is low; high affinity lets haemoglobin load enough oxygen |
| Small mammals (mouse) | Right of human | High metabolic rate; lower affinity unloads oxygen more readily |
| Fetus (OCR A, Edexcel B) | Left of adult | Fetal haemoglobin must take oxygen from the mother's haemoglobin across the placenta, where pO₂ is low |
| Myoglobin (Edexcel B) | Far left, hyperbolic (one chain) | Stores oxygen in muscle; releases it only when pO₂ is very low, during hard exercise |

**Carbon dioxide transport** (OCR A, Eduqas, Edexcel B 4.5). About 5% dissolves in plasma; about 10% binds to haemoglobin as carbaminohaemoglobin; about 85% travels as hydrogencarbonate ions:

1. CO₂ diffuses into red blood cells.
2. Carbonic anhydrase catalyses CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ (carbonic acid).
3. H₂CO₃ dissociates into H⁺ and HCO₃⁻.
4. HCO₃⁻ diffuses out into the plasma. Chloride ions (Cl⁻) move in to balance the charge: the **chloride shift**.
5. H⁺ binds to haemoglobin, forming haemoglobinic acid. This buffers the blood pH and makes haemoglobin release its oxygen (the Bohr effect).

In the lungs the process reverses and CO₂ diffuses out.

**The circulation.** Mammals have a closed, double circulation: blood passes through the heart twice per circuit, once to the lungs (pulmonary) and once to the body (systemic). The heart raises blood pressure again after the lungs, so blood reaches the body quickly at high pressure, while the low pressure in the lungs avoids damage to capillaries there.

Vessels to know:

| Organ | In | Out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heart muscle | Coronary arteries (from the aorta) | Coronary veins |
| Lungs | Pulmonary artery (deoxygenated, from right ventricle) | Pulmonary vein (oxygenated, to left atrium) |
| Kidneys | Renal artery | Renal vein |
| Rest of body | Aorta (from left ventricle) | Vena cava (to right atrium) |

**The heart.** Four chambers. The right side pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs; the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body. The left ventricle wall is much thicker, because it must pump blood all round the body at high pressure. The atria have thin walls; they only pump blood into the ventricles.

- Atrioventricular valves (tricuspid on the right, bicuspid or mitral on the left) between atria and ventricles, held by tendinous cords so they do not turn inside out.
- Semilunar valves at the base of the aorta and pulmonary artery.
- Valves open when pressure behind them is higher than in front, and close when pressure in front is higher; they stop backflow.
- A septum separates the two sides so oxygenated and deoxygenated blood do not mix.

**The cardiac cycle** (about 0.8 s at rest).

| Stage | What happens | Valves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Atrial systole | Atria contract; pressure in atria rises above ventricles; blood pushed into ventricles | AV open; semilunar closed |
| Ventricular systole | Ventricles contract from the bottom up; ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure | AV close (first heart sound "lub"); when ventricular pressure exceeds pressure in the arteries, semilunar valves open and blood is ejected |
| Diastole | Ventricles and atria relax; ventricular pressure falls below arterial pressure | Semilunar close (second sound "dub"); when ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, AV valves open and blood flows passively into the ventricles |

On a pressure graph: the AV valves close where ventricular pressure crosses above atrial pressure; the semilunar valves open where ventricular pressure crosses above aortic pressure; they close where it falls below; AV valves open where ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure.

**Cardiac output** = stroke volume × heart rate. Stroke volume is the volume pumped by one ventricle per beat.

**Blood vessels.**

| Vessel | Structure | Function link |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Artery | Thick muscle layer; thick elastic layer; thick wall; small lumen; folded endothelium; no valves except at the base of the aorta and pulmonary artery | Elastic tissue stretches during systole and recoils during diastole, smoothing flow and keeping pressure high; thick wall resists bursting |
| Arteriole | Thicker muscle layer relative to elastic layer | Muscle contracts (vasoconstriction) or relaxes to control blood flow into capillary beds |
| Capillary | Wall one cell thick (endothelium); narrow lumen; many branches; gaps between cells | Short diffusion distance; large surface area; slow flow; tissue fluid forms through the gaps |
| Vein | Thin muscle and elastic layers; large lumen; valves | Low pressure, so little muscle or elastic tissue needed; valves stop backflow; contraction of surrounding skeletal muscle squeezes blood towards the heart |

**Tissue fluid** surrounds cells and is how substances pass between blood and cells.

1. At the arteriole end of a capillary, hydrostatic pressure (from the heart) is higher than the opposing forces, so water and small molecules (glucose, amino acids, ions, oxygen) are forced out through the gaps: ultrafiltration. Large plasma proteins and blood cells stay in.
2. Proteins in the blood give the plasma a lower water potential (an oncotic pressure).
3. At the venule end, hydrostatic pressure has fallen (fluid has left, and friction lowers it), so water moves back into the capillary by osmosis, carrying CO₂ and urea.
4. Not all fluid returns. The excess drains into lymph capillaries and is carried in lymph vessels, which have valves, back to the blood via ducts that join veins near the heart. Lymph is moved by contraction of nearby muscles.

If blood pressure is high, more fluid is forced out and less returns, so fluid builds up in tissues (oedema). A diet very low in protein also causes oedema, because the plasma water potential is higher.

### Worked example

1. A person's heart rate is 72 beats min⁻¹ and stroke volume is 70 cm³. Calculate cardiac output in dm³ min⁻¹.

72 × 70 = 5040 cm³ min⁻¹ = 5.04 dm³ min⁻¹.

2. On a cardiac cycle graph, one cycle lasts 0.75 s. Calculate the heart rate.

60 ÷ 0.75 = 80 beats min⁻¹.

3. At pO₂ = 12 kPa haemoglobin is 98% saturated; at 3 kPa it is 40% saturated. Each 100 cm³ of fully saturated blood carries 20 cm³ of oxygen. How much oxygen does 100 cm³ of blood unload?

(98 − 40)% of 20 = 0.58 × 20 = 11.6 cm³.

### Common mistakes

- Saying a shift to the right means "higher affinity". It means lower affinity: at any pO₂, less oxygen is bound.
- Saying the first oxygen binds easily. The first is hardest; then cooperative binding makes the next ones easier.
- Saying the left ventricle is thicker "to pump more blood". Both ventricles pump the same volume; the left pumps at higher pressure.
- Saying arteries "pump" blood. Their elastic recoil maintains pressure; the heart pumps.
- Saying "valves open because the heart contracts". Valves move because of pressure differences.
- Saying tissue fluid returns by "diffusion". Water returns by osmosis.
- Saying pulmonary veins carry deoxygenated blood.

### Practical

**Heart dissection** (AQA RP5, OCR A 3.1.2(e) and PAG2).

1. Identify the front (ventral) side: the coronary arteries run diagonally across it.
2. Find the aorta, pulmonary artery, vena cava and pulmonary veins at the top. Push a blunt probe or glass rod into vessels to find which chamber each joins.
3. Cut through the wall of the right ventricle from the pulmonary artery downwards; then do the same on the left. Compare wall thicknesses.
4. Find the AV valves and tendinous cords, and the semilunar valves (cup-shaped pockets) at the base of the arteries.
5. Make a labelled drawing.

Safety: scalpel cuts away from the body; gloves; disinfect surfaces; wash hands. Ethics: use hearts from animals killed for food.

# Pathogens, communicable disease, plant defences and antibiotics

Pathogens are organisms that cause disease. This file covers the kinds of pathogen and how they cause harm, how diseases spread in animals and plants, named diseases, epidemiology, how plants defend themselves, sources of medicines, and antibiotics and resistance. AQA does not have this section; it covers the immune response only (file 07).

The immune response is in file 07. Culturing bacteria, Gram staining and practicals with antibiotics are in file 28. HIV structure and replication are in file 07; here HIV is treated as a disease.

## Pathogens and how they cause disease

*Refs: OCR A 4.1.1(a), (b) · OCR B 3.2.1(a), (b) · Edexcel A 6.6, 6.11 · Edexcel B 2.2 i to v, 6.2, 6.5 · Eduqas OA.1(a), (b), (d). Level: AS & A (OCR A, OCR B); A-level only (Edexcel, Eduqas).*

### What you need to know

- The types of pathogen: bacteria, viruses, protoctista and fungi; how each causes disease.
- How pathogens are transmitted between animals and between plants, and the factors that affect transmission.
- The main features of named diseases.
- (Edexcel B) The lytic cycle of a virus and latency; antivirals; ethics of using untested drugs in an epidemic.
- (Eduqas) Terms: pathogenic, infectious, carrier, disease, epidemic, pandemic, endemic, vector, reservoir.

### Explained

**Terms** (Eduqas, OCR B).

- *Pathogen:* a microorganism that causes disease. *Infectious (communicable) disease:* caused by a pathogen and passed between organisms.
- *Carrier:* an infected individual who shows no symptoms but can pass on the pathogen.
- *Vector:* an organism that carries a pathogen from one host to another (the *Anopheles* mosquito for malaria).
- *Reservoir:* where the pathogen lives and multiplies outside the main host (cattle for some TB).
- *Endemic:* always present at a low level in a population (malaria in parts of Africa; chickenpox in the UK). *Epidemic:* a sudden rise in cases in one area (SARS in China, 2002). *Pandemic:* an epidemic across many countries (H1N1 influenza in 2009).

**How pathogens cause harm.**

- *Bacteria:* damage host tissues and release toxins. Exotoxins are proteins secreted by living bacteria (cholera toxin makes gut cells lose chloride ions and water, causing severe diarrhoea). Endotoxins are lipopolysaccharides in the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, released when they die, causing fever and inflammation (Edexcel B 6.2 ii).
- *Viruses:* invade cells, take over the cell's metabolism to make new viruses, then burst the cell.
- *Fungi:* grow hyphae through tissue and secrete enzymes that digest it (ringworm; stem rust digests wheat leaf tissue).
- *Protoctista:* enter and destroy cells (*Plasmodium* in red blood cells and liver cells).

**The lytic cycle and latency** (Edexcel B 2.2 ii). In the lytic cycle a virus attaches to a host cell, injects its nucleic acid, the cell makes new viral nucleic acid and proteins, these assemble, and the cell bursts (lysis), releasing many viruses. In latency (the lysogenic pathway), the viral DNA is inserted into the host's DNA and copied each time the cell divides, without making new viruses. Later, a trigger (stress, UV, a weakened immune system) can start the lytic cycle. HIV and herpes viruses show latency.

**Antivirals** (Edexcel B 2.2 iii). Viruses have no metabolism of their own, so antivirals target viral processes: blocking attachment proteins or receptors; inhibiting viral enzymes such as reverse transcriptase or protease. Antiviral drugs are fewer than antibiotics and viruses often become resistant.

**Untested drugs in epidemics** (Edexcel B 2.2 iv, v). Because viral infections are hard to treat once established, control relies on prevention (vaccines, hygiene, isolation). In an emergency (Ebola in West Africa, 2014), using drugs not yet fully tested raises ethical questions: who receives them; informed consent; risk of harm against risk of death; whether a control group can be denied the drug.

**Transmission.**

| Route | Animals | Plants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct contact | Skin contact, sex (HIV), body fluids, bites | Plant-to-plant contact |
| Droplets / air | Coughs and sneezes (TB, influenza) | Spores in air (black sigatoka, stem rust) |
| Food and water | Contaminated water (cholera) | Contaminated soil (ring rot bacteria stay in soil) |
| Vectors | Mosquitoes (malaria) | Insects (aphids carry viruses) |
| Mother to child | Across the placenta, in breast milk (HIV) | In seeds (some viruses) |

Factors that increase transmission: overcrowding; poor ventilation; poor sanitation and lack of clean water; malnutrition and other diseases weakening immunity; climate (warm, wet conditions help vectors and fungi); for plants, monocultures and planting crops close together.

**Named diseases.**

| Disease | Pathogen | Host and effects | Transmission | Prevention and treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tuberculosis (TB) | Bacterium (*Mycobacterium tuberculosis*) | Humans (also cattle); infects lungs; primary infection may stay dormant in a tubercle; secondary TB (when immunity falls) destroys lung tissue, coughing blood, weight loss | Droplets | BCG vaccine; long course of several antibiotics (6 months or more); contact tracing |
| Cholera (Eduqas) | Bacterium (*Vibrio cholerae*) | Humans; small intestine; exotoxin causes severe watery diarrhoea and dehydration | Contaminated water and food | Clean water, sewage treatment; oral rehydration therapy; vaccine |
| Bacterial meningitis (OCR A) | Bacterium | Humans; membranes of brain and spinal cord; can cause septicaemia | Droplets, close contact | Vaccines; antibiotics |
| Ring rot (OCR A) | Bacterium | Potatoes, tomatoes; destroys vascular tissue | Contaminated soil, equipment, seed potatoes | Certified disease-free seed; no cure |
| HIV/AIDS | Virus (retrovirus) | Humans; destroys helper T cells, leading to AIDS and opportunistic infections | Sex, blood, needles, mother to child | Condoms, screening blood, clean needles; antiretroviral drugs (no cure, no vaccine) |
| Influenza | Virus | Humans (and birds, pigs); infects ciliated epithelium of airways; secondary infections | Droplets | Annual vaccine (antigenic variation); antivirals |
| Smallpox (Eduqas) | Virus | Humans; skin and internal organs; high mortality | Droplets, contact | Eradicated in 1980 by worldwide vaccination and isolation |
| Tobacco mosaic virus (OCR A) | Virus | Tobacco, tomatoes; mottled leaves, less photosynthesis, stunted growth | Contact, contaminated tools | Resistant varieties; hygiene |
| Black sigatoka (OCR A) | Fungus | Bananas; leaf spots, less photosynthesis | Spores in wind and water | Fungicides; resistant varieties |
| Stem rust (Edexcel B) | Fungus (*Puccinia graminis*) | Wheat; rusty pustules on stems and leaves; takes nutrients; plants weakened | Spores in wind | Resistant varieties; fungicides |
| Ringworm (OCR A) | Fungus | Cattle (and humans); circular grey patches on skin | Contact | Antifungal creams |
| Athlete's foot (OCR A) | Fungus | Humans; cracked skin between toes | Contact; damp floors | Antifungal creams |
| Potato late blight (OCR A) | Protoctist (*Phytophthora infestans*) | Potatoes, tomatoes; hyphae destroy leaves and tubers | Spores in water and air | Resistant varieties; fungicides; destroying infected plants |
| Malaria | Protoctist (*Plasmodium*) | Humans; infects liver and red blood cells; fever, anaemia, death | Female *Anopheles* mosquito vector | Nets, insecticides, draining water; antimalarial drugs; vaccines now in use in some countries |

**HIV and TB together** (Edexcel A 6.6, OCR B 3.2.1(b)). TB is a common opportunistic infection in people with AIDS, because without helper T cells the dormant TB in tubercles is no longer contained. *TB in detail:* the bacteria are taken up by macrophages in the lungs but survive inside them; the immune system walls them off in a tubercle (granuloma); later, if immunity weakens, the bacteria multiply, destroy lung tissue and spread.

### Common mistakes

- Saying viruses are "bacteria" or that antibiotics treat influenza.
- Saying malaria is caused by mosquitoes. The mosquito is the vector; *Plasmodium* is the pathogen.
- Saying endemic means "very many cases".

## Epidemiology and controlling disease

*Refs: OCR B 3.2.1(e) to (i), 3.2.2(f), 3.2.3(c), (d) · Edexcel A 6.13, 6.15 · Edexcel B 3.2 iv, 6.4 ii, 6.6 · OCR A 4.2.2(i) · Eduqas OA.3(i). Level: AS & A (OCR B); A-level only (Edexcel, Eduqas).*

### What you need to know

- How incidence and prevalence of a disease change over time; calculating incidence, prevalence and mortality rates.
- Interpreting epidemiological data; the importance of notifiable diseases.
- Testing for TB and HIV (OCR B).
- The problems of developing vaccines and controlling endemic disease.
- The "evolutionary race" between pathogens and hosts.
- Hospital-acquired infections and their control.

### Explained

**Rates** (OCR B).

- *Incidence rate:* new cases in a period ÷ population at risk (often × 100 000).
- *Prevalence rate:* all existing cases at a time ÷ total population.
- *Mortality rate:* deaths from the disease in a period ÷ population.

A disease with a long duration (HIV with treatment) can have low incidence but high prevalence.

**Notifiable diseases** (OCR B): doctors must report cases of certain diseases (TB, measles, meningitis) to public health authorities, so outbreaks are spotted early and contacts traced.

**Testing** (OCR B 3.2.2(f)). TB: the Mantoux skin test (a small amount of TB protein is injected under the skin; a raised, hard area after 2 to 3 days suggests past exposure); interferon-gamma blood tests; chest X-rays; culturing sputum. HIV: blood tests for HIV antibodies (ELISA, file 07) and for viral RNA or antigen; rapid tests.

**The evolutionary race** (Edexcel A 6.13, Edexcel B 3.2 iv). Hosts evolve defences; pathogens evolve ways to evade them, and so on. Examples: HIV kills helper T cells, mutates rapidly so its antigens keep changing, and hides inside cells in latency. TB bacteria survive inside macrophages by stopping lysosomes fusing with phagosomes. *Plasmodium* changes its surface antigens and hides inside red blood cells. In the other direction, the sickle cell allele has been selected in regions with malaria.

**Problems with vaccines** (OCR B 3.2.3(c)). Antigenic variability (HIV, influenza); pathogens that hide inside cells (TB, HIV, malaria); many strains; parasites with complex life cycles and several stages (malaria); storage needing cold chains; cost and distribution in poorer countries; public refusal.

**Controlling endemic malaria** (Edexcel B 6.6). Methods: insecticide-treated bed nets; spraying insecticide indoors; draining standing water; antimalarial drugs; vaccines (RTS,S and R21, now being rolled out). Each has social, economic and ethical implications: cost and who pays; resistance of mosquitoes to insecticides and of *Plasmodium* to drugs; effects of insecticides such as DDT on other wildlife; the scientific community validating each method in trials before use.

**Hospital-acquired infections** (Edexcel A 6.15, Edexcel B 6.4 ii). Infections such as MRSA (methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*) and *Clostridioides difficile* spread in hospitals because patients are already ill, have wounds or catheters, are close together, and many receive antibiotics, selecting for resistant bacteria. Control: handwashing and alcohol gel; isolating infected patients; screening patients on admission; cleaning; limiting antibiotic use and choosing narrow-spectrum ones; codes of practice for staff.

## Plant defences

*Refs: OCR A 4.1.1(c). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Plant defences against pathogens, including physical and chemical defences, and the production of callose.

### Explained

*Passive defences* (always present): the waxy cuticle; cellulose cell walls; bark with lignin and suberin; stomata that can close; chemicals in tissues (tannins, alkaloids).

*Active defences* (after infection is detected, when receptors on plant cells recognise molecules from the pathogen):

- **Callose**, a polysaccharide (β-1,3 glucan), is laid down between the cell wall and membrane near the infection, blocking the pathogen's spread. Callose also blocks sieve plates in phloem and plasmodesmata, sealing off infected tissue.
- Cell walls are thickened with lignin and cellulose.
- Antimicrobial chemicals are made: phenols, terpenes, defensins, saponins; and chitinases that break down fungal cell walls.
- Hydrogen peroxide and other reactive oxygen species kill pathogens.
- Infected cells die by a hypersensitive response, depriving the pathogen.
- Necrosis and canker: deliberately killing tissue around the infection.

## Antibiotics and medicines

*Refs: OCR A 4.1.1(m), (n), 4.2.2(i) · OCR B 3.2.3(e), (f) · Edexcel A 6.14 · Edexcel B 6.3, 6.4 i · Eduqas OA.2(a) to (c). Level: AS & A (OCR); A-level only (Edexcel, Eduqas).*

### What you need to know

- Possible sources of medicines (plants, microorganisms); the importance of biodiversity for finding new ones; personalised medicine and synthetic biology (OCR A).
- Bactericidal and bacteriostatic antibiotics and how they work, with named examples.
- The benefits and risks of using antibiotics; the development of resistance and how to limit it.

### Explained

**Sources of medicines** (OCR A 4.1.1(m)). Many drugs come from living things: penicillin from the fungus *Penicillium*; aspirin from a compound in willow bark; morphine from opium poppies; digoxin from foxgloves; taxol (an anti-cancer drug) from Pacific yew bark. Most species have never been tested, so losing biodiversity may lose future medicines. Today, drugs are also found by screening thousands of chemicals, designed using protein structures, and made by GM microorganisms (file 25).

**Antibiotics** are chemicals that kill or inhibit bacteria without harming human cells, because they target bacterial structures and enzymes that human cells lack or have in a different form.

- *Bactericidal:* kill bacteria. Penicillin inhibits the enzymes that form cross-links in the murein (peptidoglycan) cell wall. As the bacterium grows, its wall is weak; water enters by osmosis and the cell bursts (osmotic lysis). It works on growing, mainly Gram-positive bacteria.
- *Bacteriostatic:* stop bacteria growing and dividing; the immune system then removes them. Tetracycline binds to the 30S subunit of bacterial (70S) ribosomes, stopping tRNA binding, so protein synthesis stops. It does not affect 80S human ribosomes.

**Resistance.**

1. A random mutation gives a bacterium resistance (such as an enzyme, penicillinase, that breaks down penicillin; or a change to the target).
2. In the presence of the antibiotic, non-resistant bacteria die; resistant ones survive and reproduce (natural selection, file 13).
3. Bacteria reproduce rapidly, so the resistant population grows quickly.
4. Resistance genes on plasmids spread to other bacteria, even other species, by horizontal gene transfer (conjugation).

**Reducing resistance.** Prescribe antibiotics only when needed (not for viral infections); finish the course as prescribed; use narrow-spectrum antibiotics targeted at the known bacterium; rotate antibiotics; reduce antibiotic use in farming; improve hospital hygiene; develop new antibiotics and alternatives (bacteriophages).

**Benefits and risks** (OCR A 4.1.1(n)). Antibiotics have made infections that used to be fatal, and surgery, much safer. Overuse risks resistant strains (MRSA, multi-drug-resistant TB), side effects and harm to the normal gut bacteria (allowing *C. difficile* to grow).

### Worked example

A town of 40 000 people had 120 existing cases of a disease on 1 January, with 60 new cases during the year and 6 deaths from it.

- Incidence = 60 ÷ 40 000 × 100 000 = 150 per 100 000 per year.
- Prevalence on 1 January = 120 ÷ 40 000 × 100 000 = 300 per 100 000.
- Mortality = 6 ÷ 40 000 × 100 000 = 15 per 100 000 per year.

### Common mistakes

- Saying bacteria become resistant because they are "exposed" or "get used to" antibiotics.
- Saying penicillin "breaks down the cell wall". It prevents cross-links forming as the wall is built.
- Saying antibiotics act on human cells' ribosomes.

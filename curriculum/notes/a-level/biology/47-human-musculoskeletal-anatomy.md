# Human musculoskeletal anatomy

Bones, cartilage, joints and muscles together support and move the body. This file covers cartilage and bone tissue, bone disorders, the human skeleton and vertebral column, fractures and posture, joints and levers, arthritis, antagonistic muscles, and the energy supply for muscle contraction.

## Skeletal tissues

*Refs: Eduqas OB.1(a) to (g), (k), (l). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The structure of cartilage: hyaline, yellow elastic and white fibrous.
- The components of compact bone: a matrix about 30% organic (mainly collagen) and 70% inorganic (mainly hydroxyapatite, containing calcium and phosphate); the functions of each.
- Osteoblasts and osteoclasts; bone is constantly broken down and re-formed.
- The structure and function of Haversian systems.
- Rickets and osteomalacia; osteoporosis and brittle bone disease.
- The effects of anaerobic conditions in muscle (creatine phosphate, lactic acid) and the use of glycogen and other stores as energy sources during contraction.

### Explained

**Cartilage** is a firm, flexible connective tissue. Cells called chondrocytes sit in spaces (lacunae) in a matrix of collagen fibres and a gel of proteoglycans. It has no blood vessels, so it heals slowly.

| Type | Structure | Where | Property |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hyaline | Fine collagen fibres, smooth glassy matrix | Ends of bones in joints (articular cartilage), trachea rings, nose, embryonic skeleton | Smooth, reduces friction; flexible support |
| Yellow elastic | Collagen plus many elastic fibres | Outer ear (pinna), epiglottis | Flexible; returns to shape |
| White fibrous | Dense bundles of collagen fibres | Intervertebral discs, between pelvic bones, knee menisci | Very strong; resists compression and tension; absorbs shock |

**Compact bone.**

- *Organic part* (about 30%): mainly collagen fibres. Gives flexibility and tensile strength, so bone bends slightly rather than snapping.
- *Inorganic part* (about 70%): mainly hydroxyapatite, a calcium phosphate mineral. Gives hardness and strength under compression.
- Together they make bone strong and slightly flexible, like reinforced concrete.

**Haversian systems (osteons)** are the units of compact bone: a central Haversian canal containing blood vessels and nerves, surrounded by concentric rings (lamellae) of bone matrix. Osteocytes (mature bone cells) sit in lacunae between lamellae and connect to each other and to the canal by tiny channels (canaliculi), through which nutrients and oxygen diffuse. The cylinders run along the length of the bone, resisting bending.

**Bone remodelling.** Bone is living and constantly renewed.

- *Osteoblasts* lay down new matrix (collagen and then hydroxyapatite); when surrounded by matrix they become osteocytes.
- *Osteoclasts* break down (resorb) bone, releasing calcium into the blood.
- The balance between them, controlled by hormones (parathyroid hormone, calcitonin, oestrogen, testosterone) and by the stresses on bone (exercise increases bone density), sets bone mass. It also lets bones repair and reshape.

**Bone disorders.**

| Disorder | Cause | Effect | Treatment and prevention |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rickets (children) | Lack of vitamin D or calcium | Bones not mineralised properly; soft, bend under weight (bowed legs) | Vitamin D and calcium supplements; sunlight (vitamin D made in skin) |
| Osteomalacia (adults) | Lack of vitamin D or calcium | Soft bones; pain, weakness, fractures | As above |
| Osteoporosis | Osteoclast activity exceeds osteoblast activity; loss of bone mass with age, especially in women after the menopause (less oestrogen, file 36); also low calcium, inactivity, smoking, steroids | Porous, fragile bones; fractures (wrist, hip, spine); loss of height, stooped posture | Weight-bearing exercise; calcium and vitamin D; HRT; drugs that slow osteoclasts (bisphosphonates); bone density scans |
| Brittle bone disease (osteogenesis imperfecta) | Inherited mutation in a collagen gene (usually dominant) | Faulty or too little collagen; bones break very easily | No cure; drugs to strengthen bone; careful handling; surgery with rods in long bones |

**Energy for muscle contraction** (Eduqas OB.1(k), (l); file 19 for detail).

- ATP stored in muscle lasts only a few seconds.
- **Creatine phosphate** (phosphocreatine) regenerates ATP from ADP rapidly for about 10 seconds of maximum effort.
- **Glycogen** stored in muscle is broken down to glucose for glycolysis: anaerobically in intense exercise (producing lactate, which lowers pH and contributes to fatigue) and aerobically over longer periods.
- In prolonged exercise, fatty acids from fat stores are respired aerobically, sparing glycogen; protein (amino acids) is used to a small extent, especially when glycogen is depleted.

## The human skeleton

*Refs: Eduqas OB.2(a) to (e). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The axial and appendicular skeleton (pectoral and pelvic girdles, forelimb and hind limb).
- Types of fracture and their causes.
- The structure and function of the vertebral column; the general structure of a vertebra; cervical, thoracic and lumbar vertebrae.
- Medical conditions affecting posture.
- The functions of the skeleton.

### Explained

**Functions of the skeleton:** support (shape, holds organs); attachment of muscles for movement (bones act as levers); protection (skull protects brain; ribs protect heart and lungs; vertebrae protect the spinal cord); production of blood cells (red bone marrow in flat bones and the ends of long bones); store of calcium and phosphate.

**Axial and appendicular.**

- *Axial skeleton:* skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum. The central axis of the body.
- *Appendicular skeleton:* the limbs and the girdles that attach them. *Pectoral girdle* (scapula, clavicle) attaches the forelimbs (humerus; radius and ulna; carpals, metacarpals, phalanges). *Pelvic girdle* (pelvis, joined to the sacrum) attaches the hind limbs (femur; tibia and fibula; tarsals, metatarsals, phalanges). The pectoral girdle allows a wide range of movement; the pelvic girdle is strongly fixed to bear weight.

**Vertebral column.** 33 vertebrae: 7 cervical (neck), 12 thoracic (chest), 5 lumbar (lower back), 5 fused sacral (sacrum), and about 4 fused coccygeal (coccyx). Intervertebral discs of white fibrous cartilage between vertebrae absorb shock and allow bending. Its S-shaped curves help balance and absorb shock.

A typical vertebra has a **centrum** (body, bears weight), a **neural arch** surrounding the **neural canal** (spinal cord passes through), a **neural spine** and **transverse processes** (muscle attachment), and articulating surfaces (facets) where vertebrae join.

| | Cervical | Thoracic | Lumbar |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Centrum | Small (light load) | Medium | Large, thick (bears most weight) |
| Features | Holes in the transverse processes (vertebrarterial canals) for arteries to the brain; short spine; first two (atlas and axis) allow nodding and turning the head | Long, downward-pointing spine; facets for the ribs | Short, thick spine and processes for attachment of large back muscles |
| Movement | Most flexible | Limited (ribs) | Bending and some rotation |

**Fractures.**

- *Simple (closed):* bone breaks but skin is not broken.
- *Compound (open):* bone breaks through the skin; risk of infection.
- *Greenstick:* bone bends and cracks on one side (children's flexible bones).
- *Comminuted:* bone shatters into several pieces.
- *Stress fracture:* small cracks from repeated stress (runners).
- Causes: falls, impacts, sport, osteoporosis (fractures with little force), bone disease. Treatment: realign (set) the bone and immobilise it (cast or splint; metal pins or plates for complex fractures); the bone heals as osteoblasts lay down new bone.

**Posture conditions.**

- *Scoliosis:* sideways curvature of the spine; often unknown cause in teenagers; treated with a brace or surgery.
- *Kyphosis:* excessive outward curve of the upper back (hunchback); caused by poor posture, osteoporosis (collapsed vertebrae) or abnormal development; treated with exercise, bracing, surgery.
- *Lordosis:* excessive inward curve of the lower back; caused by obesity, posture, pregnancy; treated with exercise and weight loss.
- *Slipped (herniated) disc:* the soft centre of an intervertebral disc bulges out and presses on nerves, causing back pain or sciatica; treated with rest, physiotherapy, painkillers, sometimes surgery.

## Joints, levers and muscles

*Refs: Eduqas OB.3(a) to (f) · Edexcel A 7.1. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Types of joint: immovable (fused), gliding, hinge, ball and socket.
- Osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis.
- Joints acting as levers: first, second and third order.
- The structure of a synovial joint: cartilage, synovial membrane, synovial fluid, ligaments.
- Antagonistic muscle action in the forelimb; the role of tendons.

### Explained

**Types of joint.**

| Type | Movement | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Immovable (fused, fibrous) | None | Sutures of the skull |
| Gliding | Bones slide over each other | Between carpals; between vertebral facets |
| Hinge | Movement in one plane | Elbow, knee |
| Ball and socket | Movement in all planes and rotation | Shoulder, hip |

**Synovial joint** (freely movable joints).

- *Articular (hyaline) cartilage* covers the ends of the bones: smooth, reduces friction and absorbs shock.
- *Synovial membrane* lines the joint capsule and secretes synovial fluid.
- *Synovial fluid* lubricates the joint, reduces friction and nourishes the cartilage.
- *Joint capsule:* fibrous tissue enclosing the joint.
- *Ligaments:* strong, slightly elastic bands of collagen joining bone to bone; stabilise the joint and prevent dislocation.
- (Knee) *menisci* of white fibrous cartilage absorb shock.

**Tendons** are tough, inelastic bands of collagen joining muscle to bone; because they do not stretch, the full force of contraction moves the bone.

**Arthritis.**

- *Osteoarthritis:* wear and tear; articular cartilage thins and breaks down, so bones rub together; pain, stiffness, bony growths. Risk increases with age, obesity, joint injury, repetitive use. Treatment: exercise, weight loss, painkillers and anti-inflammatory drugs, physiotherapy, joint replacement.
- *Rheumatoid arthritis:* an autoimmune disease (file 07): the immune system attacks the synovial membrane, which becomes inflamed and thickened, damaging cartilage and bone; usually affects several joints on both sides, with swelling and stiffness, especially in the morning. Treatment: anti-inflammatory drugs, steroids, drugs that suppress the immune system, biological drugs (monoclonal antibodies against inflammatory cytokines), physiotherapy, surgery.

**Levers.** A joint is the fulcrum (pivot, F), the muscle supplies the effort (E), and the weight of the body part or object is the load (L).

| Order | Arrangement | Example | Property |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First | F between E and L | Nodding the head (the top of the spine is the fulcrum; neck muscles at the back are the effort; weight of the face is the load) | Balance |
| Second | L between F and E | Rising onto tiptoe (ball of foot is the fulcrum; body weight load; calf muscle effort at the heel) | Large load moved with less effort |
| Third | E between F and L | Bending the elbow (elbow fulcrum; biceps attaches just below; load in the hand) | Most common; small muscle shortening gives large, fast movement of the hand, but needs more force |

**Antagonistic muscles in the forelimb.** Biceps (origin on the scapula, insertion on the radius) contracts to flex the elbow while the triceps relaxes; triceps (insertion on the ulna) contracts to extend the elbow while the biceps relaxes. The muscle that contracts is the agonist; the one that relaxes is the antagonist. Tendons transmit the force to the bones; the incompressible skeleton allows the force to cause movement.

### Common mistakes

- Confusing ligaments (bone to bone) with tendons (muscle to bone).
- Saying osteoarthritis is autoimmune.
- Saying bone is not living tissue.
- Mixing up osteoblasts (build) and osteoclasts (break down).

## Mark scheme

1. Collagen (organic) gives flexibility / tensile strength (1); hydroxyapatite (inorganic) gives hardness / compressive strength (1); together strong but not brittle (1).
2. Central Haversian canal with blood vessels / nerves (1); concentric lamellae of matrix (1); osteocytes in lacunae connected by canaliculi (1).
3. Oestrogen falls after the menopause (1); oestrogen normally limits osteoclast activity / bone resorption (1); bone broken down faster than it is made, so bone density falls (1).
4. Rickets: lack of vitamin D / calcium, poor mineralisation, soft bones (1); brittle bone disease: inherited mutation in collagen gene (1); bones fragile and break easily; rickets treatable with diet / sunlight, brittle bone disease not curable (1).
5. Any two with function (2 each): lumbar larger centrum bears more weight; thoracic has facets for ribs; thoracic long downward spine limits movement / lumbar short thick spine and processes for large muscles.
6. Articular cartilage reduces friction / absorbs shock (1); synovial membrane secretes synovial fluid (1); synovial fluid lubricates / nourishes cartilage (1); ligaments join bones / stabilise joint; capsule encloses (1).
7. Effort (biceps insertion) is between fulcrum (elbow) and load (hand) (1); small contraction gives large / fast movement of the hand (1); but more force is needed (1).
8. Osteoarthritis: wear and tear of cartilage, usually older people, often weight-bearing joints (1); rheumatoid: autoimmune attack on synovial membrane, inflammation, often symmetrical / several joints (1); treatment differs, e.g. immune suppressants for rheumatoid (1).

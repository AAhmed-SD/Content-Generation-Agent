# Organisation of the nervous and hormonal systems

Multicellular organisms coordinate their cells using nerves and hormones. This file covers cell signalling, types of neurone, the organisation of the nervous system, the spinal cord and reflexes, the regions of the brain, the endocrine system, the adrenal glands and the fight-or-flight response.

## Communication and cell signalling

*Refs: OCR A 5.1.1(a), (b) · Edexcel A 8.7 · Edexcel B 9.2 i, ii · OCR A 5.1.4(a). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The need for communication systems in multicellular organisms.
- Communication between cells by cell signalling.
- Endocrine communication by hormones; the difference between nervous and hormonal coordination.
- (Edexcel B) The two main modes of action of hormones.

### Explained

Cells in a multicellular organism are specialised and depend on each other. They must respond to changes in the internal and external environment, and coordinate their activities. Two systems do this: the nervous system and the hormonal (endocrine) system. Both rely on **cell signalling**: one cell releases a chemical that binds to a receptor on a target cell. In nerves the chemical (neurotransmitter) crosses a tiny gap; in hormones it travels in the blood.

| | Nervous | Hormonal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Signal | Electrical impulses along neurones, chemical at synapses | Chemicals (hormones) in the blood |
| Speed | Very fast | Slower |
| Duration of effect | Short | Can be long-lasting |
| Target | Very localised (specific cells) | Widespread (any cell with receptors) |
| Example | Withdrawal reflex | Growth, menstrual cycle, blood glucose |

**Endocrine glands** secrete hormones directly into the blood (ductless): pituitary, thyroid, pancreas (islets), adrenal glands, ovaries, testes. **Exocrine glands** secrete through ducts (salivary glands; the enzyme-making part of the pancreas).

**Two modes of hormone action** (Edexcel B 9.2 ii).

1. *Peptide and amine hormones* (insulin, glucagon, adrenaline): water-soluble, cannot cross the membrane. They bind to receptors on the cell-surface membrane and act through a **second messenger** such as cAMP (file 20).
2. *Steroid hormones* (oestrogen, testosterone, cortisol): lipid-soluble, diffuse through the membrane, bind to receptors in the cytoplasm or nucleus, and the hormone–receptor complex acts as a transcription factor, changing gene expression (file 24).

## Neurones and the organisation of the nervous system

*Refs: OCR A 5.1.3(b), 5.1.5(g) · OCR B 5.2.1(a) · Edexcel A 8.1 · Edexcel B 9.4 i, iv, v · Eduqas C3.5(a), OC.1(c). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The structure and functions of sensory, relay and motor neurones.
- The organisation of the mammalian nervous system: central and peripheral; somatic and autonomic; sympathetic and parasympathetic.
- Why the autonomic system is divided into sympathetic and parasympathetic parts.

### Explained

**Neurones.**

| | Sensory | Relay | Motor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carries impulses | From receptor to CNS | Within the CNS, between sensory and motor | From CNS to effector |
| Shape | Long dendron to the cell body; cell body part-way along, outside the CNS (in a ganglion); short axon | Short, many branched dendrites and axon | Cell body in the CNS, many dendrites; long axon |
| Myelin | Often | Usually not | Often |

**Organisation.**

- **Central nervous system (CNS):** brain and spinal cord.
- **Peripheral nervous system (PNS):** all the neurones outside the CNS; sensory neurones bring impulses in; motor neurones take them out.
  - **Somatic** (voluntary) system: motor neurones to skeletal muscle, under conscious control.
  - **Autonomic** (involuntary) system: motor neurones to smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands, not under conscious control.
    - **Sympathetic:** prepares the body for action ("fight or flight"). Neurotransmitter at the effector: noradrenaline. Increases heart rate and breathing, dilates pupils, inhibits digestion.
    - **Parasympathetic:** "rest and digest". Neurotransmitter: acetylcholine. Slows heart rate, constricts pupils, stimulates digestion.

The two autonomic divisions are **antagonistic**: they act on the same organs with opposite effects. Having both gives fine control in both directions (Edexcel B 9.4 v), like the separate negative feedback mechanisms in homeostasis.

## The spinal cord and reflexes

*Refs: OCR A 5.1.5(i) · OCR B 5.2.1(f) · Edexcel A 8.2 · Edexcel B 9.4 ii · Eduqas C3.5(b) to (e). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The structure of the spinal cord and spinal nerves.
- Reflex actions: the knee-jerk and blinking reflexes; their survival value.
- (Eduqas) The nerve net of Cnidaria compared with the mammalian nervous system.

### Explained

**Spinal cord.** In cross-section: a central canal filled with cerebrospinal fluid; an H- or butterfly-shaped area of **grey matter** (cell bodies of relay and motor neurones, and synapses) surrounded by **white matter** (myelinated axons running up and down the cord). The cord is protected by the vertebrae and membranes (meninges).

**Spinal nerves** leave the cord in pairs between vertebrae. Each joins the cord by two roots: the **dorsal root** carries sensory neurones in, with their cell bodies in a swelling called the dorsal root ganglion; the **ventral root** carries motor neurones out.

**Reflex arc** (file 18 for the basic arc): receptor → sensory neurone (dorsal root) → relay neurone (grey matter) → motor neurone (ventral root) → effector. Reflexes are rapid, involuntary and protective, and do not need the brain to decide (though the brain may be informed and can sometimes override them).

- **Knee-jerk reflex** (a spinal reflex with no relay neurone: sensory neurone synapses directly with motor neurone). Tapping the patellar tendon stretches the quadriceps muscle; stretch receptors (muscle spindles) send impulses; the motor neurone makes the quadriceps contract and the leg kicks. At the same time a relay neurone inhibits the antagonistic hamstring. It helps balance and posture. An absent or exaggerated knee-jerk can indicate nerve damage.
- **Blinking reflex** (a cranial reflex, through the brain stem). Touching the cornea (or a sudden bright light) causes both eyelids to close quickly, protecting the eye. Doctors test it to check brain stem function in unconscious patients.

**Survival value.** Speed (few synapses); no conscious decision needed; protects from damage; automatic control of posture and balance.

**Nerve net** (Eduqas). Cnidarians such as *Hydra* have no CNS. Neurones form a diffuse net through the body wall, with impulses spreading in all directions from the stimulus; most neurones are not myelinated and many synapses can pass impulses either way. The response is slow and spreads through the whole body. Mammals have a centralised system with a brain, one-way synapses, myelinated neurones and specialised neurone types, giving fast, precise, varied responses.

## The brain

*Refs: OCR A 5.1.5(h) · OCR B 5.2.1(b) · Edexcel A 8.8 · Edexcel B 9.4 iii · Eduqas OC.1(a), (b), (d). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The structure of the human brain and the functions of its parts: cerebrum (cerebral hemispheres), cerebellum, medulla oblongata, hypothalamus and pituitary gland (and for some boards the thalamus, hippocampus and basal ganglia).

### Explained

| Region | Position | Functions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cerebrum (cerebral hemispheres) | Largest part; two hemispheres joined by the corpus callosum; folded outer layer (cerebral cortex) | Conscious thought, memory, language, reasoning, emotions; vision (occipital lobe), hearing, touch and movement (sensory and motor areas). Left hemisphere controls the right side of the body and vice versa |
| Cerebellum | Back of the brain, under the cerebrum | Coordinates movement, balance and posture; fine control of muscles; learned movements (riding a bike) |
| Medulla oblongata | Base of the brain, at the top of the spinal cord | Controls automatic actions: heart rate (cardiac centre), breathing (ventilation centre), blood pressure (vasomotor), swallowing |
| Hypothalamus | Below the thalamus, above the pituitary | Homeostasis: body temperature, water balance (osmoreceptors, ADH); controls the pituitary; sleep, hunger, thirst |
| Pituitary gland | Below the hypothalamus | Posterior lobe releases ADH and oxytocin (made in the hypothalamus); anterior lobe makes and releases hormones (FSH, LH, growth hormone, TSH, ACTH) controlled by releasing hormones from the hypothalamus |
| Thalamus (Edexcel A) | Centre of the brain | Relays sensory information to the cortex |
| Hippocampus (Edexcel A) | In the temporal lobe | Forming long-term memories |
| Basal ganglia (Edexcel A) | Deep in the cerebrum | Selecting and starting movements (damaged in Parkinson's disease, file 34) |

**The hypothalamus links nerves and hormones** (Eduqas OC.1(d)). Neurosecretory cells in the hypothalamus make hormones (ADH, oxytocin) and pass them down their axons to the posterior pituitary for release. Other cells release hormones into blood vessels that carry them to the anterior pituitary, controlling its secretions. So nervous signals can produce hormonal responses.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the cerebellum controls conscious thought.
- Saying the pituitary controls body temperature.
- Saying the medulla controls voluntary movement.

## The adrenal glands and the fight-or-flight response

*Refs: OCR A 5.1.4(b), 5.1.5(j), (k) · Edexcel B 9.8 ii · Edexcel A 8.7. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The structure and functions of the adrenal glands.
- The coordination of responses by the nervous and endocrine systems in the fight-or-flight response.

### Explained

**Adrenal glands** sit on top of the kidneys.

- **Adrenal cortex** (outer layer) makes steroid hormones: glucocorticoids such as cortisol (regulates metabolism; raises blood glucose; helps the body cope with long-term stress), mineralocorticoids such as aldosterone (controls sodium and potassium in the blood, so blood pressure), and small amounts of sex hormones.
- **Adrenal medulla** (inner part) makes adrenaline and noradrenaline, released in response to impulses from sympathetic neurones.

**Fight or flight.**

1. A threat is detected by receptors; impulses go to the brain (cerebrum).
2. The hypothalamus is activated. It stimulates the sympathetic nervous system, which sends impulses directly to many organs and to the adrenal medulla, which releases adrenaline into the blood.
3. The hypothalamus also releases a hormone that makes the anterior pituitary release ACTH, which makes the adrenal cortex release cortisol.

Effects of adrenaline and the sympathetic system:

| Effect | Benefit |
| --- | --- |
| Heart rate and stroke volume increase | More blood to muscles |
| Breathing rate increases; bronchioles dilate | More oxygen taken in |
| Glycogen converted to glucose in liver (glycogenolysis) | More respiratory substrate |
| Blood diverted from gut and skin to muscles (vasoconstriction and vasodilation) | More oxygen and glucose to muscles |
| Pupils dilate | More light enters |
| Sweating; hairs stand up | Cooling; appear larger |
| Mental awareness increases | Faster response |

**Nervous and hormonal control of heart rate** (OCR A 5.1.5(k)): the cardiac centre in the medulla changes heart rate via sympathetic and parasympathetic (vagus) nerves (file 18); adrenaline also binds to receptors on the SAN and increases heart rate and the force of contraction.

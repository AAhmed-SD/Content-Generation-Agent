# Blood tests, blood donation and first aid

Blood is easy to sample and tells doctors a lot about health. This file covers the components of blood and what they do, blood smears and differential staining, counting cells with a haemocytometer, flow cytometry, blood groups and blood donation, measuring pulse and blood pressure, and the emergency treatment of heart attack and cardiac arrest.

## Blood and its components

*Refs: OCR B 2.1.1(c) · Edexcel B 4.4 vi, vii · Eduqas C3.2(d), (g). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The structure of blood as plasma and blood cells: erythrocytes, leucocytes (neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes) and platelets.
- The functions of blood: transport, defence, formation of lymph and tissue fluid.

### Explained

Blood is about 55% plasma and 45% cells.

**Plasma** is mostly water, carrying dissolved substances:

- nutrients (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, mineral ions)
- waste (CO₂ as hydrogencarbonate, urea)
- hormones
- plasma proteins (albumin keeps water potential low so water returns to capillaries; fibrinogen and prothrombin for clotting; antibodies)
- heat (distributed around the body).

| Component | Structure | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Erythrocytes (red blood cells) | Biconcave discs, about 7 µm; no nucleus or mitochondria; full of haemoglobin | Carry oxygen (and some CO₂); large surface area to volume ratio; flexible to squeeze through capillaries |
| Neutrophils | Lobed (multi-lobed) nucleus; granular cytoplasm with many lysosomes | Phagocytosis of bacteria; first to arrive at infections |
| Lymphocytes | Small; large round nucleus filling most of the cell; little cytoplasm | B cells make antibodies; T cells in cell-mediated immunity |
| Monocytes | Largest white cell; kidney-shaped nucleus | Leave the blood and become macrophages (phagocytosis, antigen presentation) |
| Eosinophils and basophils | Bilobed nucleus with red-staining granules (eosinophils); dark granules (basophils) | Parasite and allergic responses; histamine release |
| Platelets | Cell fragments, no nucleus | Blood clotting |

Excess tissue fluid drains into the lymphatic system (file 10).

## Blood smears, staining and counting cells

*Refs: OCR B 2.1.1(a), (b), (e), (f). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- How to prepare a blood smear (film) for light microscopy, and differential staining with Leishman's stain to identify leucocytes.
- Using a haemocytometer to count cells and convert to a concentration.
- The principles and uses of flow cytometry, including fluorescent labels.

### Explained

**Blood smear.** Place a small drop of blood (sterile animal blood or a prepared sample, following safety guidance) near one end of a slide. Hold a second slide at about 30° in front of the drop, draw it back to touch the drop so the blood spreads along its edge, then push it smoothly forwards to make a thin, even film that is one cell thick at the feathered end. Air-dry; fix with methanol.

**Differential staining with Leishman's stain.** A mixture of methylene blue (basic dye; stains acidic structures like DNA blue or purple) and eosin (acidic dye; stains basic structures like haemoglobin pink or red). Nuclei of white blood cells stain purple; red blood cells stain pink; granules stain differently. This makes it possible to tell the types of white cell apart and to do a **differential white cell count** (the percentage of each type). Normal proportions are roughly neutrophils 60%, lymphocytes 30%, monocytes 5%, eosinophils 3%, basophils under 1%. A raised neutrophil count suggests a bacterial infection; raised lymphocytes a viral infection; raised eosinophils allergy or parasites; very high abnormal white cells, leukaemia.

**Haemocytometer counts.** A thick glass slide with a counting chamber of known depth (0.1 mm) and an engraved grid of squares of known area.

1. Dilute the blood by a known factor (for red cells, often 1 in 200, using an isotonic diluting fluid so cells do not burst).
2. Place a coverslip on the chamber; fill it by capillary action.
3. Count cells in several squares under the microscope. To avoid counting cells twice, count cells touching the top and left lines but not the bottom and right lines.
4. Calculate: cells per mm³ = (number counted ÷ volume counted in mm³) × dilution factor.

Normal red cell count is about 4.5 to 6 million per mm³ (5 × 10¹² per dm³). A low count suggests anaemia.

**Flow cytometry.** Cells in suspension pass one at a time in a narrow stream through a laser beam. Detectors measure how much light each cell scatters (showing its size and how granular it is) and any fluorescence. Cells are first labelled with fluorescent antibodies that bind particular surface proteins (for example CD4 on helper T cells). Thousands of cells are analysed per second. Uses: full blood counts; counting CD4 T cells to monitor HIV infection (file 27); diagnosing and monitoring leukaemia and lymphoma; sorting cells.

### Worked example

A blood sample was diluted 1 in 200. In a haemocytometer, 480 red blood cells were counted in 80 small squares, each 0.0025 mm² with a chamber depth of 0.1 mm. Calculate the concentration of red cells in the original blood.

1. Volume of one small square = 0.0025 × 0.1 = 0.00025 mm³.
2. Volume counted = 80 × 0.00025 = 0.02 mm³.
3. Cells per mm³ of diluted blood = 480 ÷ 0.02 = 24 000.
4. Original blood = 24 000 × 200 = 4.8 × 10⁶ per mm³.

## Blood groups and blood donation

*Refs: OCR B 2.1.3(g). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The donation of blood; blood groups; the types and uses of stored blood products: whole blood, leucodepleted blood, packed red cells, platelets, clotting factors and plasma.

### Explained

**ABO blood groups.** Red blood cells carry antigens A, B, both or neither (inheritance in file 21). Plasma contains antibodies against the antigens the person does not have.

| Group | Antigens on red cells | Antibodies in plasma | Can receive red cells from |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | A | Anti-B | A, O |
| B | B | Anti-A | B, O |
| AB | A and B | None | A, B, AB, O (universal recipient) |
| O | None | Anti-A and anti-B | O only (O is the universal donor of red cells) |

If incompatible blood is given, the recipient's antibodies bind to the donor's red cells, causing agglutination (clumping) and destruction of the cells (haemolysis), which can be fatal.

**Rhesus (Rh) factor.** Rh-positive people have the D antigen; Rh-negative people do not and can make anti-D antibodies if exposed. Matters in transfusion and in pregnancy: a Rh-negative mother carrying a Rh-positive fetus may make anti-D antibodies, which in a later pregnancy could attack the fetus's red cells. Prevented by giving the mother anti-D injections (passive immunity, file 07) during pregnancy and after birth.

**Donation.** Donors are healthy adults (checked for anaemia with a finger-prick haemoglobin test, and screened by questionnaire). About 470 cm³ of blood is collected into a bag containing an anticoagulant (citrate, which binds calcium ions needed for clotting) and nutrients (glucose). Every donation is tested for blood group and for infections (HIV, hepatitis B and C, syphilis). Issues: donor safety; screening out donations that might carry infection; not enough donors of some groups and ethnic backgrounds; consent; religious views on transfusion.

**Blood products.** Whole blood is separated by centrifugation into components, so one donation helps several patients and each gets only what they need.

| Product | Use | Storage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whole blood | Rarely used now; major blood loss | Refrigerated |
| Leucodepleted blood | White cells filtered out (standard in the UK): reduces reactions and risk of transmitting some infections | |
| Packed red cells | Anaemia; blood loss in surgery or accidents | 4 °C for about 35 days |
| Platelets | Patients with low platelets (leukaemia, chemotherapy) who bleed easily | Room temperature, agitated; about 7 days |
| Plasma (fresh frozen) | Replaces clotting factors in severe bleeding or liver disease | Frozen, up to about 3 years |
| Clotting factors | Haemophilia (factor VIII or IX), now often made by GM cells | |

## Measuring pulse and blood pressure

*Refs: OCR B 2.2.1(g), 2.2.2(e). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The measurement and interpretation of pulse rate and blood pressure.
- Interpreting systolic and diastolic blood pressure (file 38 for health significance).

### Explained

**Pulse.** Each contraction of the left ventricle sends a pressure wave along the arteries, felt where an artery runs near the surface over a bone: the radial pulse (wrist) or carotid pulse (neck). Count beats for 15 seconds and multiply by 4 (or 30 seconds × 2). Use two fingers, not the thumb (which has its own pulse). Resting pulse is typically 60 to 100 beats per minute; lower in fit people. Pulse rhythm and strength also give information.

**Blood pressure.** Measured with a sphygmomanometer (manual or digital).

1. An inflatable cuff around the upper arm is pumped up until it stops blood flow in the brachial artery.
2. The pressure is slowly released while listening with a stethoscope below the cuff (or a digital sensor detects vibrations).
3. When blood first spurts through, a tapping sound starts: this is the **systolic** pressure (peak pressure as the ventricles contract).
4. When the sounds disappear because blood flows smoothly again: the **diastolic** pressure (lowest pressure as the heart relaxes).
5. Recorded as systolic/diastolic in mmHg, for example 120/80.

Readings are affected by exercise, stress, caffeine, posture and cuff size, so several readings at rest are taken. Hypertension: 140/90 or higher (file 38).

## Emergency treatment

*Refs: OCR B 2.2.1(i), 2.2.3(d). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The first-aid treatment of a person with a suspected heart attack or cardiac arrest; the use of a defibrillator.
- How expired air resuscitation (rescue breaths) is carried out.

### Explained

**Heart attack** (myocardial infarction, file 38): a coronary artery is blocked, so part of the heart muscle is starved of oxygen. The person is usually conscious and breathing, with crushing chest pain that may spread to the arm or jaw, breathlessness, sweating and nausea. First aid: call 999; sit the person down in a comfortable position (half-sitting); give one 300 mg aspirin to chew slowly if they are not allergic (it reduces platelet aggregation, limiting the clot); monitor and be ready to start CPR.

**Cardiac arrest:** the heart stops pumping effectively (often ventricular fibrillation, where the ventricles quiver irregularly). The person is unresponsive and not breathing normally. Without circulation, brain cells begin to die within minutes.

1. Check for danger; check response; open the airway (head tilt, chin lift); check breathing for no more than 10 seconds.
2. Call 999 and send someone for an **automated external defibrillator (AED)**.
3. Start **CPR**: 30 chest compressions (centre of the chest, about 5 to 6 cm deep, 100 to 120 per minute), which squeeze the heart between the sternum and spine and keep blood flowing to the brain.
4. Then 2 **rescue breaths** (expired air resuscitation), if trained: tilt the head, pinch the nose, seal your mouth over theirs and blow steadily until the chest rises. Expired air still contains about 16% oxygen (air has about 21%), enough to oxygenate the casualty's blood. Continue 30:2.
5. When the AED arrives, follow its spoken instructions: pads are placed on the bare chest; the machine analyses the heart rhythm and, if it detects a shockable rhythm (ventricular fibrillation), delivers an electric shock. The shock depolarises all the heart muscle at once, stopping the chaotic activity, so the SAN can restore a normal rhythm. No one touches the casualty during the shock.
6. Continue CPR until help arrives or the person starts breathing normally.

### Common mistakes

- Saying a heart attack and a cardiac arrest are the same.
- Saying a defibrillator "restarts a stopped heart"; it stops chaotic electrical activity so the normal pacemaker can take over.
- Saying group O blood can receive any blood (it is AB that can receive all ABO groups of red cells).
- Counting cells on all four edges of haemocytometer squares.

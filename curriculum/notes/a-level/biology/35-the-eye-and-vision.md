# The eye and vision

The eye focuses light onto the retina, where rods and cones turn it into nerve impulses. This file covers the structure of the eye and retina, the pupil reflex, accommodation, how rods and cones work, how vision is tested, and how vision changes with age.

## Overview

A-level only. Rods and cones compared (sensitivity, acuity, convergence) and rhodopsin are explained in file 18, which AQA uses. The visual cortex and its critical period are in file 34.

## Structure of the eye and retina

*Refs: OCR B 5.2.2(a), (c) · Edexcel B 9.7 i, iii · Edexcel A 8.5. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The structure of the eye and the functions of its parts.
- The structure of the human retina and the distribution of rods and cones.

### Explained

| Part | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Sclera | Tough white outer coat; protects; muscles attach |
| Cornea | Transparent front; refracts (bends) most of the light entering the eye |
| Conjunctiva | Thin protective membrane over the front |
| Iris | Coloured ring of muscle; controls the size of the pupil |
| Pupil | Hole in the iris; lets light in |
| Lens | Transparent, flexible; fine-focuses light onto the retina |
| Ciliary muscle and suspensory ligaments | Change the shape of the lens (accommodation) |
| Aqueous and vitreous humour | Transparent fluids that keep the eye's shape |
| Retina | Light-sensitive layer of rods and cones and neurones |
| Fovea | Small area of the retina opposite the lens, packed with cones; sharpest vision |
| Blind spot | Where the optic nerve leaves; no receptors |
| Choroid | Layer with blood vessels and dark pigment that stops light reflecting inside the eye |
| Optic nerve | Carries impulses to the brain |

**The retina** has three main layers of cells. Strangely, light must pass through the neurone layers first:

1. **Ganglion cells** (nearest the light): their axons form the optic nerve.
2. **Bipolar neurones** (middle): link rods and cones to ganglion cells.
3. **Rods and cones** (at the back, next to the pigmented layer).

**Distribution.** The fovea has only cones, densely packed, each with its own bipolar neurone: high acuity and colour vision in bright light. Further from the fovea, rods increase and cones decrease; at the edges there are almost only rods, many sharing one bipolar neurone: high sensitivity in dim light, low acuity. This is why you see faint stars better by looking slightly to one side of them. There are about 120 million rods and 6 million cones in each eye.

**How rods respond** (Edexcel B 9.7 ii; detail in file 18). Light breaks down rhodopsin into retinal and opsin; this closes sodium channels; the rod hyperpolarises and stops releasing an inhibitory neurotransmitter onto the bipolar cell; the bipolar cell depolarises and passes impulses on to a ganglion cell, which sends action potentials to the brain.

## The pupil reflex and accommodation

*Refs: Edexcel A 8.2 · OCR B 5.2.2(b). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- How the pupil reflex controls the amount of light entering the eye.
- How the eye focuses on near and distant objects (accommodation).

### Explained

**Pupil reflex.** Protects the retina from bright light and lets in more light when it is dim.

- *Bright light:* photoreceptors in the retina send impulses along the optic nerve to the brain (midbrain). Impulses pass along parasympathetic motor neurones to the iris. The **circular muscles** contract and the **radial muscles** relax; the pupil constricts.
- *Dim light:* sympathetic motor neurones cause the radial muscles to contract and circular muscles to relax; the pupil dilates.

Both eyes respond even if light is shone into only one. Doctors use the reflex to test brain stem function.

**Accommodation.**

| | Near object | Distant object |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ciliary muscle | Contracts | Relaxes |
| Suspensory ligaments | Slacken | Pulled tight |
| Lens | Becomes fatter (more convex), refracts more | Pulled thinner, refracts less |

## Testing vision and age-related changes

*Refs: OCR B 5.2.2(d), 5.2.3(b). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- How the activity of the retina and visual function are assessed.
- Age-related changes in vision.

### Explained

**Assessing visual function.**

- *Visual acuity:* a Snellen chart (rows of letters getting smaller) read from 6 m; 6/6 is normal; 6/12 means the person reads at 6 m what a typical person reads at 12 m. Tests the fovea (cones).
- *Colour vision:* Ishihara plates (numbers made of coloured dots); people with red-green colour blindness (an X-linked recessive condition, file 21, affecting cone pigments) cannot see some numbers.
- *Visual field:* the person looks at a central point and reports when lights appear in different parts of the field; gaps show damage to the retina or optic pathways (for example in glaucoma).
- *Retinal examination:* an ophthalmoscope or retinal camera views the retina and blood vessels (detecting diabetic retinopathy, damage from high blood pressure).
- *Electroretinogram:* electrodes record the electrical response of rods and cones to flashes of light.
- *Eye pressure test* (tonometry) for glaucoma.

**Age-related changes** (OCR B 5.2.3(b)).

- **Presbyopia:** the lens becomes stiffer with age, so it cannot become fat enough to focus on near objects; corrected with reading glasses (convex lenses).
- **Cataracts:** the lens becomes cloudy as its proteins change, causing blurred vision and glare; treated by replacing the lens with a plastic one.
- **Age-related macular degeneration (AMD):** damage to the macula (around the fovea) causes loss of central vision; "wet" AMD (leaky new blood vessels) can be slowed by injections of drugs that block a growth factor; "dry" AMD has no cure. Stem cell treatments are being tested.
- **Glaucoma:** raised pressure in the eye damages the optic nerve, causing loss of the visual field from the edges; treated with eye drops or surgery.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the radial muscles contract in bright light.
- Saying the lens does most of the focusing (the cornea does most; the lens adjusts).
- Saying cones are spread evenly over the retina.

## Mark scheme

1. Rods and cones at the back / next to pigment layer (1); bipolar neurones in the middle (1); ganglion cells nearest the light, axons form optic nerve (1).
2. Fovea has only cones / densely packed (1); each cone connects to its own bipolar neurone / no convergence (1); brain receives separate information from each cone, so high acuity (1).
3. Light detected by photoreceptors in the retina (1); impulses along optic nerve to brain (1); impulses along parasympathetic / motor neurones to iris (1); circular muscles contract, radial relax, pupil constricts (1).
4. Ciliary muscle contracts (1); suspensory ligaments slacken (1); lens becomes fatter / more convex, refracts light more (1).
5. Rods are concentrated away from the fovea / at the edges (1); rods are sensitive to dim light (because of convergence / summation) (1).
6. Any two (2 each): Snellen chart, visual acuity / fovea; Ishihara plates, colour vision / cones; visual field test, gaps from retina or nerve damage; ophthalmoscope, retinal damage.
7. Lens loses elasticity / stiffens with age (1); cannot become fat enough to focus near objects / presbyopia (1).

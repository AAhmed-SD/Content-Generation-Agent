# Cell structure, viruses and methods of studying cells

Cells are the basic units of life. This file covers the organelles of eukaryotic cells, prokaryotic cells and viruses, microscopes and magnification, and cell fractionation.

## 3.2.1.1 Structure of eukaryotic cells

*Refs: AQA 3.2.1.1 · Edexcel A 3.2, 3.3, 3.5, 3.13, 4.7, 4.8 · Edexcel B 2.1 i, ii, v · OCR A 2.1.1(g) to (j), 2.1.6(h), (i) · OCR B 2.1.1(g), (o) · Eduqas CC2(a), (c), (d), CC5(o). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

The structure of eukaryotic cells, limited to the standard cells of plants, animals, fungi and protoctists, and the function of:

- cell-surface membrane
- nucleus (with chromosomes, nuclear envelope with pores, and nucleolus)
- mitochondria
- chloroplasts (in plants and algae)
- Golgi apparatus and Golgi vesicles
- lysosomes (a type of Golgi vesicle that releases lysozymes)
- ribosomes
- rough and smooth endoplasmic reticulum
- cell wall (cellulose in plants and algae; chitin in fungi)
- cell vacuole (in plants).

Also: in multicellular organisms, cells are specialised and organised into tissues, organs and organ systems. Recognise organelles in electron micrographs.

### Explained

| Organelle | Structure | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nucleus | Double membrane (nuclear envelope) with pores; chromatin (DNA with histones); one or more nucleoli | Holds the genetic information; controls the cell; nucleolus makes ribosomal RNA and assembles ribosomes; pores let mRNA out |
| Mitochondrion | Double membrane; inner membrane folded into cristae; matrix with enzymes, ribosomes and DNA | Aerobic respiration; makes ATP |
| Chloroplast | Double membrane; thylakoids stacked into grana; fluid stroma with enzymes, starch grains, ribosomes and DNA | Photosynthesis |
| Rough ER | Flattened membrane sacs (cisternae) covered with ribosomes | Makes and transports proteins |
| Smooth ER | Membrane sacs without ribosomes | Makes and processes lipids, including steroids |
| Golgi apparatus | Stack of flattened membrane sacs, with vesicles | Modifies proteins (adds carbohydrate to make glycoproteins), packages them into vesicles; makes lysosomes |
| Golgi vesicles | Small membrane sacs | Carry proteins to the cell-surface membrane for secretion |
| Lysosome | Vesicle containing lysozymes (hydrolytic enzymes) | Hydrolyse pathogens taken in by phagocytes, worn-out organelles, and dead cells |
| Ribosome | Two subunits of RNA and protein, no membrane; 80S in eukaryotic cytoplasm, 70S in prokaryotes, mitochondria and chloroplasts | Site of protein synthesis |
| Cell wall | Cellulose microfibrils (plants, algae); chitin (fungi) | Strength; stops the cell bursting when turgid |
| Vacuole | Large, surrounded by the tonoplast, filled with cell sap | Keeps cell turgid; stores pigments and some solutes |
| Centrioles (OCR A, Edexcel) | Two bundles of microtubules at right angles; not in most plant cells | Organise the spindle in cell division |
| Cytoskeleton (OCR A) | Network of microfilaments, microtubules and intermediate filaments | Support; moves organelles and vesicles; cell movement; forms cilia, flagella and the spindle |
| Cilia and flagella (OCR A) | Microtubules in a 9 + 2 arrangement | Move fluid past cells (cilia) or move the cell (flagella) |

Extra plant structures (Edexcel A):

- Amyloplasts store starch (abundant in potato tubers).
- Plasmodesmata are channels through cell walls that join the cytoplasm of neighbouring cells.
- Pits are thin regions of the cell wall where water can pass between cells.
- The middle lamella, made mostly of calcium pectate, sticks neighbouring plant cells together.

**Making and secreting a protein** (AQA, OCR A, Edexcel A). The gene is transcribed in the nucleus; mRNA leaves through a nuclear pore; ribosomes on the rough ER translate it; the polypeptide enters the ER and folds; it is carried in a vesicle to the Golgi apparatus; the Golgi modifies it (for example adds carbohydrate) and packages it; a vesicle carries it to the cell-surface membrane; the vesicle fuses with the membrane and releases the protein by exocytosis. ATP from mitochondria powers the transport. (Eduqas CC5(o): some polypeptides are joined or modified after translation, as in haemoglobin or insulin.)

**Specialised cells** (all boards). The organelles a cell has match its job. Cells in a pancreas that secrete enzymes have lots of rough ER and Golgi; sperm and muscle cells have many mitochondria; root hair cells have no chloroplasts. Examples OCR A names: erythrocytes (no nucleus, biconcave, full of haemoglobin), neutrophils (lobed nucleus, many lysosomes), squamous and ciliated epithelia, palisade cells, guard cells, root hair cells.

**Levels of organisation.** Cells → tissues (similar cells working together, such as squamous epithelium, xylem) → organs (several tissues for one or more functions, such as stomach, leaf) → organ systems (organs working together, such as the digestive system).

### Common mistakes

- Saying mitochondria "make energy". They release energy from respiratory substrates and make ATP.
- Saying ribosomes are organelles with a membrane.
- Saying plant cells do not have mitochondria.
- Mixing up lysosomes (vesicles) and lysozymes (the enzymes inside).
- Describing protein secretion with the Golgi before the rough ER.

## 3.2.1.2 Structure of prokaryotic cells and of viruses

*Refs: AQA 3.2.1.2 · Edexcel A 3.1, 3.4, 6.5 · Edexcel B 2.1 iii, 2.2 i · OCR A 2.1.1(k) · OCR B 2.1.1(h) · Eduqas CC2(b), (c). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Prokaryotic cells are much smaller than eukaryotic cells. They have cytoplasm without membrane-bound organelles; smaller ribosomes (70S); no nucleus, with a single circular DNA molecule not associated with proteins; and a cell wall containing murein (a glycoprotein, also called peptidoglycan).
- Many have one or more plasmids, a capsule around the cell and one or more flagella.
- Viruses are acellular and non-living. Viral particles have genetic material, a capsid and attachment proteins. (The material asked for at AQA AS is limited to these.)

### Explained

| Feature | Prokaryote | Eukaryote |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Size | About 0.5 to 5 µm | About 10 to 100 µm |
| Nucleus | None; DNA free in cytoplasm | Present |
| DNA | Circular, not bound to histones; plasmids | Linear, in chromosomes bound to histones |
| Ribosomes | 70S | 80S in cytoplasm |
| Organelles with membranes | None | Many |
| Cell wall | Murein | Cellulose (plants), chitin (fungi), none (animals) |

Plasmids often carry genes for antibiotic resistance and can be passed between bacteria. The capsule (slime layer) protects against drying and helps bacteria avoid phagocytes.

**Viruses.** A virus particle has a nucleic acid core (DNA or RNA, never both) inside a protein coat called a capsid. Some have a lipid envelope taken from the host's membrane. Attachment proteins on the surface bind to receptors on host cells, which is why each virus infects only certain cell types. Viruses cannot reproduce outside a host cell. Edexcel B classifies viruses by nucleic acid and structure: DNA viruses (such as λ phage, a bacteriophage), RNA viruses (such as tobacco mosaic virus, Ebola) and retroviruses (such as HIV, which carry reverse transcriptase).

### Common mistakes

- Saying bacteria have "no DNA" or "no ribosomes".
- Saying viruses are prokaryotes. They are not cells at all.
- Saying the bacterial cell wall is made of cellulose.

## 3.2.1.3 Methods of studying cells

*Refs: AQA 3.2.1.3 · Edexcel B 2.1 vi, vii, CP2 · OCR A 2.1.1(a) to (f) · OCR B 2.1.1(c), (d), (i) · Eduqas CC2(a). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The principles and limitations of optical microscopes, transmission electron microscopes (TEM) and scanning electron microscopes (SEM).
- Measuring the size of an object with an optical microscope using an eyepiece graticule calibrated with a stage micrometer.
- The difference between magnification and resolution.
- magnification = size of image ÷ size of real object.
- The principles of cell fractionation and ultracentrifugation to separate organelles.
- Appreciate that there was a considerable period when artefacts in electron micrographs were mistaken for cell organelles.

### Explained

**Magnification** is how many times larger the image is than the object. **Resolution** is the minimum distance apart two objects can be and still be seen as separate. Magnifying beyond the resolution limit just makes a bigger blur.

| | Light (optical) | TEM | SEM |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Beam | Light | Electrons pass through | Electrons bounce off the surface |
| Resolution | About 200 nm | About 0.1 nm in practice | About 10 nm (lower than TEM) |
| Image | 2D, colour | 2D, black and white, internal detail | 3D surface, black and white |
| Specimen | Living or dead | Dead; vacuum; very thin sections; stained with heavy metals | Dead; vacuum; coated with metal |
| Limits | Cannot see small organelles | Complex preparation may create artefacts; cannot view living cells | Lower resolution than TEM; no internal detail |

Electrons have a much shorter wavelength than light, which is why electron microscopes resolve finer detail.

**Artefacts** are things that appear in a preparation but are not in the living cell, caused by fixing, staining or cutting. Scientists checked whether a structure was real by preparing specimens in different ways and seeing whether it always appeared.

**Graticule calibration.**

1. Line up the eyepiece graticule (a scale with arbitrary units) with the stage micrometer (a slide with a scale of known size, often 1 division = 10 µm).
2. Count how many graticule divisions match a known length on the micrometer.
3. One graticule division = that length ÷ number of divisions.
4. Recalibrate at each objective lens, because the graticule stays the same size but the specimen is magnified more.

**Slides and staining** (OCR A, Edexcel B, OCR B). Dry mounts (pollen, hair), wet mounts (onion epidermis in water, with a coverslip lowered at an angle to avoid bubbles), squash preparations (root tip) and sections. Stains increase contrast because most cell structures are transparent: iodine stains starch, methylene blue stains DNA, eosin stains cytoplasm. Differential staining shows different structures in different colours (as in Gram staining). Biological drawings: sharp pencil, clear continuous lines, no shading, label lines ruled and not crossing, magnification or scale stated.

**Cell fractionation.**

1. Homogenise the tissue (blender) to break cells open.
2. Keep the solution ice-cold (reduces enzyme activity that would break down organelles), isotonic (same water potential, so organelles do not gain or lose water and burst or shrivel) and buffered (keeps pH constant so proteins are not denatured).
3. Filter to remove large debris.
4. Ultracentrifugation: spin at a low speed; the heaviest organelles (nuclei) form a pellet. Remove the supernatant and spin it faster; the next pellet is mitochondria (and chloroplasts in plants); faster again gives ER, Golgi and lysosomes; fastest gives ribosomes.

### Worked example

1. An image of a mitochondrion is 24 mm long. The magnification is ×8000. How long is the real mitochondrion in µm?

Real size = image ÷ magnification = 24 mm ÷ 8000 = 0.003 mm = 3 µm.

2. At ×400, 25 eyepiece graticule divisions line up with 0.1 mm on the stage micrometer. A cell is 18 graticule divisions long. How long is it?

One division = 0.1 mm ÷ 25 = 0.004 mm = 4 µm. Cell = 18 × 4 = 72 µm.

### Common mistakes

- Defining resolution as "how clear the image is".
- Forgetting to convert units: 1 mm = 1000 µm; 1 µm = 1000 nm.
- Saying the solution must be cold "to stop the organelles moving".
- Saying the TEM gives a 3D image.

## Check questions

1. Describe how a protein made on the rough ER is secreted from the cell. (4 marks)
2. Give three structures found in a bacterial cell but not in an animal cell. (3 marks)
3. Explain why a TEM gives higher resolution than an optical microscope. (1 mark)
4. Give two limitations of a TEM. (2 marks)

2. Any three: cell wall (of murein); plasmid; capsule; flagellum; circular DNA free in cytoplasm; 70S ribosomes (accept "smaller ribosomes").

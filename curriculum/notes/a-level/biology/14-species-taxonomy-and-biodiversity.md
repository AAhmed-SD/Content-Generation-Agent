# Species, taxonomy, biodiversity and investigating diversity

Biologists name and group organisms and measure how varied life is in a place. This file covers the species concept, classification, measuring biodiversity, and comparing species using DNA and proteins.

## 3.4.5 Species and taxonomy

*Refs: AQA 3.4.5 · Edexcel A 4.6 · Edexcel B 3.1 i to vii · OCR A 4.2.2(a) to (d) · OCR B 3.1.3(a) to (d), (f) · Eduqas C2.1(a) to (g). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Two organisms belong to the same species if they are able to produce fertile offspring. Courtship behaviour as a necessary precursor to successful mating; its role in recognising members of the same species, identifying a mate that is capable of breeding, forming a pair bond, synchronising mating, and becoming able to breed.
- A phylogenetic classification system attempts to arrange species into groups based on their evolutionary origins and relationships. It uses a hierarchy in which smaller groups are placed within larger groups, with no overlap between groups. Each group is called a taxon (plural taxa).
- One hierarchy comprises the taxa: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species.
- Each species is universally identified by a binomial consisting of the name of its genus and species, eg *Homo sapiens*.
- Appreciate that advances in immunology and genome sequencing help to clarify evolutionary relationships between organisms.

### Explained

**The species concept.** A species is a group of similar organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring. A horse and a donkey can mate, but their offspring (a mule) is infertile, so horses and donkeys are separate species.

**Limits of the definition** (Edexcel B 3.1 ii, iii). It cannot be used for organisms that reproduce asexually (bacteria), for extinct organisms known only from fossils, or for populations that never meet. Some species hybridise and produce fertile young (some plants, some gulls). So biologists also use the morphological concept (similar appearance) and the phylogenetic concept (a group sharing a recent common ancestor, defined by DNA).

**Courtship.** Courtship behaviours (displays, songs, dances) are species-specific. They let animals:

- recognise a member of their own species, so they do not waste energy on matings that give no fertile offspring
- recognise a mate of the opposite sex who is sexually mature and fertile (or receptive)
- form a pair bond, so both parents raise the young
- synchronise mating so it happens when eggs are available.

**Classification.** Organisms are placed in a hierarchy: **D**omain, **K**ingdom, **P**hylum, **C**lass, **O**rder, **F**amily, **G**enus, **S**pecies. Each group contains organisms that share a more recent common ancestor the further down you go. Groups do not overlap.

Example, the grey wolf: Eukarya, Animalia, Chordata, Mammalia, Carnivora, Canidae, *Canis*, *lupus*.

**Binomial naming.** Genus (capital first letter) plus species (lower case), in italics or underlined. It is universal, so it avoids confusion from different common names.

**Five kingdoms and three domains** (OCR A, Edexcel A, Edexcel B, Eduqas).

| Kingdom | Main features |
| --- | --- |
| Prokaryotae | Unicellular; no nucleus; murein walls; 70S ribosomes |
| Protoctista | Mostly unicellular eukaryotes; some like plants, some like animals (algae, amoeba) |
| Fungi | Eukaryotic; chitin walls; saprotrophic nutrition; hyphae; spores |
| Plantae | Multicellular; cellulose walls; chlorophyll; autotrophic |
| Animalia | Multicellular; no cell walls; heterotrophic; nervous coordination |

In 1990 Carl Woese proposed three domains above the kingdoms: **Bacteria**, **Archaea** and **Eukarya**. Evidence: sequences of ribosomal RNA showed archaea are as different from bacteria as both are from eukaryotes. Archaea have different membrane lipids, different RNA polymerase (more like eukaryotes'), and histone-like proteins; bacteria have murein walls but archaea do not. This divides Prokaryotae into two domains.

**Validating new ideas** (Edexcel A, Edexcel B). A new classification is published in a scientific journal after peer review (other experts check the method and conclusions), discussed at conferences, and tested by other scientists. If repeated evidence supports it, it becomes accepted. The three-domain model went through this.

**Phylogeny.** A phylogenetic tree shows evolutionary relationships. Branch points show common ancestors; species that branch apart more recently are more closely related. Classification is now based on phylogeny.

**Evidence used to classify** (OCR B, Eduqas, Edexcel B):

- physical features (anatomy, embryology)
- DNA base sequences and the amino acid sequences of proteins (more different = more distant)
- immunological comparisons: antibodies against a protein from one species are mixed with the same protein from another; the more precipitate forms, the more similar the proteins, so the more closely related the species
- DNA barcoding (OCR B): sequencing a short, standard gene region (in animals, part of the mitochondrial gene for cytochrome c oxidase I; in plants, chloroplast genes) and matching it against a database to identify a species quickly
- gel electrophoresis of DNA or proteins (Edexcel B; method in file 25).

**Language as a model** (OCR B). Languages change over time by small changes passed on, and split when groups of speakers become separated, much as species do. Linguists group words by shared features and build family trees of languages, using the same kind of methods as phylogenetics. It is a model, not evidence for biological evolution.

### Common mistakes

- Writing the binomial with a capital letter for the species name.
- Saying classification is based on "what organisms look like". Modern classification is based on evolutionary relationships.
- Saying "the more groups two species share, the less related they are".

## 3.4.6 Biodiversity within a community

*Refs: AQA 3.4.6 · Edexcel A 4.1, 4.2, 4.3 · Edexcel B 3.2 ii, 3.3 i · OCR A 4.2.1(a) to (d) · OCR B 3.1.3(h), 4.3.1(l) · Eduqas C2.1(h) to (j). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Biodiversity can relate to a range of habitats, from a small local habitat to the Earth.
- Species richness is a measure of the number of different species in a community.
- An index of diversity describes the relationship between the number of species in a community and the number of individuals in each species. Calculation of an index of diversity (d) from d = N(N − 1) ÷ Σn(n − 1), where N = total number of organisms of all species and n = total number of organisms of each species.
- Farming techniques reduce biodiversity. The balance between conservation and farming.

### Explained

**Levels of biodiversity** (OCR A, Edexcel B): habitat diversity (the number of different habitats in an area), species diversity (species richness and evenness) and genetic diversity (the variety of alleles within a species).

**Species richness** counts species but ignores how many of each. **Species evenness** describes how equal the numbers of each species are. Two woods may each have 10 species, but if one is 95% oak it is less diverse than one with all ten in similar numbers. An index of diversity combines both.

**Endemism** (Edexcel A): an endemic species is found naturally in only one place (such as the Galápagos giant tortoise). Areas with many endemic species are priorities for conservation.

**Niche** (Edexcel A, Edexcel B). An organism's niche is its role in its habitat: what it eats, what eats it, where and when it feeds, the conditions it tolerates. No two species occupy exactly the same niche in a habitat for long: one outcompetes the other. Adaptations fit organisms to their niche.

**Farming and biodiversity.** Farming reduces biodiversity by: clearing woodland and removing hedgerows; growing monocultures; using pesticides and herbicides; draining wetlands; overgrazing; using fertilisers that favour a few fast-growing species. Ways to balance farming and conservation: keep and replant hedgerows, leave field margins uncultivated, rotate crops, use integrated pest management, maintain ponds and wetlands, pay farmers through agri-environment schemes.

### Worked example

A pond survey found: species A 10, B 5, C 3, D 2.

**AQA / Edexcel A index:**

1. N = 20. N(N − 1) = 20 × 19 = 380.
2. Σn(n − 1) = (10 × 9) + (5 × 4) + (3 × 2) + (2 × 1) = 90 + 20 + 6 + 2 = 118.
3. d = 380 ÷ 118 = 3.22.

A higher d means more diversity. The lowest possible value is 1 (only one species).

**OCR A / Eduqas Simpson's index:** D = 1 − Σ(n/N)².

1. (10/20)² = 0.25; (5/20)² = 0.0625; (3/20)² = 0.0225; (2/20)² = 0.01.
2. Σ = 0.345. D = 1 − 0.345 = 0.655.

D lies between 0 and 1; values nearer 1 mean higher diversity.

### Common mistakes

- Saying species richness takes numbers of individuals into account.
- Using N² instead of N(N − 1) in the AQA formula.
- Comparing an AQA index with a Simpson's index; they are on different scales.

## 3.4.7 Investigating diversity

*Refs: AQA 3.4.7 · Edexcel A 4.2 · Edexcel B 3.1 iv, v · OCR A 4.2.1(b), (e) · OCR B 3.1.3(i) · Eduqas C2.1(e), (k), (l). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Genetic diversity within, or between, species can be made by comparing: the frequency of measurable or observable characteristics; the base sequence of DNA; the base sequence of mRNA; the amino acid sequence of the proteins encoded by DNA and mRNA.
- Interpret data relating to similarities and differences in the base sequences of DNA and in the amino acid sequences of proteins to suggest relationships between different organisms within a species and between species.
- Appreciate that gene technology has caused a change in the methods of investigating genetic diversity; inferring DNA differences from measurable or observable characteristics has been replaced by direct investigation of DNA sequences.
- Quantitative investigations of variation within a species involve: collecting data from random samples; calculating a mean value and the standard deviation of that mean; interpreting mean values and their standard deviations.

### Explained

**Comparing species.** Closely related species share a recent common ancestor, so they have had less time for mutations to build up. Their DNA base sequences, and so their mRNA and amino acid sequences, are more similar. Count the differences: fewer differences mean a closer relationship.

Comparing observable characteristics was the old method. Its problems: many characteristics are controlled by many genes and affected by the environment, and similar features can evolve separately (convergent evolution). DNA sequencing is now fast and cheap, and compares genes directly.

**Random sampling** (all boards). To avoid bias, choose sample sites at random: lay out two tape measures at right angles and use random numbers as coordinates. Take a large sample to reduce the effect of chance. Non-random sampling (OCR A) includes systematic sampling along a transect (when there is a gradient), opportunistic sampling (quick but biased) and stratified sampling (sampling each area in proportion to its size). Field methods are in file 23.

**Mean and standard deviation.** The mean is the average. The standard deviation (SD) measures the spread of data around the mean: about 68% of values lie within 1 SD of the mean, about 95% within 2 SD. If two samples' means ± SD overlap a lot, the difference between them is probably due to chance. Formula and test in file 26.

**Genetic biodiversity within a species** (OCR A, OCR B). Proportion of polymorphic gene loci = number of polymorphic loci ÷ total number of loci tested. A locus is polymorphic if it has more than one allele in the population (commonly with the most common allele below 99% frequency). A higher proportion means more genetic diversity. Eduqas also lists DNA fingerprinting for comparing genetic diversity (file 25).

### Worked example

The amino acid sequence of one protein was compared in four species. Differences from species W: X 2, Y 14, Z 9. Which species is most closely related to W?

X: fewest amino acid differences, so it shares the most recent common ancestor with W. Order of relatedness to W: X, then Z, then Y.

Genetic diversity: 40 gene loci were tested in a population; 13 were polymorphic. Proportion = 13 ÷ 40 = 0.325.

### Common mistakes

- Saying that the same amino acid sequence means identical DNA. The code is degenerate, so DNA can differ.
- Saying a large standard deviation means "the data are wrong". It means more spread.
- Choosing sample sites "randomly" by walking about.

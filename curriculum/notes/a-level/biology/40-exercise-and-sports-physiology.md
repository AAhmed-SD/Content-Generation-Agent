# Exercise and sports physiology

Exercise changes how the body supplies oxygen and energy to muscles. This file covers the control of breathing and heart rate during exercise, cardiac output, oxygen deficit and lactate, aerobic fitness and VO₂ max, the effects of exercise on health, medical technology for sports injuries, and performance-enhancing substances and their ethics.

## Responses to exercise

*Refs: Edexcel A 7.7, 7.9 · Edexcel B 5.5 iii · OCR B 2.2.1(e), (f), 4.1.2(a), (j). Level: A-level only (AS for OCR B 2.2.1).*

### What you need to know

- Cardiac output; how ventilation and heart rate are controlled and change during exercise.
- What happens to lactate after anaerobic respiration; oxygen deficit and EPOC.
- The short-term and long-term effects of exercise on the body.

### Explained

**Cardiac output** = stroke volume × heart rate. At rest about 5 dm³ min⁻¹; in hard exercise it can rise above 20 dm³ min⁻¹ (more in trained athletes).

**Control of heart rate during exercise.**

- Chemoreceptors (aorta, carotid arteries, medulla) detect the rise in CO₂ and fall in pH; stretch receptors in muscles and tendons detect movement.
- The cardiovascular centre in the medulla sends more impulses along sympathetic nerves to the SAN: heart rate rises.
- Adrenaline (released even before exercise starts, in anticipation) raises heart rate and stroke volume.
- More blood returns to the heart (venous return), stretching the ventricles, which contract more strongly (larger stroke volume).

**Control of ventilation.**

- The **ventilation centre** in the medulla controls breathing rate and depth. It sends impulses along nerves to the intercostal muscles and diaphragm.
- During exercise, more CO₂ lowers the pH of blood and of cerebrospinal fluid; chemoreceptors in the medulla, aorta and carotid arteries detect this and send impulses to the ventilation centre; breathing rate and depth (tidal volume) increase.
- Stretch receptors in the lungs stop over-inflation; receptors in muscles and joints signal movement, increasing ventilation quickly at the start.
- Minute ventilation = tidal volume × breathing rate.

**Oxygen deficit and lactate.**

- At the start of exercise, the oxygen supply cannot rise quickly enough, so muscles use phosphocreatine and anaerobic respiration. The difference between the oxygen needed and the oxygen supplied is the **oxygen deficit**.
- After exercise, breathing and heart rate stay raised. The extra oxygen taken in is **EPOC** (excess post-exercise oxygen consumption; older name: oxygen debt). It is used to:
  - oxidise lactate back to pyruvate (and respire it), or convert it to glucose and glycogen in the liver (Cori cycle)
  - resynthesise phosphocreatine and ATP
  - reoxygenate myoglobin and haemoglobin
  - meet the raised metabolic rate while temperature returns to normal.
- Lactate lowers pH (as lactic acid), inhibiting enzymes and contributing to muscle fatigue. It is carried in the blood to the liver.

**Short-term effects of exercise** (OCR B): higher heart rate, stroke volume and cardiac output; deeper, faster breathing; vasodilation of arterioles to muscles and vasoconstriction to the gut; more sweating; rising body temperature; more lactate in hard exercise.

**Long-term effects of training:**

- Heart: enlarged, with a thicker ventricle wall; larger stroke volume; lower resting heart rate (bradycardia).
- Lungs: stronger respiratory muscles; slightly larger vital capacity; more efficient ventilation.
- Blood: more red blood cells and plasma; more capillaries in muscles.
- Muscles: more mitochondria and myoglobin in slow fibres (endurance training); larger fast fibres (strength training); more glycogen stores; more enzymes for aerobic respiration.
- Health: lower blood pressure, better blood lipid profile, better insulin sensitivity.

### Worked example

At rest an athlete's heart rate is 52 beats min⁻¹ and stroke volume 100 cm³. During a race the heart rate is 180 beats min⁻¹ and stroke volume 160 cm³. Calculate the percentage increase in cardiac output.

Rest: 52 × 100 = 5200 cm³ min⁻¹. Race: 180 × 160 = 28 800 cm³ min⁻¹. Increase = (28 800 − 5200) ÷ 5200 × 100 = 454%.

## Aerobic fitness and VO₂ max

*Refs: OCR B 4.1.2(b) to (d), (f) · Edexcel A CP17. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Factors affecting aerobic fitness; the significance of VO₂ max as a measure of aerobic fitness.
- Practical investigations into the effects of exercise.

### Explained

**Aerobic fitness** is the ability to take in, transport and use oxygen. It depends on: lung function; heart size and stroke volume; haemoglobin and red blood cell count; capillary density in muscles; numbers of mitochondria and myoglobin in muscles; proportion of slow-twitch fibres (partly inherited); age (falls with age); sex; training; health (smoking reduces it).

**Training and the F.I.T.T. principles** (OCR B 4.1.2(d)). Aerobic fitness improves with training planned by four factors: **F**requency (how often, such as three to five sessions a week), **I**ntensity (how hard, often judged as a percentage of maximum heart rate, roughly 220 minus age), **T**ime (how long each session lasts) and **T**ype (the kind of exercise, matched to the goal: running or cycling for endurance). Training must be progressive (gradually increased) to keep producing the long-term effects described above; the effects are lost if training stops. Practical investigations measure resting heart rate, breathing rate or recovery time before and after a training programme, or in people with different training histories.

**VO₂ max** is the maximum rate at which a person can take in and use oxygen during intense exercise, measured in cm³ of oxygen per kg of body mass per minute (cm³ kg⁻¹ min⁻¹). Typical values: untrained adults 35 to 45; elite endurance athletes 70 to 85. It is measured on a treadmill or exercise bike with increasing intensity while expired air is analysed, or estimated from tests such as a multi-stage shuttle run. A higher VO₂ max means more work can be done aerobically before lactate builds up.

### Practical

**Edexcel A CP17** (also OCR B 4.1.2(c)): investigate the effects of exercise on tidal volume, breathing rate, minute ventilation and oxygen consumption, using data from spirometer traces.

1. The subject breathes in and out of a spirometer (oxygen-filled chamber, with soda lime to absorb CO₂) at rest for a minute; the trace records volume against time.
2. The subject exercises (for example step-ups for 2 minutes), then breathes into the spirometer again.
3. From the traces: tidal volume = height of one wave; breathing rate = waves per minute; minute ventilation = tidal volume × breathing rate; oxygen consumption = the fall in the overall level of the trace per minute (CO₂ is absorbed, so the volume falls as oxygen is used).

Safety: the subject must be healthy (no asthma or heart problems); disinfect the mouthpiece; use a nose clip; stop if dizzy; soda lime is corrosive; check the spirometer has enough oxygen. Simpler heart rate practicals (OCR B 2.2.1(e)): measure pulse at the wrist for 15 seconds × 4 at rest and after exercise, or with a monitor, and investigate recovery time.

## Exercise, health and medical technology

*Refs: Edexcel A 7.13, 7.14. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Analyse data on the possible disadvantages of exercising too much (wear and tear on joints, suppression of the immune system) and exercising too little (CVD, obesity, type 2 diabetes).
- How medical technology, including keyhole surgery and prostheses, is enabling those with injuries and disabilities to participate in sports.

### Explained

**Too little exercise:** higher risk of obesity, CVD, high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, some cancers, osteoporosis, depression.

**Too much exercise:**

- *Wear and tear on joints:* repeated impact damages cartilage (osteoarthritis); damage to ligaments, tendons and bursae (sacs of fluid that reduce friction) causes inflammation (bursitis).
- *Immune suppression:* very intense or long exercise (marathons) is followed by a period of lower numbers and activity of natural killer cells, phagocytes and lymphocytes, and less IgA in mucus, so athletes are more likely to catch respiratory infections. Moderate exercise improves immunity, giving a J-shaped curve of infection risk against amount of exercise.

**Keyhole surgery (arthroscopy).** Repairs to damaged joints (such as a torn anterior cruciate ligament or cartilage in the knee) through small cuts, using a fibre-optic camera and fine instruments. Compared with open surgery: less tissue damage, less blood loss, less pain, smaller scars, lower risk of infection, shorter hospital stay and faster recovery, so athletes return to sport sooner.

**Prostheses.** Artificial body parts: knee and hip replacements (metal and plastic) relieve pain from damaged joints; prosthetic limbs (carbon-fibre running blades) allow people with amputations to compete. Some include electronics that respond to muscle signals.

## Performance-enhancing substances and ethics

*Refs: Edexcel A 7.15 · OCR B 4.1.2(d), (e). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Performance-enhancing substances and methods, including anabolic steroids, erythropoietin (EPO), creatine, stimulants, blood doping and altitude training.
- Different ethical positions on whether their use is acceptable.

### Explained

| Substance or method | Effect | Risks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anabolic steroids (testosterone-like) | Bind to receptors and act as transcription factors, increasing protein synthesis: bigger, stronger muscles, faster recovery | Liver damage, high blood pressure, heart disease, infertility, mood changes, acne; banned |
| EPO | Increases red blood cell production: more oxygen carried, more endurance | Thicker blood: clots, stroke, heart attack; banned |
| Blood doping | Removing blood, storing it, and reinfusing red cells before competition: more red cells | As EPO; infection; banned |
| Altitude training | Low pO₂ at altitude stimulates natural EPO release and more red blood cells | Legal; altitude sickness; effect wears off |
| Creatine supplements | Increase phosphocreatine stores: more power in short bursts | Legal; water retention, stomach upset; possible kidney strain at high doses |
| Stimulants (caffeine, amphetamines) | Increase alertness, heart rate, reduce fatigue | Some banned; addiction, heart problems |
| Narcotic painkillers | Mask pain, so injured athletes compete | Further injury, addiction; banned in competition |

Testing for steroids and EPO uses urine and blood samples (file 30).

**Ethical positions.**

*Against use:*

- It is unfair (cheating) to athletes who do not use them.
- Health risks; athletes may feel pressured to use them to compete.
- Athletes are role models for young people.
- Rules are agreed by all who take part; breaking them is wrong (a deontological, rule-based view).

*For allowing use:*

- Athletes are adults who can choose for themselves (autonomy), if informed of the risks.
- If everyone could use them, the playing field would be level; legal, monitored use might be safer than secret use.
- Other advantages are allowed (altitude training, expensive equipment), so the line is arbitrary.

A utilitarian view weighs the total benefits (entertainment, records) against total harms (health, fairness).

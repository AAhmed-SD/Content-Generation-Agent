# Temperature control, thyroxine and managing diabetes

Mammals and birds keep their core temperature almost constant; many other animals rely on their behaviour. This file covers ectotherms and endotherms, how the hypothalamus controls body temperature, thyroxine, measuring body temperature, hypothermia and hyperthermia, and how diabetes is diagnosed, treated and managed.

## Ectotherms, endotherms and the control of body temperature

*Refs: OCR A 5.1.1(d) · OCR B 5.3.1(c), (e), (f) · Edexcel A 7.12 · Edexcel B 9.9 vi, vii. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The physiological and behavioural responses involved in temperature control in ectotherms and endotherms.
- How an endotherm produces heat through metabolism and regulates its temperature through the hypothalamus.
- (OCR B) Techniques for measuring body temperature; the causes, symptoms and treatment of hypothermia and hyperthermia.

### Explained

**Ectotherms** (most fish, amphibians, reptiles, invertebrates) get most of their heat from the environment. Their body temperature varies with it. They control it mainly by **behaviour**:

- basking in the sun; lying on warm rocks; pressing against warm ground
- orienting the body to expose more or less surface to the sun
- seeking shade, burrows or water when hot
- becoming inactive when it is cold.

Some have physiological responses too: changing heart rate; darkening skin; locusts ventilating more to lose water vapour.

Ectotherms need less food (less energy goes into heat), but are less active in cold conditions and more at risk from predators then.

**Endotherms** (mammals, birds) produce heat by metabolism and keep their core temperature nearly constant, regardless of the environment. Heat comes mainly from respiration in the liver and muscles. They can be active in cold conditions, but need much more food.

**Control by the hypothalamus.**

1. **Thermoreceptors** in the hypothalamus detect the temperature of the blood (core temperature). **Peripheral thermoreceptors** in the skin detect external temperature and send impulses to the hypothalamus, giving early warning.
2. The hypothalamus has a heat-loss centre and a heat-gain centre. It sends impulses through the autonomic nervous system to effectors.

| | Too hot | Too cold |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Skin arterioles | **Vasodilation**: arterioles near the skin surface widen, more blood flows through skin capillaries, more heat is radiated | **Vasoconstriction**: arterioles narrow, less blood to skin, less heat lost |
| Sweating | Sweat glands secrete sweat; evaporation uses latent heat from skin | Less sweating |
| Hairs | Erector muscles relax; hairs lie flat | Erector muscles contract; hairs stand up, trapping an insulating layer of air (less effective in humans) |
| Muscles | | **Shivering**: rapid involuntary contractions release heat from respiration |
| Metabolism | Lower metabolic rate | Adrenaline and thyroxine raise metabolic rate |
| Behaviour | Seek shade; remove clothes; panting (dogs); licking fur | Seek warmth; huddle; add clothes |

Brown fat (in babies and hibernating mammals) releases heat directly in respiration instead of making ATP.

**Measuring body temperature** (OCR B 5.3.1(e)). Clinical thermometers (digital probes) under the tongue, in the armpit, rectum or ear (infrared tympanic thermometer measures the eardrum, close to the hypothalamus); forehead infrared thermometers; thermal imaging for many people. Core temperature is about 37 °C; the mouth and armpit read slightly lower; the reading varies with time of day and activity. Monitoring is important in illness (fever), surgery and newborns.

**Hypothermia and hyperthermia** (OCR B 5.3.1(f)).

- **Hypothermia:** core temperature below 35 °C. Causes: cold exposure, wet clothing, immersion in cold water; more likely in the elderly and babies. Symptoms: shivering (stops in severe cases), confusion, slurred speech, slow heart rate and breathing, unconsciousness. As temperature falls, enzymes work slowly, metabolism slows, less heat is made: positive feedback. Treatment: remove wet clothing; warm slowly with blankets and warm drinks (if conscious); warmed fluids by drip in hospital; avoid rapid rewarming of the limbs.
- **Hyperthermia** (heat exhaustion and heat stroke): core temperature above about 38 to 40 °C. Causes: hot, humid conditions, heavy exercise, dehydration (no water for sweating), some drugs. Symptoms: headache, dizziness, heavy sweating then hot dry skin, rapid pulse, confusion, seizures. Enzymes may denature; organ damage. Treatment: move to a cool place; cool with water and fans; drink fluids; medical treatment for heat stroke.

### Common mistakes

- Saying blood vessels "move" closer to or further from the skin.
- Saying capillaries dilate. Arterioles do.
- Saying ectotherms are "cold-blooded" and cannot control their temperature at all.

## Thyroxine

*Refs: OCR B 5.3.1(d). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The regulation of thyroxine release and the effect of thyroxine on metabolic rate.

### Explained

Thyroxine is a hormone made by the thyroid gland in the neck, containing iodine. It increases the metabolic rate of most cells (more respiration and heat), and is needed for normal growth and brain development.

**Control by negative feedback.**

1. When metabolic rate or temperature falls, the hypothalamus releases TRH (thyrotropin-releasing hormone).
2. TRH stimulates the anterior pituitary to release TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone).
3. TSH stimulates the thyroid to release thyroxine.
4. Rising thyroxine inhibits the release of TRH and TSH, so thyroxine levels stay in a narrow range.

Cold exposure increases thyroxine release.

**Disorders.** Hypothyroidism (too little thyroxine): tiredness, weight gain, feeling cold; treated with synthetic thyroxine tablets. Hyperthyroidism (too much): weight loss, rapid heart rate, feeling hot, anxiety; treated with drugs, radioactive iodine or surgery. Lack of iodine in the diet causes a goitre (swollen thyroid), because low thyroxine removes the negative feedback and TSH keeps stimulating the gland.

## Diagnosing, treating and managing diabetes

*Refs: OCR A 5.1.4(e), (f) · OCR B 5.3.2(c) to (g). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The differences between type 1 and type 2 diabetes (file 20 covers causes).
- (OCR B) The fasting blood glucose test, glucose tolerance test, HbA1c test and urine tests.
- The treatment and management of type 1 and type 2 diabetes; potential treatments, including insulin from GM bacteria and stem cells.
- (OCR B) The care team for people with diabetes; the future impact of diabetes on human health and the NHS.

### Explained

**Types.** Type 1: autoimmune destruction of β cells; no insulin. Type 2: cells become resistant to insulin and the pancreas may later make less. (OCR B also notes *gestational diabetes*, which develops in pregnancy and usually goes after birth, but raises later risk of type 2.)

**Tests** (OCR B 5.3.2(d)).

- *Fasting blood glucose:* blood glucose after no food for 8 hours or more. Normal below about 6 mmol dm⁻³; 7 or above suggests diabetes.
- *Glucose tolerance test:* after fasting, the person drinks a set amount of glucose, and blood glucose is measured at intervals (usually after 2 hours). In diabetes, glucose rises higher and stays high for longer, because it is not taken up into cells.
- *HbA1c test:* measures the proportion of haemoglobin with glucose attached (glycated). Red blood cells last about 3 months, so it shows the average blood glucose over the last 2 to 3 months. Used to diagnose and to check control.
- *Biosensors:* handheld blood glucose meters use a biosensor: glucose oxidase immobilised on a test strip oxidises glucose in a drop of blood, and the reaction produces a small electric current proportional to the glucose concentration, shown as a reading (file 01). Continuous glucose monitors use a sensor under the skin.
- *Urine test:* dipsticks with glucose oxidase show glucose in urine (glucose exceeds the kidney's reabsorption capacity). Ketones in urine show the body is breaking down fat because glucose cannot enter cells (dangerous in type 1).

**Treatment.**

- *Type 1:* insulin injections several times a day (or an insulin pump), with doses matched to food intake and exercise; regular blood glucose monitoring (finger-prick testing or continuous glucose monitors). Too much insulin causes hypoglycaemia (low glucose: shaking, confusion, unconsciousness; treated with sugar or a glucagon injection). Pancreas or islet cell transplants are possible in some cases.
- *Type 2:* lose weight; eat less refined sugar and more complex carbohydrates and fibre; exercise regularly (increases insulin sensitivity). Drugs such as metformin (reduces glucose release by the liver and increases sensitivity); later, insulin if needed.

**Sources of insulin** (OCR A 5.1.4(f)). Insulin was once extracted from the pancreases of pigs and cattle, which risked allergic reactions and ethical or religious objections, and supply was limited. Now human insulin is made by GM bacteria or yeast (file 25): identical to human insulin, cheap, made in large amounts, fewer side effects, and acceptable to more people. Modified insulins act faster or for longer.

**Future treatments.** Stem cells grown into β cells and implanted (possibly in a protective capsule to avoid immune attack); an artificial pancreas (a glucose monitor linked to an insulin pump by software); gene therapy.

**The care team** (OCR B 5.3.2(f)). A GP, diabetes specialist nurse, consultant (diabetologist), dietitian, podiatrist (foot care: diabetes damages nerves and blood vessels in the feet), optometrist (eye screening for diabetic retinopathy), pharmacist and psychologist. Regular checks for complications: eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart.

**Future impact** (OCR B 5.3.2(g)). The number of people with type 2 diabetes is rising fast, linked to obesity and inactive lifestyles. Effects: more complications (heart disease, stroke, blindness, kidney failure, amputations); a large and growing share of NHS spending; lost working days. Prevention (healthy diet, exercise, weight loss, sugar taxes, NHS prevention programmes) is cheaper than treatment.

### Worked example

In a glucose tolerance test, person A's blood glucose was 5.0 mmol dm⁻³ fasting and 6.5 at 2 hours; person B's was 7.4 fasting and 13.2 at 2 hours. Interpret.

A is normal: glucose rose slightly and fell back as insulin caused uptake into cells. B has a fasting level above 7 and a 2-hour level above 11.1, which suggests diabetes: glucose is not taken up from the blood efficiently.

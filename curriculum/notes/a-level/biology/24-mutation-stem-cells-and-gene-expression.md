# Gene mutations, stem cells and the control of gene expression

Every cell has the same genes, but each uses only some of them. This file covers the types and effects of gene mutation, stem cells, how transcription and translation are controlled (transcription factors, epigenetics, RNA interference, the lac operon, homeobox genes), and how faulty control leads to cancer.

## Overview

A-level only for AQA. Basic mutations are introduced in file 13; chromosome disorders in file 39; cancer screening and treatment in file 44.

## 3.8.1 Alteration of the sequence of bases in DNA can alter the structure of proteins

*Refs: AQA 3.8.1 · Edexcel A 2.12 · Edexcel B 1.4 viii, ix, 8.1 i · OCR A 6.1.1(a) · OCR B 5.1.1(b) · Eduqas C2.5(f), (g). Level: A-level only (AS for Edexcel A and B).*

### What you need to know

- Gene mutations might arise during DNA replication. They include addition, deletion, substitution, inversion, duplication and translocation of bases.
- Gene mutations occur spontaneously. The mutation rate is increased by mutagenic agents. Some mutations result in a different amino acid sequence in the encoded polypeptide. Due to the degenerate nature of the genetic code, not all base substitutions cause a change in the sequence of encoded amino acids. Some gene mutations change only one triplet code; others result in a frame shift.
- Relate the nature of a gene mutation to its effect on the encoded polypeptide.

### Explained

| Mutation | What happens | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Substitution | One base replaced by another | One triplet changes; may be silent (same amino acid), missense (one different amino acid) or nonsense (stop codon, short polypeptide) |
| Addition (insertion) | Extra base added | Frame shift: every triplet after it changes |
| Deletion | Base lost | Frame shift |
| Duplication | One or more bases repeated | Frame shift (unless a whole number of triplets) |
| Inversion | A group of bases reversed | Changes the triplets within that section |
| Translocation of bases | A group of bases moves from one location to another (same or different chromosome) | Can disrupt genes at both locations |

Adding or deleting three bases removes or adds one amino acid without a frame shift.

**Effect on the protein.** A different primary structure may change the bonds that form between R groups, so the tertiary structure changes. An enzyme's active site may no longer be complementary to its substrate. Some mutations are harmful, most are neutral, and a few are beneficial.

**Sickle cell anaemia** (Edexcel A, Edexcel B, Eduqas). A substitution in the gene for the β chain of haemoglobin changes one base on the coding strand from A to T, so the triplet GAG (glutamic acid) becomes GTG (valine); on the mRNA, GAG becomes GUG. Valine is non-polar, so haemoglobin molecules stick together at low oxygen, forming long fibres. Red blood cells become sickle-shaped, carry less oxygen and block capillaries. Heterozygotes are protected against malaria, so the allele is common where malaria is.

**Mutagens.** Ionising radiation (breaks DNA strands); UV light (makes adjacent thymines bond together); chemicals such as benzene, mustard gas, nitrogen oxides and tar in tobacco smoke (alter bases or insert between them); some viruses insert DNA into the genome. A carcinogen is a mutagen that causes cancer (Eduqas C2.5(g)).

### Common mistakes

- Saying all mutations are harmful.
- Saying a substitution "always changes the protein".
- Saying a frame shift changes "one amino acid".

## 3.8.2.1 Most of a cell's DNA is not translated (stem cells)

*Refs: AQA 3.8.2.1 · Edexcel A 3.11, 3.12 · Edexcel B 7.3 i to v · OCR A 2.1.6(j) to (m) · OCR B 3.1.1(e) · Eduqas C2.7(i). Level: A-level only (AS for Edexcel A, OCR A, OCR B).*

### What you need to know

- Totipotent cells are cells that can mature into any body cell. During development, totipotent cells translate only part of their DNA, resulting in cell specialisation.
- In mature mammals, only a few totipotent cells, called stem cells, remain. Stem cells are undifferentiated cells that can divide indefinitely and can become specialised.
- Totipotent cells occur only for a limited time in early mammalian embryos. Pluripotent cells are found in embryos; multipotent and unipotent cells are found in mature mammals.
- Unipotent cells, exemplified by the formation of cardiomyocytes.
- Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPS cells) can be produced from adult somatic cells using appropriate protein transcription factors.
- Stem cells can be used in the treatment of human disorders.
- Evaluate the use of stem cells in treating human disorders.

### Explained

| Type | Can become | Found in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Totipotent | Any cell, including placenta | Zygote and the first few divisions (morula) |
| Pluripotent | Almost any cell type, but not placenta | Inner cell mass of the blastocyst (embryonic stem cells) |
| Multipotent | A few related types | Adult tissues: bone marrow (all blood cells) |
| Unipotent | One type | Adult tissues: cells in the heart that form cardiomyocytes (heart muscle cells) |

**Specialisation.** All cells have the same genes. As cells differentiate, some genes are switched on and others off, so only some proteins are made (differential gene expression, Edexcel A 3.12). Once specialised, most mammalian cells cannot go back.

**Examples of differentiation** (OCR A). Multipotent stem cells in bone marrow produce erythrocytes (lose the nucleus and most organelles, fill with haemoglobin; live about 120 days) and neutrophils (lobed nucleus, many lysosomes; live about 6 hours). In plants, meristem cells in the vascular cambium produce xylem vessels (lignin laid down; cells die; end walls break down) and phloem sieve tubes (lose nucleus and most organelles; sieve plates form; companion cells keep them alive).

**Induced pluripotent stem cells.** Adult cells (such as skin fibroblasts) are made pluripotent by switching on key genes using specific transcription factors (introduced with viral vectors). iPS cells behave like embryonic stem cells.

**Uses.** Bone marrow transplants treat leukaemia and sickle cell disease. Research uses: growing tissues to test drugs; studying development. Possible future uses: type 1 diabetes (new β cells), spinal cord injury, Parkinson's disease, heart damage, macular degeneration. Plant stem cells (meristems) are used in tissue culture (file 29).

**Evaluating.**

| | Benefits | Problems |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Embryonic stem cells | Pluripotent; divide indefinitely | Embryo destroyed (ethical objection: the embryo may be considered a potential person); risk of rejection; risk of tumours |
| Adult stem cells | No embryo destroyed; patient's own cells, so no rejection | Multipotent only; few in number; harder to extract |
| iPS cells | Patient's own cells (no rejection); no embryo destroyed; pluripotent | Viral vectors could cause mutations or cancer; not yet proven safe; may keep some "memory" of their original cell type |

Regulation in the UK: research on embryos is licensed by the HFEA and allowed only up to 14 days.

### Common mistakes

- Saying stem cells are "only in embryos".
- Saying differentiated cells lose genes. They keep all genes but express only some.

## 3.8.2.2 Regulation of transcription and translation

*Refs: AQA 3.8.2.2 · Edexcel A 3.14, 6.10, 7.16 · Edexcel B 7.2 i to v, 7.3 iii · OCR A 6.1.1(b) to (d) · OCR B 3.1.1(c), (d), 5.1.2(f), 5.1.3(a), (h) · Eduqas C2.5(h). Level: A-level only (AS for Edexcel A 3.14, OCR B 3.1.1).*

### What you need to know

- In eukaryotes, transcription of target genes can be stimulated or inhibited when specific transcriptional factors move from the cytoplasm into the nucleus.
- The role of the steroid hormone, oestrogen, in initiating transcription.
- Epigenetic control of gene expression in eukaryotes: environmental factors can alter the structure of DNA and associated proteins without changing the base sequence; increased methylation of the DNA or decreased acetylation of associated histones inhibits transcription.
- The relevance of epigenetics on the development and treatment of disease, especially cancer.
- In eukaryotes and some prokaryotes, translation of the mRNA produced from target genes can be inhibited by RNA interference (RNAi).
- Interpret data from investigations into gene expression; evaluate appropriate data for the relative influences of genetic and environmental factors on phenotype.

### Explained

**Transcription factors** are proteins that bind to the promoter region of DNA (near the start of a gene) and help or stop RNA polymerase binding.

- *Activators* help RNA polymerase bind, so transcription starts.
- *Repressors* block RNA polymerase, so the gene is not transcribed.

**Oestrogen.**

1. Oestrogen is a lipid-soluble steroid, so it diffuses through the cell-surface membrane.
2. In the cytoplasm it binds to a receptor site on a transcription factor (the oestrogen receptor), changing its shape.
3. The shape change releases the transcription factor from an inhibitor molecule, so it can enter the nucleus and bind to DNA.
4. It stimulates transcription of target genes.

(Edexcel A 7.16) Other hormones work the same way, and transcription factors respond to many signals.

**Epigenetics.** Heritable changes in gene expression without any change in the DNA base sequence. The "epigenome" is the set of chemical tags on DNA and histones.

- *DNA methylation:* methyl groups attach to cytosine bases (at CpG sites) in or near the promoter. Methylation stops transcription factors binding, and attracts proteins that condense the chromatin. Gene switched off.
- *Histone acetylation:* adding acetyl groups to histones makes them bind DNA less tightly, so the chromatin is loose and open, and transcription factors can reach the DNA. Decreased acetylation (deacetylation) makes chromatin condense; genes are switched off.

Environmental factors (diet, stress, smoking, toxins, maternal diet during pregnancy) can change these tags. Some changes pass to offspring. Epigenetic changes help keep differentiated cells specialised (Edexcel B 7.2 v), and explain why identical twins become more different as they age.

**Epigenetics and disease.** Cancers often show hypermethylation of tumour suppressor genes (switching them off) and hypomethylation of oncogenes. Treatments include drugs that inhibit DNA methyltransferase (removing methylation) or histone deacetylase inhibitors (keeping chromatin open), reactivating tumour suppressor genes. These drugs must be targeted, because they affect all cells.

**RNA interference (RNAi).** Small double-stranded RNA molecules (siRNA, or microRNA in some cases) are made or introduced. One strand joins a protein complex (RISC). The complex binds to mRNA with a complementary base sequence and the mRNA is cut up, so it is not translated. Uses: research (switching off genes to find out what they do), possible treatments for viral infection and genetic disease.

**Post-transcriptional modification** (Edexcel A 6.10, Edexcel B 7.2 iii, OCR B 5.1.3(a)). Pre-mRNA is spliced, removing introns. In *alternative splicing*, different combinations of exons are kept, so one gene gives several different mRNAs and proteins. OCR B also includes RNA editing (changing particular bases in mRNA).

**The lac operon** (OCR A 6.1.1(b)). In *E. coli*, three structural genes for lactose use (*lacZ* for β-galactosidase, *lacY* for permease, *lacA*) share one promoter and an operator, controlled by a regulatory gene that codes for a repressor protein.

- *No lactose:* the repressor binds to the operator, blocking RNA polymerase. No enzymes are made (saves energy).
- *Lactose present:* lactose binds to the repressor, changing its shape so it falls off the operator. RNA polymerase binds to the promoter and transcribes the genes; the enzymes are made.
- Glucose is the preferred respiratory substrate: when glucose is present, levels of cAMP are low, and transcription stays low even with lactose (cAMP–CRP needed for full transcription).

**Other control** (OCR A). After translation, proteins can be activated by cAMP (as in the second messenger model, file 20), by adding a phosphate group, or by cutting an inactive precursor.

**Homeobox genes** (OCR A 6.1.1(c)). Genes that control the development of the body plan. Each contains a 180-base-pair sequence (the homeobox) that codes for a homeodomain, a part of a transcription factor that binds DNA. Hox genes (a group of homeobox genes) are arranged on the chromosome in the same order as the body regions they control, and are very similar in animals as different as fruit flies and humans, showing they are highly conserved.

**Apoptosis** (OCR A 6.1.1(d), OCR B 3.1.1(c), (d)). Programmed cell death:

1. Enzymes break down the cytoskeleton.
2. The cytoplasm becomes dense; the cell shrinks; the membrane forms bulges (blebs).
3. Chromatin condenses; DNA breaks into fragments; the nucleus breaks up.
4. The cell breaks into membrane-bound vesicles, which phagocytes engulf. No inflammation, because no hydrolytic enzymes leak out.

Apoptosis removes cells that are damaged, unnecessary or harmful. In development it shapes body parts (separating fingers and toes) and removes excess neurones. Too little apoptosis can lead to cancer; too much to degenerative disease. Mitosis and apoptosis together build and shape the body.

**Genetic vs environmental influences.** Twin studies compare identical (monozygotic) and non-identical twins. If identical twins are much more alike for a trait than non-identical twins, genes have a strong influence. Twins raised apart show environmental effects (file 34 for Edexcel A 8.19).

### Common mistakes

- Saying oestrogen binds to a receptor on the cell-surface membrane.
- Saying methylation changes the base sequence.
- Saying acetylation switches genes off. Acetylation opens chromatin; decreased acetylation switches genes off.
- Saying siRNA "destroys DNA".
- In the lac operon, saying lactose binds to the operator.

## 3.8.2.3 Gene expression and cancer

*Refs: AQA 3.8.2.3 · OCR B 3.3.1(b) to (d) · Eduqas C2.5(g). Level: A-level only (AS for OCR B).*

### What you need to know

- The main characteristics of benign and malignant tumours.
- The role of the following in the development of tumours: tumour suppressor genes and oncogenes; abnormal methylation of tumour suppressor genes and oncogenes; increased oestrogen concentrations in the development of some breast cancers.
- Evaluate evidence showing correlations between genetic and environmental factors and various forms of cancer. Interpret information relating to the way in which an understanding of the roles of oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes could be used in the prevention, treatment and cure of cancer.

### Explained

**Tumours.**

| Benign | Malignant |
| --- | --- |
| Grow slowly | Grow rapidly |
| Cells are well differentiated, with a normal nucleus | Cells become unspecialised; large, darker nucleus |
| Enclosed in a capsule; stay in one place | No capsule; invade nearby tissues |
| Do not spread | Spread through blood and lymph to form secondary tumours (metastasis) |
| Usually easier to remove; rarely recur | Need surgery, chemotherapy and radiotherapy; often recur |

**Proto-oncogenes and oncogenes.** Proto-oncogenes code for proteins that stimulate cell division (growth factor receptors, for example). A mutation can make a proto-oncogene into an oncogene that is permanently active: the receptor is switched on without a growth factor, or too much growth factor is made. The cell divides without control. One mutated copy is enough (dominant effect).

**Tumour suppressor genes** code for proteins that slow cell division, repair DNA, or trigger apoptosis (such as the p53 protein, and the BRCA1 gene product). A mutation that inactivates them removes the brake. Usually both copies must be inactivated.

**Abnormal methylation.** Hypermethylation of a tumour suppressor gene switches it off; hypomethylation of a proto-oncogene switches it on. Both lead to uncontrolled division.

**Oestrogen and breast cancer.** After the menopause, fat tissue in the breast can make oestrogen. Raised oestrogen concentrations activate transcription factors that switch on genes for cell division. If a tumour has formed, oestrogen helps it grow. Drugs such as tamoxifen bind to oestrogen receptors in breast cells and block them.

**Using this knowledge.**

- Screening: testing for mutations in BRCA1 and BRCA2 identifies people at high risk, who may choose more frequent screening or preventive surgery.
- Treatment: drugs targeted at an oncogene's protein product (monoclonal antibodies blocking an overactive receptor, such as HER2 in some breast cancers); demethylating drugs (above).
- Prevention: avoiding mutagens (smoking, UV).
- Evaluating data: correlations between, say, diet and a cancer may be due to other factors; large, controlled studies are needed.

### Common mistakes

- Saying oncogenes are always present and "cause cancer". Proto-oncogenes are normal genes.
- Saying a tumour suppressor gene mutation "speeds up division". It removes the control that slows division.
- Saying benign tumours are always harmless (they can press on organs).

# Cloning and biotechnology

Clones are genetically identical organisms or cells. Biotechnology uses living organisms or their enzymes to make products. This file covers natural and artificial cloning of plants and animals, microorganisms in industry and food, batch and continuous culture, and immobilised enzymes.

## Natural and artificial cloning

*Refs: OCR A 6.2.1(a) to (d) · Edexcel B 7.3 iii. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Natural clones in plants and their use in horticulture; taking cuttings.
- Artificial clones in plants by micropropagation and tissue culture; the arguments for and against.
- Natural clones in animals.
- Artificial clones in animals by embryo twinning and somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT); the arguments for and against.

### Explained

**Natural cloning in plants (vegetative propagation).** Many plants reproduce asexually: runners (strawberries), bulbs (onions, daffodils), rhizomes (couch grass), stem tubers (potatoes), suckers (elm trees regrow from roots after the trunk dies). Meristem cells, which are totipotent, form the new plant.

*Cuttings* (horticulture): cut a short length of stem with a few leaves, just below a node, at a slant; dip in rooting powder (auxins, file 31); plant in compost; keep humid (cover with a plastic bag) to reduce water loss until roots form.

**Micropropagation (tissue culture).**

1. Take a small piece of tissue (explant), often from a meristem (shoot tip), which is free of viruses.
2. Sterilise its surface (dilute bleach or alcohol) to kill microorganisms.
3. Place it on a sterile nutrient agar medium containing sugars, minerals and plant hormones (a high ratio of auxin to cytokinin). Cells divide to form a mass of undifferentiated cells called a callus.
4. Divide the callus into pieces; transfer them to media with different hormone ratios to make shoots and then roots form.
5. Move plantlets to a greenhouse to harden off, then plant out.

(Edexcel B 7.3 iii) This works because many plant cells stay totipotent throughout life. Epigenetic marks on DNA are removed as the callus forms, letting cells express all their genes again.

| For | Against |
| --- | --- |
| Very many plants quickly from one plant | Monoculture: all plants genetically identical, so all susceptible to the same disease or change |
| All plants have the desired characteristics | Expensive; needs skilled staff and sterile conditions |
| Disease-free plants from meristems | Contamination can lose a whole batch |
| Plants that are hard to grow from seed (orchids) or seedless (bananas) | Mutations in the callus may give abnormal plants |
| Year-round production | |

**Natural clones in animals.** Identical (monozygotic) twins form when an early embryo splits in two. Some invertebrates reproduce asexually: hydra by budding; starfish regenerating from a fragment; parthenogenesis (development of an unfertilised egg) in aphids and some reptiles.

**Artificial embryo twinning.**

1. Fertilise an egg from a top-quality female with sperm from a top-quality male (in vitro).
2. Grow the zygote to an early embryo of a few cells.
3. Split the embryo into several pieces (each cell is totipotent); grow each into a new embryo.
4. Implant each into a surrogate mother.

The offspring are clones of each other, but not of either parent.

**Somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT).**

1. Take a somatic (body) cell from the adult to be cloned, such as an udder cell.
2. Take an egg cell from another female and remove its nucleus (enucleation).
3. Fuse the somatic cell with the enucleated egg using an electric shock, which also starts cell division.
4. Grow the embryo, then implant it into a surrogate mother.

The offspring is a clone of the animal that gave the somatic cell (apart from its mitochondrial DNA, which comes from the egg). Dolly the sheep (1996) was the first mammal cloned from an adult cell.

| For | Against |
| --- | --- |
| Copies animals with valuable traits (high milk yield; producing a medicine in milk) | Low success rate; many embryos fail; expensive |
| Conserving rare or endangered species | Clones may have health problems and shorter lives |
| Therapeutic cloning: making embryonic stem cells matching a patient | Reduced genetic diversity in farmed animals |
| Testing (genetically identical animals reduce variation) | Ethical concerns about animal welfare and about cloning humans |

### Common mistakes

- Saying SCNT offspring are clones of the egg donor.
- Saying embryo twinning produces clones of the parents.
- Saying micropropagation uses gametes.

## Microorganisms in biotechnology

*Refs: OCR A 6.2.1(e) to (g). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The use of microorganisms in biotechnological processes, such as brewing, baking, cheese making, yoghurt production, penicillin production, insulin production and bioremediation.
- The advantages and disadvantages of using microorganisms to produce food for human consumption.
- The importance of manipulating the growing conditions in batch and continuous fermentation to maximise yield.

### Explained

**Why microorganisms are used.** They reproduce quickly; can be grown on cheap or waste materials; need low temperatures (often below 40 °C), saving energy; make pure products; can be genetically modified; are not affected by climate or season; raise fewer ethical issues than animals.

**Examples.**

| Process | Organism | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Baking | Yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) | Respires sugars; CO₂ makes dough rise |
| Brewing | Yeast | Anaerobic respiration of sugars from malted barley makes ethanol |
| Cheese making | Bacteria (and rennet enzyme, now often from GM microorganisms) | Bacteria make lactic acid from lactose; milk proteins clot |
| Yoghurt | Bacteria (*Lactobacillus*) | Lactic acid makes milk proteins thicken; flavour |
| Penicillin | Fungus (*Penicillium*) | Secondary metabolite made in the stationary phase |
| Insulin | GM bacteria or yeast | Human insulin gene inserted (file 25) |
| Bioremediation | Bacteria | Break down pollutants such as oil spills or sewage into harmless products |
| Single-cell protein | Fungus (*Fusarium*, as mycoprotein) | Grown in fermenters as a meat substitute |

**Food from microorganisms: pros and cons.**

- Advantages: fast production; little land; can use waste; high protein, low fat; production controlled independent of weather; no animal welfare issues.
- Disadvantages: contamination risk (pathogens) means sterile conditions; product must be purified (removing toxins, nucleic acids); may not taste good, needs flavouring; people may not want to eat food grown from microorganisms.

**Batch and continuous culture.**

| | Batch | Continuous |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How | Fixed volume of medium; organisms grow through lag, log and stationary phases; product removed at the end | Nutrients added and culture removed continuously; kept in log phase |
| Products | Secondary metabolites (penicillin, made in stationary phase) | Primary metabolites (made during growth: enzymes, insulin, mycoprotein) |
| Advantages | Easy to set up; contamination only loses one batch | Higher productivity; smaller vessel |
| Disadvantages | Time lost cleaning and refilling | Hard to control; contamination loses a lot |

**Maximising yield in a fermenter.**

- Temperature: kept at the optimum with a water jacket; respiration releases heat, so cooling is needed.
- pH: monitored by a probe; acid or alkali added.
- Oxygen: sterile air bubbled in for aerobic organisms.
- Mixing: paddles keep nutrients, oxygen and temperature even.
- Nutrients: sterile medium; the right concentrations.
- Asepsis: everything sterilised with steam before use, so no competition from other microbes and no contamination of the product.

### Common mistakes

- Saying penicillin is made in the log phase.
- Saying fermenters need heating (they usually need cooling).

## Immobilised enzymes

*Refs: OCR A 6.2.1(i) · Eduqas CC4(i). Level: A-level only (OCR A); AS & A (Eduqas).*

### What you need to know

- The uses of immobilised enzymes in biotechnology and the different methods of immobilisation.
- Industrial processes use immobilised enzymes that are fixed, so they can be reused (Eduqas).

### Explained

**Methods of immobilisation.**

- *Adsorption:* enzymes stick to a surface (clay, glass beads, resin). Simple; enzymes may leak off.
- *Covalent bonding:* enzymes bonded to an insoluble support. Little leakage, but may change the active site.
- *Entrapment:* enzymes trapped in a gel bead (calcium alginate) or a matrix. Active site unaffected, but substrate must diffuse in, so the reaction is slower.
- *Membrane separation:* enzymes separated from the reaction mixture by a partially permeable membrane.

**Advantages.** The product is not contaminated with enzyme (no separation needed); enzymes can be reused, saving cost; enzymes are more stable, working over wider ranges of temperature and pH, because the matrix protects them; continuous production is possible.

**Disadvantages.** Setting up costs more; the rate may be lower (substrate has to reach the enzyme); some activity may be lost when immobilising.

**Uses.**

- Lactase: lactose-free milk (lactose → glucose + galactose).
- Glucose isomerase: glucose → fructose (sweeter, used in food).
- Penicillin acylase: making semi-synthetic penicillins that bacteria are not yet resistant to.
- Aminoacylase: separating amino acid isomers for drugs.
- Glucoamylase: starch → glucose.
- Nitrile hydratase: making acrylamide for plastics.
- Biosensors: glucose oxidase in blood glucose meters (file 01).

### Worked example

A column of immobilised lactase converts 85% of lactose in milk at a flow rate of 10 cm³ min⁻¹, and 60% at 25 cm³ min⁻¹. Explain the difference.

At a faster flow rate the milk spends less time in contact with the enzyme, so fewer substrate molecules collide with active sites before leaving the column; a smaller proportion is hydrolysed. (But the total mass converted per minute is higher: 0.60 × 25 = 15 against 0.85 × 10 = 8.5 units.)

## Mark scheme

1. Cut stem below a node / with leaves (1); dip in rooting powder / hormone (1); plant in compost and keep humid / cover (1).
2. Explant from meristem, sterilised (1); on agar with nutrients and hormones (1); callus forms and is divided (1); different hormone ratios produce shoots and roots (1); disadvantage (1): monoculture / genetically identical, all susceptible to disease; expensive; contamination; mutations.
3. Mitochondrial DNA comes from the egg donor (1); environment / epigenetics also affect phenotype (1).
4. Twinning splits an embryo from fertilisation; clones of each other, not of parents (1); SCNT transfers a body cell nucleus into an enucleated egg; clone of the adult (1).
5. Penicillin is a secondary metabolite (1); made in the stationary phase, which continuous culture avoids (1).
6. Oxygen needed for aerobic respiration / growth (1); respiration releases heat (1); cooling keeps temperature at the optimum so enzymes are not denatured (1).
7. Any three: enzyme reused; product not contaminated with enzyme; more stable / wider temperature or pH range; continuous process.
8. Any two: fast growth; use waste / cheap substrates; little land; not affected by climate; high protein, low fat; controlled conditions.

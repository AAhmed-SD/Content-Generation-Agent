# The cell cycle, mitosis, binary fission and cancer

All cells arise from other cells. This file covers the eukaryotic cell cycle, the stages of mitosis, how prokaryotes and viruses replicate, uncontrolled division in cancer, and AQA required practical 2.

## 3.2.2 All cells arise from other cells

*Refs: AQA 3.2.2, RP2 · Edexcel A 3.10, CP5 · Edexcel B 2.3 i to iii, CP3 · OCR A 2.1.6(a) to (e) · OCR B 3.1.1(a), (b) · Eduqas C2.2(a) to (c). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- In multicellular organisms not all cells keep the ability to divide.
- Eukaryotic cells that can divide go through a cell cycle: interphase (DNA replication) followed by nuclear division (mitosis) and then division of the cytoplasm (cytokinesis).
- Mitosis produces two genetically identical daughter cells. Its stages: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase.
- Mitosis is controlled by genes. Uncontrolled division can lead to tumours and cancers; many cancer treatments target the cell cycle.
- Binary fission in prokaryotes: replication of the circular DNA and plasmids, then division of the cytoplasm to give two daughter cells, each with one copy of the circular DNA and a variable number of plasmids.
- Viruses do not undergo cell division. After injecting their nucleic acid, the host cell's machinery replicates the virus.
- Recognise the stages of the cell cycle and explain the appearance of cells in each stage of mitosis.
- Calculate the mitotic index.

### Explained

**Interphase** is most of the cycle. It has three parts:

- G1: the cell grows, makes organelles and proteins.
- S: DNA replicates. Each chromosome is now two identical sister chromatids joined at a centromere.
- G2: the cell grows more, makes proteins for division, and checks the copied DNA.

Some cells leave the cycle and enter G0, where they stop dividing (most neurones, for example).

**Mitosis.**

| Stage | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Prophase | Chromosomes condense and become visible; the nuclear envelope breaks down; centrioles (in animal cells) move to opposite poles and spindle fibres form |
| Metaphase | Chromosomes line up on the equator of the cell; spindle fibres attach to each centromere |
| Anaphase | Centromeres divide; spindle fibres shorten and pull sister chromatids to opposite poles, centromere first (V shapes) |
| Telophase | Chromatids (now chromosomes) reach the poles and uncoil; a nuclear envelope forms around each set |

Cytokinesis then splits the cytoplasm: a cleavage furrow in animal cells; a cell plate forming a new wall in plant cells.

**Why mitosis matters.** It gives genetically identical cells for growth, repair of damaged tissue, replacing worn-out cells, and asexual reproduction (in yeast, many plants, and some animals).

**Regulation and checkpoints** (OCR A). Proteins check the cell before it moves on. The G1 checkpoint checks cell size, nutrients, growth factors and DNA damage. The G2 checkpoint checks that DNA has replicated fully and without damage. The metaphase (spindle assembly) checkpoint checks every chromosome is attached to the spindle before anaphase. If damage cannot be repaired, the cell may enter G0 or undergo apoptosis.

**Cancer.** Cancer starts when mutations in the genes that control the cell cycle let a cell divide without control. Its descendants form a mass called a tumour. Benign tumours stay in one place; malignant tumours invade and spread (metastasis). Many treatments target dividing cells:

- some drugs stop DNA replication in S phase
- some stop spindle formation, so cells stall in metaphase
- radiation damages DNA so cells fail the checkpoints.

Normal cells that divide often (hair follicles, gut lining, bone marrow) are also affected, which is why chemotherapy has side effects.

**Binary fission.** The circular DNA replicates; the two copies move to opposite ends; plasmids replicate; the cytoplasm divides and a new wall forms between the two cells. There is no spindle and no mitosis.

**Viral replication.** A virus's attachment proteins bind to receptors on a host cell; the nucleic acid enters; the host's ribosomes, enzymes and nucleotides make new viral nucleic acid and proteins; new particles assemble and are released, often bursting the cell (lysis).

**Mitotic index** = number of cells with visible chromosomes (in mitosis) ÷ total number of cells counted. A high index means rapid division, as in tumours or root tips.

### Worked example

1. In a field of view of a root tip, 36 cells are in mitosis and 164 are in interphase. Calculate the mitotic index.

Total = 36 + 164 = 200. Mitotic index = 36 ÷ 200 = 0.18 (18%).

2. A cell cycle lasts 20 hours. In a sample of 400 cells, 30 are in metaphase. Estimate how long metaphase lasts.

Fraction in metaphase = 30 ÷ 400 = 0.075. Time = 0.075 × 20 h = 1.5 h = 90 minutes. This assumes cells are spread evenly through the cycle, so the fraction in a stage is proportional to its duration.

### Common mistakes

- Saying DNA replicates during prophase. It replicates in S phase of interphase.
- Calling interphase a "resting" stage. The cell is very active.
- Saying chromosomes, rather than sister chromatids, separate in anaphase.
- Saying "cancer is caused by mitosis". It is caused by mutations that make mitosis uncontrolled.
- Saying viruses divide by binary fission.

### Practical

**AQA required practical 2** (also Edexcel A CP5, Edexcel B CP3, OCR A 2.1.6(d) and PAG1, OCR B 3.1.1(b)): preparation of stained squashes of cells from plant root tips; set-up and use of an optical microscope to identify the stages of mitosis; measurement of the mitotic index.

1. Cut the last 5 mm of a garlic or onion root tip (the meristem, where cells divide).
2. Put it in warm hydrochloric acid (about 1 mol dm⁻³ at 60 °C) for a few minutes. This breaks down the middle lamella so cells separate, and stops division.
3. Rinse in water, then place on a slide and add a stain that binds DNA, such as toluidine blue or acetic orcein.
4. Break up the tip with a mounted needle, add a coverslip, and press down firmly through filter paper (squash) to get a single layer of cells; do not twist the coverslip.
5. Find cells under low power, then switch to high power. Count cells in each stage and calculate the mitotic index.

Points examiners ask about: why the tip is used (only part where cells divide); why squash (single layer so light passes through and cells are seen separately); why stain (chromosomes otherwise transparent); safety (hydrochloric acid is corrosive: eye protection; stain is an irritant). To make counts reliable, count several fields of view chosen at random, and decide in advance how to count cells on the edge of the field.

## Check questions

1. Describe what happens to the chromosomes during prophase and metaphase. (3 marks)
2. Explain why the DNA content of a cell doubles during interphase. (2 marks)
3. A drug stops spindle fibres forming. Explain why it can be used to treat cancer and why it causes hair loss. (3 marks)
4. Describe binary fission. (3 marks)
5. Explain why viruses are not described as dividing. (2 marks)
6. In a root tip squash 45 of 300 cells were in mitosis. Calculate the mitotic index. (1 mark)
7. Explain why the root tip is heated in hydrochloric acid in a root tip squash. (2 marks)
8. A student counted cells in only one field of view. Suggest why their mitotic index might not represent the whole root tip. (2 marks)

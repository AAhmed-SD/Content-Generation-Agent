# Microbiology: classifying, culturing and counting bacteria

Microbiologists grow bacteria in the laboratory to identify them, test antibiotics and make products. This file covers bacterial shapes and Gram staining, aseptic technique, growth media, measuring population growth, the bacterial growth curve, and the related practicals.

## Overview

Prokaryotic cell structure is in file 04. Antibiotics and resistance are in file 27. AQA required practical 6 (antimicrobial substances) is in file 13; the culturing skills here apply to it. Industrial culture (batch and continuous fermentation) is in file 29.

## Classifying bacteria

*Refs: Edexcel B 2.1 iv · OCR B 3.2.1(d) · Eduqas C1.4(a). Level: AS & A (Edexcel B, OCR B); A-level only (Eduqas).*

### What you need to know

- Bacteria are classified by shape and by their reaction to Gram stain.
- The difference between Gram-positive and Gram-negative cell walls, and why it matters for antibiotic treatment.

### Explained

**Shapes.**

| Shape | Name | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spherical | Coccus (cocci); in chains (strepto-), clusters (staphylo-) | *Staphylococcus aureus* |
| Rod | Bacillus (bacilli) | *E. coli*, *Bacillus subtilis* |
| Spiral | Spirillum (spirilla) | *Spirillum* |
| Comma-shaped | Vibrio | *Vibrio cholerae* |

**Gram staining.**

1. Make a smear of bacteria on a slide and heat-fix it.
2. Stain with crystal violet; then add iodine (fixes the stain).
3. Wash with alcohol (decolouriser).
4. Counter-stain with safranin (red).

| | Gram-positive | Gram-negative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Colour seen | Purple / violet | Red / pink |
| Cell wall | Thick peptidoglycan (murein) layer, which keeps the crystal violet | Thin peptidoglycan layer between two membranes; outer membrane of lipopolysaccharide; alcohol washes out the crystal violet |
| Antibiotics | Sensitive to penicillin and lysozyme (wall exposed) | Outer membrane protects the wall from penicillin and lysozyme; need other antibiotics |

Colony morphology (OCR B): the size, shape, colour, edge and surface texture of colonies on agar also help identify bacteria.

### Common mistakes

- Saying Gram-negative bacteria have a thicker wall.
- Saying the "Gram-positive" colour is red.

## Culturing and aseptic technique

*Refs: Edexcel A 4.14, CP9, CP15 · Edexcel B 6.1 i to iii, CP13 · OCR A 6.2.1(g) · OCR B 3.2.1(d), 3.2.3(g) · Eduqas C1.4(b), (c). Level: AS & A for Edexcel A 4.14; A-level for the rest.*

### What you need to know

- The conditions bacteria need to grow.
- Aseptic technique: what it is, why it is needed.
- Culturing in broth and on agar; selective media.
- Streak plating to isolate pure colonies.

### Explained

**Conditions for growth** (Edexcel A 4.14, Eduqas C1.4(c)).

- Nutrients: a carbon source (glucose), a nitrogen source (amino acids, nitrate), vitamins and minerals.
- Suitable temperature: many pathogens grow best at about 37 °C; school cultures are incubated at no more than 25 °C to avoid encouraging human pathogens.
- Suitable pH (most bacteria near neutral).
- Oxygen: obligate aerobes need it; obligate anaerobes are killed by it; facultative anaerobes grow either way.
- Water.

**Aseptic technique** prevents contamination of the culture by unwanted microorganisms and stops the cultured microorganisms escaping into the environment or infecting the user.

- Wash hands; disinfect the bench before and after.
- Work next to a lit Bunsen burner: the rising convection current carries airborne microbes away from the work.
- Sterilise media and glassware in an autoclave (121 °C under pressure for 15 minutes).
- Flame the wire inoculating loop until red hot; let it cool before touching the culture.
- Flame the neck of culture bottles after removing the lid and before replacing it.
- Lift a Petri dish lid only slightly and for as short a time as possible.
- Secure lids with two pieces of tape (not sealed all round, to keep conditions aerobic and prevent anaerobic pathogens growing).
- Incubate plates upside down (condensation does not drip onto the agar).
- After use, autoclave all plates and cultures before disposal.

**Media.**

- *Broth:* liquid nutrient medium in a flask or tube; used to grow large numbers quickly and to measure growth by turbidity.
- *Agar:* nutrient broth set with agar (a polysaccharide from seaweed) in Petri dishes; single cells grow into visible colonies.
- *Selective media:* contain substances that let only some microorganisms grow (an antibiotic; high salt for *Staphylococcus*; MacConkey agar for Gram-negative gut bacteria). Used to isolate or identify a species, or to select bacteria that have taken up a plasmid (file 25).

**Streak plating** (Edexcel B CP13). Flame a loop, dip it in a mixed culture, and streak it in parallel lines across one section of an agar plate. Flame the loop again, drag it from the end of the first streaks into a fresh section, and streak again; repeat for three or four sections. Each step dilutes the bacteria; in the last section single cells are separated and grow into single colonies, each a pure culture of one species. Pick a single colony with a sterile loop to subculture it.

**Testing antibiotics** (Edexcel A CP15, OCR B 3.2.3(g); also AQA RP6, file 13). Spread a lawn of bacteria on agar; add paper discs soaked in different antibiotics (or a multodisc); incubate; measure the zone of inhibition. A larger clear zone means the bacteria are more sensitive (but the zone also depends on how fast the antibiotic diffuses through agar). **Antimicrobial plants** (Edexcel A CP9): discs soaked in extracts of garlic, mint or other plants, made by grinding in ethanol and letting the ethanol evaporate; a disc soaked in ethanol and dried is the control.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the Bunsen burner "kills bacteria in the air".
- Sealing the Petri dish all the way round.
- Incubating at 37 °C in a school lab.

## Measuring growth and the growth curve

*Refs: Edexcel B 6.1 iv, v, CP12 · OCR A 6.2.1(h) · Eduqas C1.4(d). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Methods to measure population growth: total and viable counts, serial dilution and plating, haemocytometer counts, turbidity (optical density), dry mass.
- The phases of a bacterial growth curve in a closed culture: lag, log (exponential), stationary and death (decline).
- Use N = N₀ × 2ⁿ and logarithmic scales.

### Explained

**Total and viable counts.** A *total count* counts all cells, living and dead (haemocytometer, turbidity). A *viable count* counts only living cells that can divide (dilution plating).

**Serial dilution and plating (viable count).**

1. Make a series of ten-fold dilutions: 1 cm³ culture into 9 cm³ sterile water (10⁻¹), then 1 cm³ of that into 9 cm³ (10⁻²), and so on.
2. Spread 0.1 cm³ of several dilutions onto agar plates; incubate.
3. Choose a plate with 30 to 300 separate colonies. Each colony grew from one cell (a colony-forming unit).
4. Number of cells in original culture = number of colonies × dilution factor ÷ volume plated.

**Haemocytometer** (total count; file 49 for blood cells). A slide with a grid of known area and a chamber of known depth (0.1 mm). Count cells in several squares; multiply by the volume factor. Adding trypan blue (or methylene blue) stains dead cells so live and dead can be told apart.

**Turbidity.** As bacteria multiply, the broth becomes cloudy. A colorimeter measures absorbance (optical density); a calibration curve links absorbance to cell number. Fast, but counts living and dead cells.

**Dry mass.** Filter, dry and weigh samples. Accurate for biomass, but slow and destroys the sample.

**The growth curve** in a closed culture (a fixed volume of medium with nothing added or removed).

| Phase | What happens | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lag | Little increase in number | Cells adjust to new conditions: taking up water, making enzymes for the new nutrients, copying DNA |
| Log (exponential) | Number doubles each generation | Plenty of nutrients and space; little toxic waste |
| Stationary | Number stays roughly constant | Reproduction rate = death rate; nutrients running out; waste (acids, toxins) building up |
| Death (decline) | Number falls | Nutrients exhausted; toxic waste high; death rate exceeds reproduction |

Because numbers rise so quickly, the y-axis often uses a log scale (log₁₀ of cell number). On a log scale the log phase is a straight line.

**N = N₀ × 2ⁿ** (OCR A), where N₀ is the starting number and n is the number of generations (doublings).

### Worked example

1. Serial dilution: 0.1 cm³ of the 10⁻⁵ dilution produced 64 colonies. How many bacteria per cm³ were in the original culture?

64 ÷ 0.1 = 640 per cm³ in the 10⁻⁵ dilution; × 10⁵ = 6.4 × 10⁷ bacteria per cm³.

2. A culture starts with 500 bacteria and divides every 20 minutes during the log phase. How many after 3 hours?

n = 180 ÷ 20 = 9 generations. N = 500 × 2⁹ = 500 × 512 = 256 000 = 2.56 × 10⁵.

### Common mistakes

- Saying bacteria "do not grow" in the lag phase. They are active, just not dividing much yet.
- Counting plates with overlapping colonies.
- Forgetting the volume plated when calculating the original concentration.

### Practical

**Growth of bacteria in liquid culture** (Edexcel B CP12; OCR A PAG7). Inoculate sterile nutrient broth with a known volume of bacteria (or yeast). Incubate at a constant temperature, shaking. Every 30 minutes, take a sample aseptically and measure absorbance with a colorimeter (zeroed with sterile broth), or do dilution plating. Plot absorbance (or log number) against time to show the phases. Vary temperature or glucose concentration to investigate their effect.

**Bacteria in fresh and stale milk** (Eduqas). Use serial dilution and plating, or add resazurin (a redox indicator that changes from blue to pink to colourless as bacteria respire); the faster it changes colour, the more bacteria.

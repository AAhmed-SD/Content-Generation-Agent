# Gas exchange and circulation across animal groups

Animals of different sizes and lifestyles exchange gases and move blood in different ways. This file compares gas exchange across the body surface in *Amoeba*, flatworms and earthworms with specialised surfaces, compares open and closed, single and double circulations, and compares the oxygen-carrying pigments of animals adapted to different conditions.

## Gas exchange across the body surface

*Refs: Eduqas C3.1(b), (c). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Gas exchange in small animals across their general body surface.
- Comparison of gas exchange in *Amoeba*, flatworm and earthworm.

### Explained

| | *Amoeba* | Flatworm (e.g. *Planaria*) | Earthworm |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Size and shape | Single cell, tiny | Multicellular, flattened | Larger, cylindrical |
| Surface area to volume ratio | Very large | Large because the body is flat: no cell far from the surface | Smaller |
| Exchange surface | Cell-surface membrane | Body surface | Moist skin with many capillaries just beneath |
| Transport | Diffusion through cytoplasm | Diffusion (short distances); branched gut also reaches all cells | Closed circulatory system with blood containing haemoglobin, pumped by "hearts" (pseudohearts) |
| Ventilation | None | None | None (but moves through soil; slow metabolism) |
| Habitat need | Water | Water or damp places | Damp soil (skin must stay moist; mucus keeps it moist) |

The earthworm is the largest animal that can rely on its body surface: it manages because its long thin shape gives a reasonable surface area, it has a low metabolic rate, and blood transport keeps a steep gradient at the skin.

**Larger, more active animals** have higher metabolic rates and smaller surface area to volume ratios, so they need (file 08): a specialised respiratory surface with a large area, thin walls and a good blood supply; a ventilation mechanism to keep the gradient steep; and a transport system with a respiratory pigment.

**Water and air.** Water holds much less oxygen than air and is denser, so aquatic animals need efficient surfaces (gills with countercurrent flow) and use more energy to ventilate. Air-breathing surfaces must be kept moist and are inside the body (lungs, tracheae) to reduce water loss.

## Circulatory systems

*Refs: Eduqas C3.2(a) · OCR A 3.1.2(b) · Edexcel B 4.4 ii. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Open and closed circulatory systems; single and double circulations.
- The vascular systems of earthworms, insects, fish and mammals.
- The advantages of a double circulation in mammals.

### Explained

**Open circulation.** Blood is pumped out of vessels into a body cavity (haemocoel), where it bathes the organs directly, then returns to the heart through openings (ostia).

- *Insects:* a dorsal, tube-shaped heart pumps blood (haemolymph) forwards along the body, then it flows back through the haemocoel. Blood carries food, wastes and hormones, but not respiratory gases: insects have no respiratory pigment, and oxygen is delivered directly to tissues by the tracheal system (file 08). Low pressure, slow flow, little control over where blood goes; enough because gases do not depend on it.

**Closed circulation.** Blood stays inside vessels; substances pass to tissues through capillary walls into tissue fluid. Higher pressure, faster flow, and blood can be directed to where it is needed.

- *Earthworm:* closed system; a dorsal vessel and a ventral vessel joined by five pairs of muscular "pseudohearts" that pump blood; capillaries in the skin pick up oxygen; blood contains haemoglobin dissolved in plasma.
- *Fish:* single circulation. The heart (two main chambers) pumps blood to the gills, then it flows on directly to the body, then back to the heart: heart → gills → body → heart. Blood passes through the heart once per circuit. Pressure falls a lot in the gill capillaries, so blood reaches the body at low pressure; this suits fish, which are supported by water and are ectotherms with lower metabolic rates.
- *Mammal:* double circulation (file 10). Heart → lungs → heart → body → heart. Blood passes through the heart twice per circuit.

**Advantages of a double circulation.**

- Blood returning from the lungs is pumped again, so it reaches the body at high pressure and flows quickly, delivering oxygen and glucose fast to support a high metabolic rate (needed for endothermy and activity).
- The pulmonary circulation can be at lower pressure, so lung capillaries are not damaged and tissue fluid does not flood the alveoli.
- Oxygenated and deoxygenated blood are kept separate (in mammals and birds, by a complete septum), so blood leaving for the body is fully oxygenated.

Amphibians and most reptiles have a double circulation with a three-chambered heart, where some mixing happens.

## Oxygen-carrying pigments compared

*Refs: Eduqas C3.2(e), (f) · OCR A 3.1.2(i) · Edexcel B 4.5 iii, iv. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The dissociation curves of animals adapted to low-oxygen habitats (llama, lugworm), of fetal haemoglobin, and of myoglobin.
- The Bohr effect and the chloride shift (file 10).

### Explained

Position of the curve shows affinity: further left means higher affinity (loads at lower pO₂); further right means lower affinity (unloads more easily).

| Pigment | Position compared with adult human haemoglobin | Explanation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lugworm haemoglobin | Far to the left | Lives in a burrow in sand, where water at low tide has little oxygen; high affinity lets it load oxygen at low pO₂; it is inactive, so it does not need to unload easily |
| Llama haemoglobin | To the left | Lives at high altitude where the partial pressure of oxygen in air is low; high affinity lets it saturate in the lungs |
| Fetal haemoglobin | To the left | Must take oxygen from maternal haemoglobin at the placenta, where pO₂ is low |
| Myoglobin | Far left, and hyperbolic (one polypeptide, one haem, so no cooperative binding) | Stores oxygen in muscle; releases it only at very low pO₂, during intense exercise, delaying anaerobic respiration |
| Small mammal (mouse) haemoglobin | To the right | High metabolic rate; oxygen unloads easily to respiring tissues |

**The Bohr effect** (higher CO₂ shifts the curve right) helps unload oxygen in active tissues; **the chloride shift** maintains charge balance when hydrogencarbonate ions leave red blood cells (both in file 10).

### Common mistakes

- Saying insects carry oxygen in their blood.
- Saying fish have a double circulation.
- Saying a curve to the left means "lower affinity".

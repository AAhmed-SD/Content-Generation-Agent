# Nerve impulses, synapses and skeletal muscle

Neurones carry electrical impulses; synapses pass the signal chemically to the next cell; muscles turn the signal into movement. This file covers the resting and action potential, conduction speed, synaptic transmission and the sliding filament model of muscle contraction.

## 3.6.2.1 Nerve impulses

*Refs: AQA 3.6.2.1 · Edexcel A 8.1, 8.3 · Edexcel B 9.5 i to iii · OCR A 5.1.3(b), (c) · OCR B 5.2.1(c), (d) · Eduqas C3.5(f) to (i). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The structure of a myelinated motor neurone.
- The establishment of a resting potential in terms of differential membrane permeability, electrochemical gradients and the movement of sodium ions and potassium ions.
- Changes in membrane permeability lead to depolarisation and the generation of an action potential. The all-or-nothing principle.
- The passage of an action potential along non-myelinated and myelinated axons, resulting in nerve impulses.
- The nature and importance of the refractory period in producing discrete impulses and in limiting the frequency of impulse transmission.
- Factors affecting the speed of conductance: myelination and saltatory conduction; axon diameter; temperature.

### Explained

**A motor neurone** has a cell body (nucleus, many ribosomes and rough ER to make neurotransmitter and proteins), dendrites that receive impulses from other neurones, a long axon that carries impulses away, and axon terminals ending at the effector. Many axons are wrapped in a **myelin sheath** formed by **Schwann cells** wrapping round the axon. Between Schwann cells are small gaps called **nodes of Ranvier**. (Sensory neurones have the cell body part-way along; relay neurones are short and in the CNS: file 32.)

**Resting potential** (about −70 mV: the inside is negative compared to the outside).

1. The sodium–potassium pump actively moves 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in, using ATP.
2. The membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than Na⁺: many K⁺ channels are open; most Na⁺ channels are closed.
3. K⁺ diffuses back out down its gradient. Na⁺ cannot easily diffuse back in.
4. More positive ions are outside than inside, so the axon is polarised.

**Action potential.**

1. *Depolarisation:* a stimulus opens some voltage-gated Na⁺ channels; Na⁺ diffuses in. If the potential reaches the **threshold** (about −55 mV), many more voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open (positive feedback); Na⁺ floods in; the inside reaches about +40 mV.
2. *Repolarisation:* Na⁺ channels close; voltage-gated K⁺ channels open; K⁺ diffuses out; the potential falls.
3. *Hyperpolarisation:* K⁺ channels are slow to close, so too many K⁺ leave and the potential briefly drops below −70 mV.
4. The resting potential is restored by the sodium–potassium pump and K⁺ channels closing.

**All-or-nothing.** If the threshold is reached, an action potential of the same size (+40 mV) always follows; below threshold, none. A stronger stimulus does not give a bigger action potential; it gives more action potentials per second (higher frequency).

**Passage along the axon.** In a non-myelinated axon, Na⁺ entering one region diffuses sideways (local currents), depolarising the next region to threshold; a wave of depolarisation travels along. In a myelinated axon, the myelin insulates the axon, so depolarisation happens only at the nodes of Ranvier; the impulse jumps from node to node (**saltatory conduction**), much faster.

**Refractory period.** After an action potential, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are closed and cannot open again for a short time. This:

- means the impulse travels in one direction only (the region behind cannot fire)
- produces discrete (separate) impulses rather than merging
- limits the number of impulses per second.

**Speed of conduction.**

- Myelination: much faster (saltatory conduction).
- Axon diameter: wider axons conduct faster (less resistance to the flow of ions; less leakage).
- Temperature: faster up to about 40 °C, because ions diffuse faster and the pump works faster; above this proteins denature.

(Eduqas) Invertebrates such as squid and earthworms have **giant axons** (up to 1 mm wide, non-myelinated) for fast escape responses. A myelinated mammalian axon conducts up to about 100 m s⁻¹; a non-myelinated one about 1 m s⁻¹.

**Oscilloscope traces** (Eduqas) plot membrane potential against time. Read off the resting potential, threshold, peak, and the time of one action potential (about 1 to 2 ms) to calculate maximum frequency.

### Worked example

An action potential takes 3 ms from the start of depolarisation to the end of the refractory period. What is the maximum frequency of impulses?

1 ÷ 0.003 = 333 impulses per second.

A myelinated neurone 0.9 m long conducts at 90 m s⁻¹. How long does an impulse take to travel its length? 0.9 ÷ 90 = 0.01 s = 10 ms.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the inside is positive at rest.
- Saying the sodium–potassium pump causes depolarisation. Voltage-gated channels do.
- Saying a bigger stimulus gives a bigger action potential.
- Saying the impulse "jumps from one Schwann cell to another"; it is from node to node.
- Saying myelin conducts the impulse; it insulates.

## 3.6.2.2 Synaptic transmission

*Refs: AQA 3.6.2.2 · Edexcel A 8.4 · Edexcel B 9.5 iv, v · OCR A 5.1.3(d) · OCR B 5.2.1(e) · Eduqas C3.5(j) to (l). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The detailed structure of a synapse and of a neuromuscular junction.
- The sequence of events involved in transmission across a cholinergic synapse in sufficient detail to explain: unidirectionality; temporal and spatial summation; inhibition by inhibitory synapses.
- A comparison of transmission across a cholinergic synapse and across a neuromuscular junction.
- Use information provided to predict the effects of specific drugs on a synapse.

### Explained

**Structure.** The presynaptic neurone ends in a swollen knob with many mitochondria and vesicles of neurotransmitter. A gap of about 20 nm (the synaptic cleft) separates it from the postsynaptic membrane, which has receptor proteins for the neurotransmitter. In a cholinergic synapse the neurotransmitter is acetylcholine (ACh).

**Transmission.**

1. An action potential reaches the presynaptic knob.
2. Voltage-gated calcium ion channels open; Ca²⁺ diffuses into the knob.
3. Ca²⁺ causes vesicles to move to and fuse with the presynaptic membrane, releasing ACh into the cleft by exocytosis.
4. ACh diffuses across the cleft and binds to complementary receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.
5. Sodium ion channels in the receptors open; Na⁺ diffuses in, depolarising the postsynaptic membrane. If threshold is reached, an action potential starts.
6. Acetylcholinesterase in the cleft hydrolyses ACh into choline and ethanoic acid (acetyl). These diffuse back into the presynaptic knob and are recombined into ACh using ATP. Removal stops the signal and lets the receptor be used again.

**Unidirectional.** Only the presynaptic knob has vesicles; only the postsynaptic membrane has receptors.

**Summation.** One release of neurotransmitter may not reach threshold.

- *Spatial summation:* several presynaptic neurones release neurotransmitter onto one postsynaptic neurone at the same time; together they reach threshold.
- *Temporal summation:* one presynaptic neurone releases neurotransmitter repeatedly in quick succession; the effect builds up.

**Inhibitory synapses.** Some neurotransmitters (such as GABA) open chloride channels (Cl⁻ diffuses in) or potassium channels (K⁺ diffuses out) on the postsynaptic membrane. The inside becomes more negative (hyperpolarised), so more excitatory input is needed to reach threshold. Edexcel B calls these **inhibitory postsynaptic potentials (IPSPs)**; depolarisations that move towards threshold are **excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs)**. The postsynaptic neurone sums EPSPs and IPSPs.

**Neuromuscular junction** compared with a cholinergic synapse:

| | Cholinergic synapse | Neuromuscular junction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Connects | Neurone to neurone | Motor neurone to muscle fibre |
| Effect | Excitatory or inhibitory | Always excitatory |
| Postsynaptic membrane | Smooth | Folded into clefts with many receptors and acetylcholinesterase |
| Result | Action potential in next neurone (if threshold reached) | Every impulse normally causes depolarisation of the muscle fibre membrane |

**Drugs** (predict effects from information).

- A drug that mimics the neurotransmitter (same shape, binds receptors) increases stimulation (nicotine at some ACh receptors).
- A drug that blocks receptors reduces it (curare at the neuromuscular junction causes paralysis).
- A drug that inhibits acetylcholinesterase (organophosphate insecticides, nerve agents) leaves ACh in the cleft, so muscles stay contracted (Eduqas C3.5(l)).
- A drug that stops neurotransmitter release (some anaesthetics) reduces stimulation.
- A drug that stops reuptake leaves more neurotransmitter in the cleft (file 34 for MDMA and others).

### Common mistakes

- Saying the impulse "jumps" across the synapse.
- Forgetting calcium ions.
- Saying acetylcholinesterase "breaks down the receptor".
- Saying inhibitory synapses "stop the action potential". They make reaching threshold less likely.

## 3.6.3 Skeletal muscles are stimulated to contract by nerves and act as effectors

*Refs: AQA 3.6.3 · Edexcel A 7.1, 7.2, 7.10 · OCR A 5.1.5(l) · OCR B 4.1.2(k), (l) · Eduqas OB.1(h) to (k). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Muscles act in antagonistic pairs against an incompressible skeleton.
- Gross and microscopic structure of skeletal muscle. The ultrastructure of a myofibril.
- The roles of actin, myosin, calcium ions and ATP in myofibril contraction. The roles of calcium ions and tropomyosin in the cycle of actinomyosin bridge formation. (The role of troponin is not required by AQA.)
- The roles of ATP and phosphocreatine in muscle contraction.
- The structure, location and general properties of slow and fast skeletal muscle fibres.

### Explained

**Antagonistic pairs.** Muscles can only pull. One muscle contracts while its partner relaxes: biceps contracts to flex (bend) the elbow; triceps contracts to extend it. The skeleton is incompressible, so the force moves the bones. Tendons join muscle to bone (strong, inelastic); ligaments join bone to bone at joints (Edexcel A 7.1).

**Structure.** A muscle is made of bundles of muscle fibres. Each fibre is a long multinucleate cell with a cell membrane (sarcolemma) folded inwards as T-tubules, sarcoplasm with many mitochondria, sarcoplasmic reticulum (stores Ca²⁺), and many myofibrils.

**Myofibril.** Made of repeating units called **sarcomeres**, joined end to end at Z-lines.

- Thin filaments: actin (with tropomyosin wrapped round them, and troponin attached).
- Thick filaments: myosin, each with many heads.
- A-band (dark): the length of the myosin filaments, including where they overlap actin.
- I-band (light): actin only.
- H-zone: myosin only, in the middle of the A-band.
- M-line: middle of the sarcomere.

**Sliding filament model.** In contraction the actin filaments slide over the myosin, pulled towards the middle. The sarcomere shortens: the I-band and H-zone get shorter; the A-band stays the same length.

**The cycle.**

1. An action potential arrives at the neuromuscular junction; depolarisation spreads along the sarcolemma and down T-tubules.
2. Ca²⁺ is released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum into the sarcoplasm.
3. Ca²⁺ binds to troponin, which moves tropomyosin away from the binding sites on actin.
4. Myosin heads (with ADP attached) bind to the exposed binding sites, forming actinomyosin cross-bridges.
5. The myosin heads change angle, pulling the actin filament towards the M-line (the power stroke), releasing ADP.
6. ATP binds to the myosin head, causing it to detach from actin.
7. ATP hydrolase (ATPase) in the myosin head hydrolyses the ATP; the energy returns the head to its original position.
8. The head binds further along the actin, and the cycle repeats while Ca²⁺ and ATP are present.

When stimulation stops, Ca²⁺ is actively transported back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum (using ATP); tropomyosin covers the binding sites again; the muscle relaxes.

**ATP sources.** A muscle's ATP lasts only a few seconds.

- Aerobic respiration (main source over time).
- Anaerobic respiration (glycolysis to lactate; fast but short).
- **Phosphocreatine** (PCr), stored in muscle, gives its phosphate to ADP: PCr + ADP → creatine + ATP. Very fast, no oxygen needed, lasts a few seconds (sprints). It is regenerated from ATP when the muscle is resting.

**Slow and fast fibres.**

| | Slow-twitch | Fast-twitch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contraction | Slower, less powerful, for a long time | Rapid, powerful, short bursts |
| Where common | Calves, back (posture), endurance | Arms, legs used for sprinting |
| Respiration | Aerobic | Anaerobic; also phosphocreatine stores |
| Mitochondria | Many | Few |
| Blood supply | Many capillaries | Fewer |
| Myoglobin | Lots (red colour; stores oxygen) | Little (white) |
| Fatigue | Resistant | Tire quickly (lactate) |
| Other | | Thicker, more myosin filaments; more enzymes for glycolysis; more glycogen |

**Three muscle types** (OCR A).

| | Skeletal | Cardiac | Involuntary (smooth) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Appearance | Striated, long multinucleate fibres | Striated, branched cells joined by intercalated discs | Not striated; spindle-shaped cells, one nucleus |
| Control | Voluntary (somatic nervous system) | Myogenic; rate changed by autonomic nerves | Involuntary (autonomic) |
| Contraction | Rapid; fatigues | Rhythmic; does not fatigue | Slow, sustained |
| Where | Attached to bones | Heart | Walls of gut, blood vessels, bronchioles |

### Worked example

A sarcomere is 2.4 µm long when relaxed; the A-band is 1.6 µm. When contracted the sarcomere is 2.0 µm. Find the A-band and total I-band lengths when contracted.

1. The A-band stays the same: 1.6 µm.
2. Sarcomere − A-band = I-band total (the two half I-bands): 2.0 − 1.6 = 0.4 µm (relaxed it was 0.8 µm).

### Common mistakes

- Saying the filaments themselves shorten.
- Saying the A-band shortens.
- Saying ATP is needed for the power stroke. ATP is needed to detach the head and recock it; the power stroke releases ADP.
- Saying calcium binds to tropomyosin (it binds to troponin; AQA accepts "calcium ions cause tropomyosin to move").
- Saying slow-twitch fibres "contract slowly so they use less energy" without linking to aerobic respiration.

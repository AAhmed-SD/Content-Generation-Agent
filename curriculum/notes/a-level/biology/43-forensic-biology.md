# Forensic biology: time of death and decomposition

Forensic scientists estimate when a person or animal died from changes in the body. This file covers the methods used to estimate time of death and the role of microorganisms in decomposition and the carbon cycle.

## Overview

A-level only. Only Edexcel A has this as content. DNA profiling and PCR, used in forensic identification, are in file 25. The carbon cycle is in file 17.

### Board differences

- Edexcel A 6.1, 6.2 only.

## Determining the time of death

*Refs: Edexcel A 6.1. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- How to determine the time of death of a mammal by examining the extent of decomposition, stage of succession, forensic entomology, body temperature and degree of muscle contraction (rigor mortis).

### Explained

No single method is precise; investigators combine several, and each is affected by conditions.

**Body temperature (algor mortis).** After death, respiration stops, so no more heat is released and the body cools until it reaches the temperature of its surroundings. A rough rule: about 1 to 1.5 °C per hour for the first several hours (from 37 °C). Temperature is measured in the rectum or liver. Faster cooling with: low surrounding temperature, wind, wet clothes or immersion in water, a thin or small body, no clothing. Slower with: warm conditions, clothing, more body fat. Not useful once the body has reached air temperature (usually after about 24 hours).

**Rigor mortis (muscle stiffening).**

1. After death, respiration stops, so ATP is no longer made aerobically; anaerobic respiration briefly continues, lactate builds up and pH falls.
2. Without ATP, myosin heads cannot detach from actin (file 19); cross-bridges stay locked and muscles become stiff.
3. Rigor starts about 2 to 6 hours after death, in small muscles first (face, neck), spreads to larger muscles, is complete at about 12 hours, and lasts until about 24 to 36 hours.
4. It then wears off as proteins in the muscle are broken down by enzymes (autolysis).

Rigor develops faster at higher temperatures and in people who were very active just before death (less ATP and glycogen in muscle).

**Extent of decomposition.**

1. Hours to days: cells are broken down by their own enzymes (autolysis); the skin may turn greenish (bacteria in the gut digest tissues and produce pigments).
2. Days: bacteria multiply, releasing gases (hydrogen sulfide, methane) that make the body bloat; strong smell; skin blisters.
3. Weeks: soft tissues liquefy and break down (active decay).
4. Months to years: only skeleton, tendons and hair remain.

Decomposition is faster in warm, moist conditions with oxygen and access to insects; slower in cold, dry or waterlogged conditions, or if buried.

**Forensic entomology.** Insects arrive at a body in a predictable order, and their development rate depends on temperature.

- Blowflies arrive within minutes to hours and lay eggs. Eggs hatch into larvae (maggots) that grow through stages, then pupate and emerge as adults.
- From the species present and the stage and length of the oldest larvae, and the temperature records, entomologists estimate how long ago eggs were laid (the minimum time since death).
- Later, other insects arrive (beetles feeding on dried tissue; predatory beetles feeding on maggots).
- Limitations: temperature must be known (development is faster when warm); bodies indoors, wrapped or buried may be colonised later; drugs in the body can change larval growth rates.

**Stage of succession.** The community of organisms on a body changes in a sequence, as on bare rock (file 23): bacteria first, then blowflies, then beetles, then mites and others as the body dries; finally, as the skeleton remains, plants may grow through it. Identifying the stage gives an estimate of time since death, especially for longer periods.

### Worked example

A body is found in a room at 18 °C with a rectal temperature of 31 °C. Assuming cooling of 1.5 °C per hour from 37 °C, estimate time since death and give two reasons the estimate may be wrong.

Fall = 37 − 31 = 6 °C. Time ≈ 6 ÷ 1.5 = 4 hours. Reasons: body temperature at death may not have been 37 °C (fever, hypothermia); cooling rate depends on clothing, body size and air movement; the room temperature may have changed.

## Decomposition and the carbon cycle

*Refs: Edexcel A 6.2. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The role of microorganisms in the decomposition of organic matter and the recycling of carbon.

### Explained

Decomposers (bacteria and fungi, file 17) secrete enzymes onto dead organic material and absorb the products: saprotrophic (saprobiotic) nutrition. They respire the products, releasing carbon dioxide (or methane in anaerobic conditions) into the air, where it can be taken up again by photosynthesis. Without decomposition, carbon would be locked in dead bodies and the CO₂ in the air would run out. Detritivores (earthworms, woodlice, maggots) break material into smaller pieces, increasing the surface area for microorganisms.

Conditions that slow decomposition (cold, dry, waterlogged and anaerobic, acidic) lock carbon away for long periods, as in peat bogs, which over millions of years can form fossil fuels.

### Common mistakes

- Saying rigor mortis is caused by "lack of oxygen making muscles contract". It is caused by lack of ATP, so cross-bridges cannot break.
- Saying insects arrive "at random".
- Saying decomposers "eat" dead material; they secrete enzymes and digest it externally.

## Check questions

1. Explain why a body cools after death and give two factors that affect the rate of cooling. (3 marks)
2. Explain why rigor mortis develops after death and why it eventually wears off. (4 marks)
3. Describe how forensic entomology can be used to estimate time of death, and one limitation. (4 marks)
4. Explain why succession on a dead body can be used to estimate time of death. (2 marks)
5. Describe the role of microorganisms in recycling carbon from a dead animal. (3 marks)
6. A body found in a cold river had a temperature similar to that of a body found indoors 6 hours after death. Explain why the river body may have been dead for less time. (2 marks)

3. Blowflies lay eggs soon after death (1); larvae develop through stages at known rates (1); stage / length of oldest larvae and temperature give time since eggs laid (1); limitation: depends on temperature / body may be wrapped or indoors / drugs affect growth (1).

# Mutation, meiosis, genetic diversity and natural selection

Genetic diversity comes from mutation, meiosis and random fertilisation. Natural selection acts on that diversity. This file covers each, with AQA required practical 6 (aseptic technique and antimicrobial substances).

## 3.4.3 Genetic diversity can arise as a result of mutation or during meiosis

*Refs: AQA 3.4.3 · Edexcel A 3.9 · Edexcel B 2.3 iv, v, vii, 8.1 i, ii · OCR A 2.1.6(f), (g), 6.1.2(a) · OCR B 3.1.2(a) to (c) · Eduqas C2.2(d), (e). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Gene mutations involve a change in the base sequence of chromosomes. They can arise spontaneously during DNA replication and include base deletion and base substitution. Due to the degenerate nature of the genetic code, not all base substitutions cause a change in the sequence of encoded amino acids. Mutagenic agents can increase the rate of gene mutation.
- Mutations in the number of chromosomes can arise spontaneously by chromosome non-disjunction during meiosis.
- Meiosis produces daughter cells that are genetically different from each other. The process of meiosis only in sufficient detail to show how: two nuclear divisions result in the formation of haploid daughter cells from a single diploid parent cell; genetically different daughter cells result from the independent segregation of homologous chromosomes; crossing over between homologous chromosomes results in further genetic variation among daughter cells.
- Appreciate that random fertilisation of haploid gametes further increases genetic variation within a species.
- Complete diagrams showing the chromosome content of cells after the first and second meiotic division, when given the chromosome content of the parent cell. Explain the different outcomes of mitosis and meiosis. Recognise where meiosis occurs when given information about an unfamiliar life cycle. Explain how random fertilisation produces genetic variation. Calculate the number of possible combinations of chromosomes, using 2ⁿ.

### Explained

**Gene mutations.**

- *Substitution:* one base replaced by another. Only one triplet changes. It may give the same amino acid (silent, because the code is degenerate), a different amino acid (missense), or a stop codon (nonsense, giving a short polypeptide).
- *Deletion:* one base lost. Every triplet after it changes: a frameshift. Usually the polypeptide is very different and does not work.

Mutagenic agents (mutagens) include ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays), ultraviolet light, and chemicals such as benzene and some in tobacco smoke.

**Homologous chromosomes** are a matching pair, one from each parent, with the same genes at the same loci but possibly different alleles. A diploid cell (2n) has two sets; a haploid cell (n) has one set. Human body cells: 2n = 46; gametes: n = 23.

**Meiosis.**

| Division | Main events |
| --- | --- |
| Meiosis I | Homologous chromosomes pair up (forming bivalents); crossing over happens; pairs line up on the equator; homologous chromosomes (each still two chromatids) are separated to opposite poles. Result: two haploid cells. |
| Meiosis II | Like mitosis: chromosomes line up singly; sister chromatids separate. Result: four haploid cells. |

Named stages for OCR A, OCR B, Edexcel B and Eduqas: prophase I, metaphase I, anaphase I, telophase I, then prophase II, metaphase II, anaphase II, telophase II.

**How meiosis makes variety.**

1. *Independent segregation (independent assortment):* at metaphase I, each homologous pair lines up independently of the others. Which parent's chromosome goes to which pole is random. With n pairs, the number of possible combinations in the gametes is 2ⁿ.
2. *Crossing over:* in prophase I, chromatids of homologous chromosomes wrap round each other; they break and swap equivalent sections at points called chiasmata. New combinations of alleles appear on each chromatid.
3. *Random fertilisation:* any male gamete can fuse with any female gamete. The number of combinations of chromosomes in a zygote from two parents is (2ⁿ)², even ignoring crossing over.

**Non-disjunction.** If homologous chromosomes (in meiosis I) or sister chromatids (in meiosis II) fail to separate, one gamete gets an extra chromosome and another gets one fewer. Fertilisation then produces a zygote with 2n + 1 (trisomy) or 2n − 1 (monosomy). Down's syndrome is trisomy 21 (detail in file 39).

**Mitosis compared with meiosis.**

| | Mitosis | Meiosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Divisions | One | Two |
| Cells made | Two | Four |
| Chromosome number | Same as parent (diploid stays diploid) | Halved (diploid to haploid) |
| Genetic make-up | Identical to each other and the parent | Different from each other and the parent |
| Homologous pairs | Do not pair | Pair up and cross over |
| Use | Growth, repair, asexual reproduction | Gamete formation for sexual reproduction |

**Unfamiliar life cycles.** Meiosis happens wherever the chromosome number halves (2n → n). In mammals it is in gamete formation; in fungi and many algae it happens straight after the zygote forms; in plants it forms spores.

### Worked example

1. A species has 2n = 8. How many combinations of chromosomes are possible in its gametes, and in a zygote from two parents (ignoring crossing over)?

n = 4. Gametes: 2⁴ = 16. Zygotes: 16² = 256.

2. A cell with 2n = 4 contains 8 units of DNA at the start of meiosis (after replication). How many units are in each cell after meiosis I and after meiosis II?

After meiosis I: 4 units (two chromosomes, each two chromatids). After meiosis II: 2 units.

### Common mistakes

- Saying crossing over happens between sister chromatids. It happens between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes.
- Saying independent segregation happens in anaphase II.
- Saying a deletion changes "one amino acid".
- Saying meiosis gives "two cells" or "diploid gametes".

## 3.4.4 Genetic diversity and adaptation

*Refs: AQA 3.4.4, RP6 · Edexcel A 4.4 · Edexcel B 3.2 i · OCR A 4.2.2(f) to (i) · OCR B 3.1.3(e), (g) · Eduqas C2.1(m), (n), C2.6(a), (b). Level: AS & A (A-level only for Eduqas C2.6).*

### What you need to know

- Genetic diversity as the number of different alleles of genes in a population.
- Genetic diversity is a factor enabling natural selection to occur.
- The principles of natural selection in the evolution of populations: random mutation can result in new alleles of a gene; many mutations are harmful but, in certain environments, the new allele of a gene might benefit its possessor, leading to increased reproductive success; the advantageous allele is inherited by members of the next generation; as a result, over many generations, the new allele increases in frequency in the population.
- Directional selection, exemplified by antibiotic resistance in bacteria, and stabilising selection, exemplified by human birth weights.
- Natural selection results in species that are better adapted to their environment. These adaptations may be anatomical, physiological or behavioural.
- Appreciate the importance of evidence in the theory of natural selection. Use unfamiliar information to explain how selection produces changes within a population.

### Explained

**Genetic diversity** is the variety of alleles in a population. A population with more alleles is more likely to contain individuals that survive a change, such as a new disease. Small populations (after a bottleneck or founder event) lose diversity (file 22).

**Natural selection, step by step.**

1. Individuals vary because of different alleles, which arise by random mutation.
2. A selection pressure acts (a predator, a disease, a change in climate, an antibiotic).
3. Individuals with an advantageous allele are more likely to survive and reproduce.
4. They pass the allele to their offspring.
5. Over many generations, the frequency of the allele increases in the population.

Selection does not cause the mutation. The allele must already be there.

**Types of selection.**

- *Directional:* individuals at one extreme are favoured, so the mean shifts. Example: bacteria with an allele for resistance survive an antibiotic and reproduce; the population becomes resistant. Others: peppered moths; insecticide resistance; warfarin resistance in rats.
- *Stabilising:* individuals near the mean are favoured and extremes are selected against, so the mean stays the same but the range (standard deviation) narrows. Example: human birth weight; very small babies are more likely to die of cold or infection, and very large babies are more likely to cause problems at birth.
- (OCR A, Edexcel B) *Disruptive:* both extremes are favoured; can lead to two forms and eventually speciation (file 22).

**Types of adaptation.**

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anatomical | Body structure | Thick fur in arctic foxes; sunken stomata; long loop of Henle in desert mammals |
| Physiological | Processes inside the body | Hibernation (lower metabolic rate); producing venom; high-affinity haemoglobin at altitude |
| Behavioural | Actions | Courtship displays; migration; woodlice moving to damp places |

**Variation** (OCR A, Eduqas). Variation is differences between individuals. *Continuous variation* has a range of values with no categories (height, mass); it is usually controlled by many genes (polygenic) plus the environment, and gives a normal distribution. *Discontinuous variation* falls into distinct categories (blood group, sex); it is usually controlled by one or two genes with little environmental effect. Variation is genetic (inherited), environmental (diet, light, training), or both. Only genetic variation is heritable and can be acted on by natural selection.

**Convergent evolution** (OCR A). Unrelated species in similar environments evolve similar adaptations: the marsupial mole of Australia and the placental mole of Europe both have spade-like forelimbs and tiny eyes.

**Importance of evidence.** Darwin's theory was accepted because it explained many observations (fossils, selective breeding, distribution of species) and made predictions that later evidence confirmed, such as the genetic basis of inheritance and DNA similarities between related species.

### Common mistakes

- Saying bacteria "become resistant" or "build up immunity" after exposure to antibiotics. A mutation gives resistance at random; the antibiotic selects those that already have it.
- Saying organisms "adapt" in their lifetime in response to need.
- Saying individuals evolve. Populations evolve.
- Forgetting the last step: "over many generations the allele frequency increases".
- Confusing genetic diversity with biodiversity.

### Practical

**AQA required practical 6** (similar to Edexcel A CP9, OCR A PAG7 and OCR B 3.2.3(g); culturing detail for those boards is in file 28): use of aseptic techniques to investigate the effect of antimicrobial substances on microbial growth.

1. Spread a liquid culture of a safe bacterium (such as *Bacillus subtilis*) evenly over a nutrient agar plate with a sterile spreader.
2. Soak paper discs in different antibiotics, antiseptics or plant extracts (and one in sterile water as a control). Place them on the agar with sterile forceps.
3. Tape the lid on with two short pieces (not sealed all round, so aerobic conditions stay and anaerobic pathogens do not grow), label the base, and incubate upside down at no more than 25 °C (so human pathogens do not grow well) for 48 hours.
4. Measure the diameter of each clear zone (zone of inhibition); calculate area = πr².

Aseptic technique: wash hands and disinfect the bench; work near a lit Bunsen burner (rising air carries microbes away); flame the neck of the culture bottle; sterilise inoculating loops in the flame until red hot; lift the Petri dish lid only slightly; dispose of plates by autoclaving. These stop contamination of the culture and protect the user.

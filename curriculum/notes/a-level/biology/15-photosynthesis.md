# Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis traps light energy and stores it in organic molecules. This file covers the light-dependent and light-independent reactions, limiting factors, and AQA required practicals 7 and 8.

## Overview

A-level only. ATP and ATP synthase are introduced in file 03; chemiosmosis is explained alongside respiration in file 16. Energy flow through ecosystems is in file 17. Uses of mineral ions such as magnesium and nitrate (Eduqas C1.2(l)) are in file 41. Crop yields and pest control (OCR B) are in file 42.

## 3.5.1 Photosynthesis

*Refs: AQA 3.5.1, RP7, RP8 · Edexcel A 5.5 to 5.9, CP11 · Edexcel B 5.6 i, ii, 5.7 i to viii, CP10, CP11 · OCR A 5.2.1(a) to (g) · OCR B 4.3.1(a) to (g) · Eduqas C1.1(b), C1.2(a) to (k). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The light-dependent reaction in such detail as to show that: chlorophyll absorbs light, leading to photoionisation of chlorophyll; some of the energy from electrons released during photoionisation is conserved in the production of ATP and reduced NADP; the production of ATP involves electron transfer associated with the electron transfer chain and passage of protons across chloroplast membranes and is catalysed by ATP synthase embedded in these membranes (chemiosmotic theory); photolysis of water produces protons, electrons and oxygen.
- The light-independent reaction uses reduced NADP from the light-dependent reaction to form a simple sugar. The hydrolysis of ATP, also from the light-dependent reaction, provides the additional energy for this reaction.
- The light-independent reaction in such detail as to show that: carbon dioxide reacts with ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP) to form two molecules of glycerate 3-phosphate (GP), catalysed by the enzyme rubisco; ATP and reduced NADP from the light-dependent reaction are used to reduce GP to triose phosphate; some of the triose phosphate is used to regenerate RuBP in the Calvin cycle; some of the triose phosphate is converted to useful organic substances.
- Identify environmental factors that limit the rate of photosynthesis; evaluate data relating to common agricultural practices used to overcome the effect of these limiting factors.

### Explained

**Overall equation:** 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ (using light energy, in chlorophyll).

**The chloroplast.**

- Double membrane (envelope).
- Thylakoids: flattened membrane sacs stacked into grana, joined by intergranal lamellae. Thylakoid membranes hold chlorophyll and other pigments in photosystems, electron carriers and ATP synthase. Site of the light-dependent reaction. Many grana give a large surface area.
- Stroma: fluid around the thylakoids with the enzymes of the Calvin cycle (including rubisco), starch grains, lipid droplets, circular DNA and 70S ribosomes. Site of the light-independent reaction.
- (Eduqas) Chloroplasts are most numerous in palisade cells near the upper surface and can move within the cell towards light. They act as transducers, turning light energy into chemical energy.

**Pigments** (OCR A, Edexcel B, Eduqas). Chlorophyll a and b absorb red and blue-violet light and reflect green. Accessory pigments (carotenoids, xanthophylls) absorb other wavelengths and pass energy to chlorophyll a, widening the range of light used. Pigments are grouped in light-harvesting systems (antenna complexes) around a reaction centre of chlorophyll a. There are two photosystems: PSI (reaction centre absorbs best at 700 nm) and PSII (680 nm).

- *Absorption spectrum:* how much light each pigment absorbs at each wavelength.
- *Action spectrum:* the rate of photosynthesis at each wavelength.

The two match closely, which is evidence that these pigments drive photosynthesis.

**The light-dependent reaction** (thylakoid membranes).

1. Light hits PSII. Energy passes to the chlorophyll in the reaction centre, exciting electrons to a higher energy level so they leave the chlorophyll: **photoionisation**.
2. The electrons pass along an electron transfer chain of carriers in the thylakoid membrane, losing energy at each step.
3. This energy is used to pump protons (H⁺) from the stroma into the thylakoid space, building up a high concentration.
4. Protons diffuse back into the stroma through ATP synthase, which uses the energy to join ADP and Pi: **photophosphorylation**. This is the chemiosmotic theory.
5. The electrons reach PSI, are re-excited by light, and then reduce NADP: NADP + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → reduced NADP.
6. PSII must replace its lost electrons. These come from **photolysis** of water: 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂. Oxygen is a by-product and diffuses out of the leaf; the protons add to the gradient and are used to reduce NADP.

*Non-cyclic photophosphorylation* uses both photosystems and makes ATP, reduced NADP and oxygen (as above). *Cyclic photophosphorylation* (OCR A, Edexcel B, Eduqas) uses PSI only: excited electrons return to PSI via the electron chain, so ATP is made but no reduced NADP and no oxygen. It tops up the ATP needed for the Calvin cycle.

**The light-independent reaction (Calvin cycle)** (stroma).

1. CO₂ combines with ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP, 5C), catalysed by rubisco. The unstable 6C product immediately splits into two molecules of glycerate 3-phosphate (GP, 3C). This is carbon fixation.
2. GP is reduced to triose phosphate (TP, 3C) using reduced NADP (supplies hydrogen) and ATP (supplies energy).
3. Of every 6 TP made, 5 are used to regenerate 3 RuBP, using more ATP. 1 TP leaves the cycle.

Six turns of the cycle fix 6 CO₂ and make one hexose (from 2 TP).

**Uses of TP** (all boards). Two TP join to make glucose, which can make sucrose, starch and cellulose. TP makes glycerol; GP makes fatty acids; with these, lipids. GP plus nitrogen (from nitrate) makes amino acids. TP also makes nucleotides.

**Limiting factors.** At any moment, the rate is limited by the factor in shortest supply: light intensity, CO₂ concentration or temperature. Increasing a limiting factor raises the rate until another factor becomes limiting.

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Light intensity | Low light: less ATP and reduced NADP made, so GP is not reduced to TP; GP rises, TP and RuBP fall |
| CO₂ concentration | Low CO₂: less RuBP is carboxylated; RuBP rises, GP and TP fall |
| Temperature | The light-independent reaction is controlled by enzymes, so rate rises with temperature to an optimum; above it rubisco and other enzymes denature, and stomata may close to save water |
| Water (OCR A) | Water stress closes stomata, reducing CO₂ uptake |

**Agricultural practice.** Glasshouse growers raise CO₂ (by burning fuel, which also warms the air), use artificial lighting, and heat the glasshouse. The extra cost must be balanced against the extra yield: adding more of a factor that is not limiting just wastes money.

**Respiration and photosynthesis** (OCR A, OCR B). Plants respire all the time. At the light compensation point, the rate of photosynthesis equals the rate of respiration, so there is no net gas exchange and no net gain in biomass. The products of each are the raw materials for the other. (OCR B 4.3.1(g)) For crop production, plants must spend as long as possible above the compensation point: growers extend the hours of light and raise light intensity and CO₂ in glasshouses, but heating at night raises respiration and lowers the net gain, so night temperatures are kept low.

### Worked example

1. In a Calvin cycle experiment, the light was switched off. Predict and explain the changes in GP and RuBP.

GP rises: it is still being made from RuBP and CO₂, but without ATP and reduced NADP it cannot be reduced to TP. RuBP falls: it is still being used, but it cannot be regenerated from TP without ATP.

2. In chromatography of leaf pigments, a spot moved 4.2 cm; the solvent moved 9.6 cm. Rf = 4.2 ÷ 9.6 = 0.44. Compare with known values to identify the pigment.

3. A plant fixes 12 molecules of CO₂. How many molecules of glucose can it make, and how many turns of the cycle are needed? 12 turns; 12 ÷ 6 = 2 glucose.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the light-independent reaction "happens in the dark". It happens in light too, and stops soon after dark because ATP and reduced NADP run out.
- Saying oxygen comes from CO₂. It comes from water.
- Saying chlorophyll "absorbs green light".
- Saying ATP is used to reduce GP. Reduced NADP reduces; ATP provides energy.
- Saying rubisco makes GP from TP.
- Saying temperature affects the light-dependent reaction most.

### Practical

**AQA required practical 7** (also Edexcel B CP11, OCR A 5.2.1(c) and PAG6): use of chromatography to investigate the pigments isolated from leaves of different plants.

1. Grind leaves with a little sand and propanone (or another solvent) to extract pigments.
2. Draw a pencil line (origin) about 1.5 cm from the bottom of the chromatography paper or TLC plate.
3. Spot the extract onto the origin with a capillary tube, letting each spot dry, many times, to get a small, concentrated spot.
4. Stand the paper in a little solvent, with the solvent below the origin. Cover.
5. Remove when the solvent nearly reaches the top; mark the solvent front.
6. Calculate Rf values; compare plants (for example shade and sun leaves, or green and red leaves).

Pencil, not ink (ink would dissolve and run). Solvent below the origin (so spots do not dissolve into the solvent). Risks: propanone is flammable and an irritant; work in a ventilated room, no flames.

**AQA required practical 8** (also Edexcel A CP11): investigation into the effect of a named factor on the rate of dehydrogenase activity in extracts of chloroplasts.

1. Grind leaves in ice-cold, isotonic buffer; filter; centrifuge to get a suspension of chloroplasts. Keep on ice.
2. Add DCPIP (blue) to the chloroplast suspension. DCPIP accepts electrons from the light-dependent reaction in place of NADP and turns colourless.
3. Shine light on the tubes; measure the time for the blue colour to go, or record absorbance with a colorimeter at intervals.
4. Vary one factor: light intensity (distance from lamp), light colour (filters), temperature, or an inhibitor.
5. Controls: a tube kept in the dark (no colour change shows light is needed); a tube with no chloroplasts (no change shows chloroplasts are needed).

Why cold, isotonic and buffered: as in cell fractionation (file 04) — cold slows enzymes that damage chloroplasts; isotonic stops osmosis damaging them; buffer keeps the pH stable.

**Rate of photosynthesis with pondweed** (Edexcel B CP10, OCR A PAG11, OCR B 4.3.1(d)). Count oxygen bubbles or collect gas in a photosynthometer from *Elodea* or *Cabomba* in sodium hydrogencarbonate solution (supplies CO₂). Vary light intensity (light intensity ∝ 1/d²) or use colour filters for wavelength. Keep temperature constant with a water bath or a heat shield.

## Mark scheme

1. Water split using light energy into protons, electrons and oxygen (1); electrons replace those lost from chlorophyll / PSII (1); protons used to reduce NADP / add to proton gradient; oxygen released as by-product (1).
2. Light causes photoionisation; electrons pass along electron transfer chain (1); energy used to pump protons into thylakoid space (1); protons diffuse back through ATP synthase (1); ADP + Pi → ATP (1).
3. CO₂ combines with RuBP (1); catalysed by rubisco (1); forms two GP (1); GP reduced to TP using reduced NADP and ATP (1); most TP regenerates RuBP using ATP; some used to make glucose / organic substances (1).
4. Less CO₂ to combine with RuBP (1); RuBP rises, as still regenerated but used less (1); GP falls as less made but still converted to TP (1).
5. Identify limiting factor (e.g. CO₂ in winter) and increase it (1); only increase factor up to point where another becomes limiting (1); compare cost of input with value of extra yield (1).
6. Any two: cyclic uses PSI only; cyclic produces no reduced NADP; cyclic produces no oxygen / no photolysis.
7. Shows DCPIP reduction / colour change needs light (1); so any change in other tubes is due to light-dependent reaction (1).
8. Intensity ∝ 1/d²; halving distance gives 4 times the intensity (1).

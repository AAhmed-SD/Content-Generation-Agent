# Homeostasis: blood glucose and blood water potential

Homeostasis keeps the internal environment within narrow limits. This file covers negative and positive feedback, the control of blood glucose by insulin, glucagon and adrenaline, diabetes, and the control of blood water potential by the kidney and ADH, with AQA required practical 11.

## 3.6.4.1 Principles of homeostasis and negative feedback

*Refs: AQA 3.6.4.1 · Edexcel A 7.11, 7.12 · Edexcel B 9.1 i to iii · OCR A 5.1.1(c) · OCR B 5.3.1(a) · Eduqas C3.4(a), (b). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Homeostasis in mammals involves physiological control systems that maintain the internal environment within restricted limits.
- The importance of maintaining a stable core temperature and stable blood pH in relation to enzyme activity.
- The importance of maintaining a stable blood glucose concentration in terms of availability of respiratory substrate and of the water potential of the blood.
- Negative feedback restores systems to their original level. The possession of separate mechanisms involving negative feedback controls departures in different directions from the original state, giving a greater degree of control.
- Positive feedback results in greater departures from the original levels. Positive feedback is often associated with a breakdown of control systems, eg in temperature control.

### Explained

**Why keep things stable.**

- *Temperature:* too low and enzymes work slowly (too little kinetic energy); too high and they denature.
- *pH:* changes break ionic and hydrogen bonds in enzymes, changing the active site.
- *Blood glucose:* too low and cells (especially brain cells) lack respiratory substrate; too high and blood water potential falls, so water leaves cells by osmosis.

Homeostasis is a dynamic equilibrium (Edexcel B): values fluctuate around a set point but are kept within limits.

**Components.** A receptor detects a change → a coordinator (often the brain or a gland) → an effector (muscle or gland) responds.

**Negative feedback.** The response reverses the change, bringing the value back to the set point. Having separate mechanisms for a rise and a fall (insulin and glucagon; sweating and shivering) gives finer control than one mechanism that is simply switched on or off.

**Positive feedback.** The response increases the change. Examples: the opening of voltage-gated sodium channels during depolarisation; oxytocin in childbirth (contractions cause more oxytocin, causing stronger contractions); blood clotting. In hypothermia, a low body temperature slows metabolism, so less heat is made and temperature falls further: control breaks down.

### Common mistakes

- Saying negative feedback "keeps things exactly constant".
- Saying positive feedback is "good feedback".

## 3.6.4.2 Control of blood glucose concentration

*Refs: AQA 3.6.4.2, RP11 · OCR A 5.1.4(c) to (e) · OCR B 5.3.2(a) to (c) · Eduqas C3.4(f) (part). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The factors that influence blood glucose concentration.
- The role of the liver in glycogenesis, glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis.
- The action of insulin by: attaching to receptors on the surfaces of target cells; controlling the uptake of glucose by regulating the inclusion of channel proteins in the surface membranes of target cells; activating enzymes involved in the conversion of glucose to glycogen.
- The action of glucagon by: attaching to receptors on the surfaces of target cells; activating enzymes involved in the conversion of glycogen to glucose; activating enzymes involved in the conversion of glycerol and amino acids into glucose.
- The role of adrenaline by: attaching to receptors on the surfaces of target cells; activating enzymes involved in the conversion of glycogen to glucose.
- The second messenger model of adrenaline and glucagon action, involving adenylate cyclase, cyclic AMP (cAMP) and protein kinase.
- The causes of types I and II diabetes and their control by insulin and/or manipulation of the diet.
- Evaluate the positions of health advisers and the food industry in relation to the increased incidence of type II diabetes.

### Explained

**What changes blood glucose.** Rises after eating carbohydrate. Falls with exercise or fasting (glucose used in respiration).

**Three liver processes.**

- **Glycogenesis:** glucose → glycogen (when glucose is high).
- **Glycogenolysis:** glycogen → glucose (when glucose is low).
- **Gluconeogenesis:** making glucose from non-carbohydrates, such as glycerol and amino acids (when glycogen runs low).

**The pancreas.** The islets of Langerhans contain α cells (make glucagon) and β cells (make insulin). The rest of the pancreas (acinar tissue, in clusters around ducts) makes digestive enzymes. In a stained section, islets are lighter-staining patches (OCR A).

**When blood glucose rises:**

1. β cells detect the rise. (OCR A, OCR B: glucose enters β cells and is respired, raising ATP; ATP closes K⁺ channels, depolarising the membrane; voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open; Ca²⁺ entry causes vesicles of insulin to be released by exocytosis.)
2. Insulin binds to specific receptors on liver and muscle cells (and others).
3. Vesicles carrying glucose transporter proteins (GLUT4) fuse with the cell-surface membrane, so more glucose enters by facilitated diffusion.
4. Insulin activates enzymes that convert glucose to glycogen (glycogenesis) and to fat; respiration rate increases.
5. Blood glucose falls; less insulin is released (negative feedback).

**When blood glucose falls:**

1. α cells detect the fall and secrete glucagon.
2. Glucagon binds to receptors on liver cells only (muscle has no glucagon receptors).
3. It activates enzymes for glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis.
4. Glucose is released into the blood; blood glucose rises; less glucagon is secreted.

**Adrenaline**, released by the adrenal glands under stress or exercise, also binds to liver cells and activates glycogenolysis, raising blood glucose to supply muscles.

**Second messenger model** (for adrenaline and glucagon, which cannot enter the cell).

1. The hormone (first messenger) binds to its receptor on the cell-surface membrane.
2. This changes the shape of the receptor, which activates the enzyme adenylate cyclase on the inside.
3. Adenylate cyclase converts ATP into cyclic AMP (cAMP), the second messenger.
4. cAMP activates protein kinase A.
5. Protein kinase A activates, through a cascade, the enzymes that break down glycogen.

The cascade amplifies the signal: one hormone molecule leads to many glucose molecules being released.

**Diabetes.**

| | Type I | Type II |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Autoimmune attack destroys β cells; no insulin made | Receptors on target cells become less responsive to insulin; β cells may also make less |
| Onset | Usually in childhood | Usually in adults; linked to obesity, poor diet, lack of exercise, family history, ethnicity |
| Control | Insulin injections (or pump), matched to diet and exercise; regular blood glucose tests | Diet (less sugar and refined carbohydrate), exercise, weight loss; drugs such as metformin; sometimes insulin |

Insulin cannot be taken by mouth: it is a protein and would be digested.

**Type II and society.** Health advisers recommend less sugar and more exercise, and support measures such as a tax on sugary drinks and clearer labels. The food industry has reduced sugar in some products, but points out that it provides choice and that sweeteners have their own questions; some argue that pressure has been resisted for commercial reasons. When evaluating, look at who funded the evidence and whether claims are supported by data.

### Common mistakes

- Confusing glycogen, glucagon and glycogenesis.
- Saying insulin "turns glucose into glycogen". Insulin activates the enzymes that do this.
- Saying glucagon acts on muscle cells.
- Saying type I diabetes is caused by eating too much sugar.

### Practical

**AQA required practical 11**: production of a dilution series of a glucose solution and use of colorimetric techniques to produce a calibration curve with which to identify the concentration of glucose in an unknown "urine" sample.

1. Make a dilution series of glucose (for example 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 mmol dm⁻³) from a stock solution (file 01 shows the calculation).
2. Add the same volume of Benedict's reagent (in excess) to the same volume of each solution. Heat in a water bath at the same temperature for the same time.
3. Allow to cool; filter or centrifuge out the precipitate if using the remaining blue colour, or use quantitative Benedict's reagent.
4. Zero the colorimeter with distilled water; use a red filter; measure absorbance (or transmission) of each.
5. Plot absorbance against glucose concentration: the calibration curve.
6. Test the unknown samples the same way and read their concentrations from the curve.

Risks: Benedict's reagent is an irritant; hot water bath. Limitations: colorimeter readings are affected by cloudiness; the method only measures reducing sugars; the unknown must lie within the range of standards.

## 3.6.4.3 Control of blood water potential

*Refs: AQA 3.6.4.3 · Edexcel B 9.9 i, iii, iv · OCR A 5.1.2(c), (d) · OCR B 5.3.3(c), (d) · Eduqas C3.4(c) to (g). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The structure of the nephron and its role in: the formation of glomerular filtrate; reabsorption of glucose and water by the proximal convoluted tubule; maintaining a gradient of sodium ions in the medulla by the loop of Henle; reabsorption of water by the distal convoluted tubule and collecting ducts.
- The roles of the hypothalamus, posterior pituitary and antidiuretic hormone (ADH) in osmoregulation.

### Explained

**The kidney.** The renal artery brings blood; the renal vein takes it away; the ureter carries urine to the bladder. Outer cortex; inner medulla; the pelvis where urine collects. Each kidney has about a million nephrons.

**The nephron.**

| Part | Where | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glomerulus and Bowman's (renal) capsule | Cortex | Ultrafiltration |
| Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) | Cortex | Selective reabsorption of glucose, amino acids, most water and ions |
| Loop of Henle | Dips into medulla | Creates a high sodium ion concentration (low water potential) in the medulla |
| Distal convoluted tubule (DCT) | Cortex | Fine-tunes water and ion balance |
| Collecting duct | Cortex to medulla | Water reabsorbed under the control of ADH |

**Ultrafiltration.**

- The afferent arteriole entering the glomerulus is wider than the efferent arteriole leaving it, so hydrostatic pressure in the glomerulus is high.
- Water, glucose, amino acids, urea and ions are forced out through the gaps in the capillary endothelium, through the basement membrane (the actual filter, which holds back proteins) and between the podocytes (cells of the capsule with foot-like processes) into the capsule.
- Blood cells and large proteins stay in the blood. The fluid in the capsule is the glomerular filtrate.

**Proximal convoluted tubule.** All glucose and amino acids, and about 85% of water and much of the sodium, are reabsorbed here.

- Na⁺ is actively pumped out of PCT cells into the blood, keeping Na⁺ low inside the cells.
- Na⁺ diffuses from the filtrate into the cells by co-transport, carrying glucose (or amino acids).
- Glucose diffuses out into the blood by facilitated diffusion.
- Water follows by osmosis.
- (Eduqas) Adaptations of PCT cells: microvilli give a large surface area; many mitochondria supply ATP for active transport; many co-transporter proteins; close to capillaries.

If blood glucose is very high (untreated diabetes), there are not enough co-transporters to reabsorb it all, and glucose appears in urine.

**Loop of Henle (countercurrent multiplier).**

1. In the ascending limb, Na⁺ (and Cl⁻) are actively transported out into the medulla. The ascending limb is impermeable to water, so water cannot follow.
2. This makes the tissue fluid of the medulla have a low water potential.
3. The descending limb is permeable to water. Water leaves it by osmosis into the medulla and is carried away by the blood capillaries (vasa recta).
4. The filtrate becomes more concentrated going down, and less concentrated going up.
5. The deepest part of the medulla has the lowest water potential.
6. The collecting duct passes through this region, so water can be drawn out of it by osmosis.

Animals living in deserts (kangaroo rat, file 30) have longer loops of Henle, giving a steeper gradient and more concentrated urine.

**Distal tubule and collecting duct.** Reabsorption of water depends on how permeable their walls are, which ADH controls.

**Osmoregulation with ADH.**

1. Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect a fall in blood water potential (for example after sweating or eating salty food). Water leaves the osmoreceptor cells by osmosis and they shrink, sending impulses.
2. The hypothalamus makes ADH; it passes down neurones to the posterior pituitary gland, which releases it into the blood.
3. ADH binds to receptors on cells of the DCT and collecting duct.
4. This activates a second messenger pathway: vesicles containing **aquaporins** (water channel proteins) fuse with the cell-surface membrane facing the filtrate.
5. More water is reabsorbed by osmosis into the medulla and blood. A small volume of concentrated urine is produced.
6. Blood water potential rises; less ADH is released (negative feedback).

When blood water potential is too high (after drinking a lot), less ADH is released; fewer aquaporins are in the membranes; less water is reabsorbed; a large volume of dilute urine is produced.

### Worked example

The glomerular filtration rate is 125 cm³ min⁻¹. Urine is produced at 1 cm³ min⁻¹. What percentage of filtrate is reabsorbed?

(125 − 1) ÷ 125 × 100 = 99.2%.

### Common mistakes

- Saying ADH "makes the kidney reabsorb salt".
- Saying the loop of Henle reabsorbs water by active transport. Water always moves by osmosis.
- Saying the descending limb pumps out sodium ions.
- Saying ADH is made in the pituitary. It is made in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary.
- Saying proteins are filtered then reabsorbed. They are too large to be filtered.

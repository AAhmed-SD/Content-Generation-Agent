# Excretion: the liver, the kidney and kidney failure

Excretion removes the waste products of metabolism. This file covers the liver (structure, deamination and the ornithine cycle, detoxification), excretory products in different animals, kidney dissection, the kidney as an endocrine gland, kidney failure and its treatment, and urine tests.

## Overview

A-level only. How the nephron works and ADH are in file 20. AQA does not ask for the liver or kidney failure.

## Excretion and the liver

*Refs: OCR A 5.1.2(a), (b) · OCR B 5.3.3(a), (b) · Edexcel B 9.9 ii. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The term excretion and its importance in maintaining metabolism and homeostasis.
- The structure, mode of action and functions of the mammalian liver; examination and drawing of stained sections of liver tissue (OCR A).
- Deamination and the formation of urea; detoxification.

### Explained

**Excretion** is the removal of waste products of metabolism from the body (not the same as egestion, removing undigested food). Main products: carbon dioxide (from respiration, by the lungs), urea (from excess amino acids, by the kidneys), bile pigments (from breakdown of haemoglobin, in faeces). Wastes must be removed because they are toxic: CO₂ lowers blood pH (as carbonic acid) and reduces haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen; ammonia is very toxic.

**Liver structure.**

- Blood supply: the hepatic artery (oxygenated blood from the aorta) and the hepatic portal vein (deoxygenated blood rich in absorbed food from the gut). Blood leaves through the hepatic vein. Bile leaves through the bile duct to the gall bladder.
- The liver is divided into lobules. Branches of the hepatic artery and portal vein run along the edge of each lobule, and their blood mixes in channels called **sinusoids** that run between rows of liver cells (**hepatocytes**) to a central vein in the middle of the lobule. Central veins join to form the hepatic vein.
- Bile is made by hepatocytes and flows the opposite way in bile canaliculi to the bile duct.
- **Kupffer cells** (macrophages) in the sinusoids break down old red blood cells and engulf bacteria.
- Hepatocytes have many mitochondria, much rough and smooth ER, and Golgi; microvilli face the sinusoids for exchange.

In a stained section (OCR A), lobules look roughly hexagonal, each with a central vein in the middle and branches of the portal vein, hepatic artery and bile duct at the corners.

**Functions.**

- Storage of glycogen; controlling blood glucose (file 20).
- **Deamination:** the amino group of excess amino acids is removed as ammonia, which is very toxic and soluble. The rest of the molecule is respired or converted to glucose or fat.
- **Ornithine cycle:** ammonia is combined with carbon dioxide to make urea, which is less toxic and less soluble: 2NH₃ + CO₂ → CO(NH₂)₂ + H₂O (with energy from ATP). Urea passes into the blood and is filtered by the kidneys.
- **Detoxification:** hepatocytes contain enzymes that make harmful substances harmless. Catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen. Ethanol is broken down by alcohol dehydrogenase to ethanal, then by ethanal dehydrogenase to ethanoate (acetate), which can be used in respiration; the NAD used becomes reduced, so less is available to oxidise fatty acids, and fat builds up (fatty liver, which can lead to cirrhosis). Cytochrome P450 enzymes break down many drugs.
- Making bile, cholesterol and plasma proteins; storing iron and vitamins.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the liver excretes urea. The liver makes it; the kidneys excrete it.
- Saying deamination makes urea directly.
- Confusing the hepatic portal vein (from gut) with the hepatic vein (to heart).

## Excretory products in different animals

*Refs: Edexcel B 9.9 v · Eduqas C3.4(i). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The need for different excretory products in different animals.
- Adaptations of the loop of Henle to the animal's habitat; the kidney of the kangaroo rat (Edexcel B).

### Explained

| Product | Toxicity | Water needed | Who | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ammonia | Very toxic | A lot, to dilute it | Most aquatic animals (bony fish, many invertebrates) | Water is plentiful; ammonia diffuses straight out across gills |
| Urea | Less toxic | Moderate | Mammals, amphibians, sharks | Can be stored and concentrated; costs energy to make |
| Uric acid | Least toxic; almost insoluble | Very little; excreted as a paste | Birds, reptiles, insects | Saves water; eggs with shells cannot get rid of soluble waste, and the embryo is protected from it; light for flight |

**Loop of Henle and habitat.** A longer loop of Henle (relative to the kidney size), and a thicker medulla, create a steeper sodium ion gradient in the medulla, so more water can be drawn from the collecting duct and urine is more concentrated.

- Freshwater animals (beavers) have short loops: they have plenty of water and need to get rid of it.
- Humans have a mixture of short and long loops.
- Desert mammals have very long loops.

**The kangaroo rat** (*Dipodomys*) lives in North American deserts and may never drink. Its adaptations:

- Very long loops of Henle and a thick medulla: urine about four times as concentrated as human urine.
- Much ADH and many aquaporins.
- Gets most water from metabolic water (oxidation of food in respiration) and from seeds.
- Very dry faeces (most water reabsorbed in the colon).
- Lives in a cool, humid burrow by day; active at night; no sweat glands.

## Kidney structure, dissection and the kidney as an endocrine gland

*Refs: OCR A 5.1.2(c) · OCR B 5.3.3(e). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The dissection, examination and drawing of the external and internal structure of the kidney (OCR A).
- The kidney as an endocrine gland: erythropoietin (EPO) and vitamin D (OCR B).

### Explained

**Kidney dissection** (OCR A PAG2; Eduqas specified practical). Identify the renal artery (thick-walled), renal vein (thin-walled, larger lumen) and ureter (white, muscular tube) at the hilum. Cut lengthways through the kidney with a scalpel to see the outer cortex (darker, granular: glomeruli), inner medulla (striped pyramids: loops of Henle and collecting ducts) and the pelvis. Make a labelled drawing. Safety: cut away from the body; gloves; wash hands.

**The kidney as an endocrine gland** (OCR B).

- **Erythropoietin (EPO):** made by the kidney when blood oxygen is low; stimulates bone marrow to make more red blood cells. People with kidney failure often become anaemic; they are given synthetic EPO. (Its misuse in sport is in file 40.)
- **Vitamin D activation:** the kidney converts vitamin D into its active form (calcitriol), which increases calcium absorption from the gut. Kidney failure can lead to weak bones.
- The kidney also releases **renin**, which starts a hormone pathway that raises blood pressure.

## Kidney failure and its treatment

*Refs: OCR A 5.1.2(e) · OCR B 5.3.3(f) to (i) · Eduqas C3.4(h). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The causes, diagnosis and consequences of kidney failure; the estimated glomerular filtration rate.
- Haemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis.
- Kidney transplants; the future of transplant surgery (OCR B).

### Explained

**Causes:** diabetes (high glucose damages glomeruli); high blood pressure (damages glomerular capillaries); infections; autoimmune disease; polycystic kidney disease (inherited); kidney stones blocking the ureter.

**Diagnosis.** Blood tests for creatinine (a breakdown product of creatine in muscle, removed only by the kidneys; high levels mean poor filtration) are used to calculate the **estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)**, adjusted for age, sex and body size. Normal eGFR is above 90 cm³ min⁻¹ per 1.73 m² of body surface; below 15 means kidney failure. Urine tests for protein (albumin) and blood: protein in urine shows the glomeruli are damaged.

**Consequences:** urea and other wastes build up (tiredness, nausea); salt and water imbalance (oedema, high blood pressure); low EPO causes anaemia; weak bones; high potassium can cause heart arrhythmias.

**Haemodialysis.**

1. Blood from an artery (often via a fistula in the arm) passes through a dialyser, then back into a vein.
2. In the dialyser, blood flows on one side of a partially permeable membrane and dialysis fluid on the other, in opposite directions (countercurrent), keeping a gradient along the whole length.
3. Dialysis fluid has the same concentrations of glucose and needed ions as normal plasma, and no urea. Urea and excess ions diffuse out; useful substances are not lost. Water balance is controlled by adjusting the fluid.
4. Heparin is added to stop the blood clotting.

Usually three sessions a week of several hours each, in hospital or at home. Between sessions the patient must restrict salt, potassium, protein and fluid.

**Peritoneal dialysis.** Dialysis fluid is passed through a tube into the abdomen; the peritoneum (the membrane lining the abdomen) acts as the dialysis membrane. After several hours, the fluid is drained and replaced. It can be done at home and while moving about (continuous ambulatory), but there is a risk of infection (peritonitis).

**Transplant.** A kidney from a donor (living relative, or someone who has died) is joined to the patient's blood vessels and bladder; the failed kidneys are usually left in place.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Freedom from dialysis; normal diet | Major surgery with risks |
| Cheaper than dialysis in the long run | Shortage of donors; long waiting lists |
| Better quality of life; EPO and vitamin D made | Immunosuppressant drugs for life, which increase the risk of infection and cancer |
| | Rejection may still happen; the kidney may last 10 to 20 years |

Matching tissue types (antigens) between donor and patient reduces rejection.

**The future of transplant surgery** (OCR B 5.3.3(i)). Opt-out donor systems (in England since 2020, consent is assumed unless a person opts out); xenotransplantation (organs from genetically modified pigs, with genes for pig antigens removed); growing organs from stem cells on a scaffold; 3D-printed tissues; improved immunosuppressants. Ethical issues: consent; fair allocation; animal welfare; the risk of passing animal viruses to humans.

## Using urine in diagnosis

*Refs: OCR A 5.1.2(f). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- How excretory products can be used in medical diagnosis: pregnancy tests and testing for anabolic steroids and drugs.

### Explained

**Pregnancy tests.** The embryo releases human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG), a small glycoprotein that passes into urine. A dipstick uses monoclonal antibodies against hCG (file 07): mobile antibodies with coloured beads bind hCG; the complex is caught by fixed antibodies at the test line; a control line catches unbound mobile antibodies to show the test worked.

**Testing for anabolic steroids and drugs.** Steroids and many drugs (and their breakdown products) are small enough to be filtered and appear in urine. Samples are tested with gas chromatography and mass spectrometry (GC/MS): the sample is vaporised and separated by a gas passing through a column; each substance leaves at a characteristic time and is identified by its mass spectrum. A positive screening result is confirmed with a second sample. Used in sport and by employers and police.

**Glucose in urine** suggests diabetes (file 20); **protein** suggests kidney damage.

### Common mistakes

- Saying dialysis fluid contains no glucose (it contains the normal plasma concentration, so glucose is not lost).
- Saying the dialysis membrane lets proteins through.
- Saying hCG is detected by Benedict's test.

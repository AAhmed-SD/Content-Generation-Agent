# Cardiovascular disease, diet and health risk

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death in the UK. This file covers atherosclerosis, blood clotting, risk factors, diet and energy balance, blood cholesterol, how to judge evidence about health risks, treatments, and Edexcel A core practicals 1 and 2.

## Overview

AS content. The heart and blood vessels are in file 10. Measuring blood pressure is in file 49. AQA asks students to interpret data on CVD risk factors (3.3.4.1); the reasoning skills here apply.

### Board differences

- Edexcel A 1.5 to 1.11, 1.15 to 1.18, CP1, CP2. Edexcel B 4.4 viii, ix. OCR B 2.2.2(e). Eduqas CC1(g).
- Edexcel A treats this as a whole topic (Lifestyle, Health and Risk), including energy budgets, BMI, perception of risk and evaluating studies.

- AQA's equivalent skill (3.3.4.1) is to analyse and interpret data on risk factors and CVD, and evaluate conflicting evidence.

## Atherosclerosis and blood clotting

*Refs: Edexcel A 1.5, 1.6 · Edexcel B 4.4 viii, ix. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The course of events that leads to atherosclerosis: endothelial damage, inflammatory response, plaque formation, raised blood pressure.
- How atherosclerosis can lead to CVD (heart disease, stroke).
- The blood-clotting process: thromboplastin release, prothrombin to thrombin, fibrinogen to fibrin.

### Explained

**Atherosclerosis.**

1. The endothelium (inner lining) of an artery is damaged, for example by high blood pressure, chemicals in tobacco smoke or high LDL.
2. An inflammatory response: white blood cells (macrophages) move into the artery wall and take up LDL cholesterol.
3. Cholesterol, calcium salts and fibrous tissue build up into a hard **plaque (atheroma)** under the endothelium.
4. The lumen narrows and the artery wall becomes less elastic, so blood pressure rises; this causes more damage elsewhere: positive feedback.
5. A plaque may rupture, causing a blood clot (thrombosis).

**Consequences.**

- *Coronary heart disease:* narrowed coronary arteries reduce oxygen to heart muscle (angina, chest pain on exercise). If a clot blocks a coronary artery, heart muscle downstream dies: a **myocardial infarction** (heart attack).
- *Stroke:* a clot blocks an artery to the brain, or a weakened artery bursts.
- *Aneurysm:* an artery wall weakened by plaque bulges and may burst.
- Peripheral artery disease (legs).

**Blood clotting.**

1. Damage to a blood vessel exposes collagen; platelets stick to it and release **thromboplastin** (an enzyme); damaged tissue also releases it.
2. Thromboplastin, with calcium ions and vitamin K, catalyses the conversion of **prothrombin** (a soluble plasma protein) to **thrombin** (an enzyme).
3. Thrombin catalyses the conversion of soluble **fibrinogen** to insoluble **fibrin**.
4. Fibrin forms a mesh of threads that traps platelets and red blood cells, forming a clot.

The cascade amplifies a small signal into a fast response. Clotting inside an intact artery, over a ruptured plaque, is dangerous.

### Common mistakes

- Saying fatty deposits build up "in the lumen" rather than in the artery wall under the endothelium.
- Saying fibrinogen is an enzyme.

## Risk factors, diet and energy balance

*Refs: Edexcel A 1.7, 1.11, 1.15, 1.16 · OCR B 2.2.2(e) · Eduqas CC1(g). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- How factors such as genetics, diet, age, sex, high blood pressure, smoking and inactivity increase the risk of CVD.
- Analyse data on energy budgets and diet; the consequences of energy imbalance; BMI and waist-to-hip ratio as indicators of obesity.
- The possible significance for health of blood cholesterol levels (HDL and LDL); the effects of saturated and unsaturated fats.
- How people use scientific knowledge to reduce their risk.
- Interpret systolic and diastolic blood pressure (OCR B).

### Explained

**Risk factors.**

| Factor | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Genetics | Inherited alleles affect cholesterol levels, blood pressure and artery structure |
| Age | Arteries become less elastic; plaques build up over time |
| Sex | Men are at higher risk before middle age; oestrogen may protect women until the menopause |
| High blood pressure | Damages endothelium; heart works harder |
| Smoking | Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin (less oxygen carried); nicotine raises heart rate and blood pressure and makes platelets stickier; chemicals damage the endothelium |
| Diet | High saturated fat raises LDL; high salt raises blood pressure; high energy intake causes obesity |
| Inactivity | Obesity, high blood pressure, raised LDL |
| Obesity and diabetes | Raised blood pressure, cholesterol and glucose damage arteries |
| Alcohol (heavy use) | Raises blood pressure |

Risk factors that cannot be changed: age, sex, genes. Those that can: diet, smoking, exercise, weight, alcohol.

**Energy balance.** Energy budget = energy in (food) − energy out (basal metabolic rate + activity). If intake exceeds use, the extra is stored as fat and mass increases; if use exceeds intake, mass decreases.

**Indicators of obesity.**

- **BMI** = mass (kg) ÷ height² (m²). 18.5 to 24.9 healthy; 25 to 29.9 overweight; 30 or more obese. Does not distinguish fat from muscle.
- **Waist-to-hip ratio** = waist circumference ÷ hip circumference. Above about 0.9 (men) or 0.85 (women) shows fat stored around the abdomen, which carries more risk.

**Cholesterol and lipoproteins.** Cholesterol is carried in the blood in lipoproteins (lipid plus protein).

- **LDL** (low-density lipoprotein): more lipid, less protein. Carries cholesterol to tissues; high LDL is associated with plaque formation ("bad").
- **HDL** (high-density lipoprotein): more protein. Carries cholesterol from tissues back to the liver for removal; high HDL is associated with lower risk ("good").
- Diets high in **saturated fats** raise LDL; **unsaturated fats** (especially polyunsaturated, in vegetable oils and oily fish) lower LDL and may raise HDL. **Trans fats** (partially hydrogenated oils) are particularly harmful. (Eduqas CC1(g).)

**Blood pressure** (OCR B 2.2.2(e)). Measured with a sphygmomanometer (file 49) in mmHg, as systolic (pressure in arteries when the ventricles contract) over diastolic (pressure when the heart relaxes). Around 120/80 is normal; 140/90 or above is high blood pressure (hypertension); below 90/60 is low. High blood pressure damages arteries, kidneys and the heart, often without symptoms.

**Reducing risk** (Edexcel A 1.16). People use knowledge of risk to make choices: stopping smoking, eating less saturated fat and salt, more fruit, vegetables and fibre, exercising, keeping a healthy weight; taking statins if advised. Knowing the risk does not always change behaviour; habits, cost, culture and how people perceive risk all matter.

### Worked example

1. A person is 1.75 m tall and has a mass of 84 kg. BMI = 84 ÷ 1.75² = 84 ÷ 3.0625 = 27.4 (overweight).

2. A man's daily intake is 11 500 kJ; his basal metabolic rate and activity use 10 300 kJ. Energy surplus = 1200 kJ per day. If 1 kg of fat stores about 37 000 kJ, how long to gain 1 kg? 37 000 ÷ 1200 ≈ 31 days.

## Evaluating evidence about health risks

*Refs: Edexcel A 1.8, 1.9, 1.10. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Analyse quantitative data on illness and mortality rates; distinguish correlation and causation.
- Evaluate the design of studies of health risk factors (sample selection, sample size, controls, validity, reliability).
- Why people's perceptions of risk often differ from the actual risk.

### Explained

**Risk** is the probability of an event, often expressed as a percentage, or as relative risk (how many times more likely in one group than another). Example: if lung cancer death rates are 1 in 1000 per year for non-smokers and 20 in 1000 for smokers, the relative risk is 20.

**Study designs.**

- *Cohort (prospective) studies:* follow a large group over years, recording lifestyle and who develops disease. Can show the order of events, but slow and costly.
- *Case-control (retrospective) studies:* compare people with the disease with similar people without it, looking back at past exposure. Quicker, but relies on memory.
- *Randomised controlled trials:* assign people randomly to treatment or control; best for showing cause, but not ethical for harmful exposures.

**Judging a study.** Is the sample large and representative (age, sex, ethnicity)? Were other variables controlled or matched? Were measurements reliable and objective? Are confidence intervals or statistical tests given? Has it been peer-reviewed and repeated? Who funded it?

**Correlation and cause.** A correlation between, say, coffee drinking and heart disease may be due to a confounding factor (coffee drinkers smoking more). A causal link needs a plausible mechanism and consistent evidence from different types of study.

**Perception of risk** (Edexcel A 1.10). People overestimate risks that are rare but dramatic, unfamiliar, out of their control or widely reported (plane crashes), and underestimate common, familiar risks they choose (driving, smoking, poor diet).

## Treatments for CVD

*Refs: Edexcel A 1.18. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The benefits and risks of treatments for CVD: antihypertensives, statins, anticoagulants and platelet inhibitors.

### Explained

| Treatment | How it works | Benefits | Risks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Antihypertensives (diuretics, beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, calcium channel blockers) | Lower blood pressure: remove more water in urine; reduce heart rate and force; widen arteries | Less damage to endothelium; lower risk of heart attack and stroke | Dizziness, tiredness, cough; some interact with other drugs |
| Statins | Inhibit an enzyme in the liver that makes cholesterol; lower LDL | Reduce plaque formation and heart attacks | Muscle pain; rare liver damage; possible slight increase in diabetes risk |
| Anticoagulants (warfarin, heparin) | Reduce clotting (warfarin blocks vitamin K action) | Prevent clots, strokes | Excessive bleeding; need monitoring |
| Platelet inhibitors (aspirin, clopidogrel) | Make platelets less sticky | Prevent clots forming on plaques | Stomach bleeding; allergic reactions |

Other treatments: angioplasty and stents (widening arteries), bypass surgery, lifestyle changes.

## Edexcel A core practicals 1 and 2

*Refs: Edexcel A 1.17, CP1, CP2. Level: AS & A.*

### Practical

**CP1: the effect of caffeine on the heart rate of *Daphnia*.**

1. Place a water flea (*Daphnia*) in a drop of pond water on a cavity slide, with cotton wool fibres to restrict movement.
2. Let it acclimatise for a few minutes; view under low power; count heartbeats for 15 seconds (using a tally or recording video and slowing it), and multiply by 4.
3. Replace the water with a caffeine solution; wait the same time; count again. Use a range of caffeine concentrations, a fresh *Daphnia* for each, and repeat.

Control variables: temperature (the light of the microscope warms the slide, so limit the time); size and age of *Daphnia*; volume of solution; time in solution. Ethics (Edexcel A 1.17): *Daphnia* are invertebrates with a simple nervous system and are thought to be less aware of pain; they are plentiful; but they should be handled gently, used for as short a time as possible and returned to pond water. Some people object to using any animal.

**CP2: the vitamin C content of food and drink.**

1. DCPIP is a blue dye that vitamin C (ascorbic acid, a reducing agent) turns colourless.
2. Add a fixed volume of DCPIP (1 cm³) to a test tube. Add a known concentration of vitamin C solution drop by drop from a burette or pipette, shaking, until the blue colour just disappears. Record the volume needed.
3. Repeat for a range of known concentrations to make a calibration curve of volume needed against concentration.
4. Repeat with fruit juices; read their concentration from the curve.

Control variables: same volume and concentration of DCPIP; same shaking; judge the end point against a white background. Limitations: coloured juices make the end point hard to see; vitamin C oxidises in air, so use fresh samples.

## Check questions

1. Describe the events that lead to the formation of an atheroma. (4 marks)
2. Explain how atherosclerosis in a coronary artery can cause a heart attack. (3 marks)
3. Describe the role of thrombin in blood clotting. (2 marks)
4. Explain why high LDL and low HDL increase the risk of CVD. (3 marks)
5. Calculate the BMI of a person with mass 68 kg and height 1.62 m, and give one limitation of BMI. (3 marks)
6. Explain why a correlation between a factor and CVD does not show that the factor causes CVD. (2 marks)
7. Give one benefit and one risk of statins and of anticoagulants. (4 marks)
8. In CP1, explain why each *Daphnia* should be left to acclimatise and why the time under the microscope is limited. (2 marks)

## Mark scheme

1. Endothelium damaged (by high blood pressure / smoking) (1); inflammatory response / white blood cells enter wall (1); LDL / cholesterol taken up and builds up (1); with calcium salts / fibrous tissue, forming plaque (1).
2. Plaque narrows lumen / ruptures (1); clot forms and blocks coronary artery (1); heart muscle receives no oxygen / cannot respire aerobically, and dies (1).
3. Thrombin is an enzyme formed from prothrombin (1); converts soluble fibrinogen to insoluble fibrin (1).
4. LDL carries cholesterol to tissues / associated with plaque formation (1); HDL carries cholesterol to the liver for removal (1); so high LDL / low HDL means more cholesterol deposited in arteries (1).
5. 68 ÷ 1.62² = 25.9 (2); limitation: does not distinguish fat from muscle / ignores fat distribution (1).
6. May be due to another (confounding) factor (1); a mechanism / controlled experiments are needed to show cause (1).
7. Statins: benefit lower LDL / fewer heart attacks (1); risk muscle pain / liver damage (1). Anticoagulants: benefit fewer clots / strokes (1); risk excessive bleeding (1).
8. Acclimatise so heart rate is not raised by handling / new conditions (1); microscope lamp warms the water, and temperature affects heart rate (1).

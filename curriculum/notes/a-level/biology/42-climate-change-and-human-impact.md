# Climate change, human impact and sustainability

Human activity is changing climates and ecosystems and driving species to extinction. This file covers the evidence for and causes of climate change, its effects on organisms, why biodiversity matters, how species and ecosystems are conserved and managed sustainably, international agreements, farms as ecosystems, human population growth and food security, and Edexcel A core practical 13.

## Climate change: evidence, causes and models

*Refs: Edexcel A 5.12 to 5.14, 5.18, 5.20, 5.21 · Eduqas C1.5(m). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The different types of evidence for climate change and its causes: records of carbon dioxide concentration (ppm) and temperature, pollen in peat bogs, dendrochronology.
- The causes of anthropogenic climate change: the role of greenhouse gases (CO₂ and methane) in the greenhouse effect.
- That data can be extrapolated to make predictions, and that these are used in models of future climate change; the limitations of models.
- How scientific conclusions about controversial issues can be affected by the people and organisations making them.
- How knowledge of the carbon cycle can be applied to reducing atmospheric CO₂.

### Explained

**Evidence for climate change.**

- *Temperature records:* thermometer records since the 1850s show a rise of about 1.2 °C in global mean temperature. Earlier records were less reliable (fewer places, different methods).
- *Dendrochronology:* trees in temperate areas lay down one growth ring per year; wide rings mean good growing conditions (warm, wet). Ring widths of old trees and timbers give a record of past climate.
- *Pollen in peat bogs:* peat forms in waterlogged, acidic conditions where decomposition is slow, so pollen grains are preserved in layers. Each species' pollen has a distinctive shape. The species present in each layer show the climate when that layer formed (different plants grow in different climates); deeper layers are older (dated by radiocarbon).
- *Ice cores:* bubbles of air trapped in Antarctic and Greenland ice give past CO₂ and methane concentrations; the ratio of oxygen isotopes in the ice gives past temperature. Records go back about 800 000 years and show CO₂ and temperature rising and falling together.
- *CO₂ records:* direct measurements since 1958 (Mauna Loa, Hawaii) show CO₂ rising from about 315 ppm to over 420 ppm, with a seasonal wave (falls in the northern summer when plants photosynthesise more).

**The greenhouse effect.** Short-wave radiation from the Sun passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth's surface, which emits longer-wave infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases (CO₂, methane, water vapour, nitrous oxide) absorb and re-emit some of this infrared, keeping the lower atmosphere warm. Without it the Earth would be about 33 °C colder. Increasing greenhouse gases enhance the effect, so temperatures rise: **anthropogenic** (human-caused) climate change.

- CO₂: burning fossil fuels; deforestation (less photosynthesis; burning trees); cement making.
- Methane: cattle and other ruminants (gut microorganisms); rice paddies; landfill; leaks from gas extraction; melting permafrost releases more (positive feedback). Methane is a much more powerful greenhouse gas per molecule than CO₂ but lasts a shorter time.

**Models and extrapolation.** Scientists extrapolate trends and use computer models that include many factors (emissions, oceans, clouds, ice) to predict future climate under different emission scenarios. Limitations: data from the past may not hold in future; future emissions depend on human choices; some processes (clouds, feedbacks from ice and permafrost) are not fully understood; extrapolating beyond the data range adds uncertainty. Models are tested by checking how well they reproduce past climate.

**Controversy and the scientific community** (Edexcel A 5.18, 5.20). Conclusions about controversial issues can be affected by who funds or publishes the research (oil companies, environmental groups, governments), by selective use of data, or by media reporting. Peer review, publication of methods and data, repetition by independent scientists, and large reviews of evidence (the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, IPCC) increase confidence. Decision-makers balance scientific evidence with economic and social factors.

**Reducing atmospheric CO₂** (Edexcel A 5.21, 5.22).

- Use renewable energy and reduce energy use.
- **Biofuels** (bioethanol from fermenting sugar cane or maize starch; biodiesel from plant oils): carbon released when burned was recently absorbed by the plants, so they are close to carbon-neutral (but growing, processing and transporting them uses energy; they may compete with food crops).
- **Reforestation:** growing trees take up CO₂ by photosynthesis and store carbon in wood. Sustainable forestry (replanting as trees are felled) and protecting peat bogs keep carbon stored.
- Carbon capture and storage.

### Worked example

Atmospheric CO₂ was 340 ppm in 1980 and 412 ppm in 2020. Assuming a linear trend, predict the concentration in 2050. Rate = 72 ÷ 40 = 1.8 ppm per year; 2050: 412 + 30 × 1.8 = 466 ppm. Limitation: the rate of increase has itself been rising, so a linear extrapolation probably underestimates, and future emissions may change.

## Effects of climate change on organisms

*Refs: Edexcel A 5.15, 5.16, CP13 · Edexcel B 10.4 i. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The effects of climate change (changing rainfall patterns and changes in seasonal cycles) on plants and animals (distribution of species, development and life cycles).
- The effect of temperature on enzyme activity and its impact on plants, animals and microorganisms, including Q10.
- Practical: effects of temperature on the development of organisms.

### Explained

- **Distribution:** species shift to cooler areas (towards the poles, or higher up mountains). Species that cannot move or that are already at the top of mountains or on islands may become extinct. Pests and disease vectors (mosquitoes) spread into new areas.
- **Seasonal cycles (phenology):** spring events happen earlier (leaves opening, flowering, egg-laying, insect emergence). If a predator's breeding no longer matches its prey's peak (birds and caterpillars), numbers fall.
- **Rainfall:** droughts and floods change habitats; drought reduces photosynthesis and crop yields.
- **Development:** in ectotherms, development rate depends on temperature; sex of some reptiles (turtles) is set by nest temperature, so warming changes sex ratios.
- **Oceans:** warmer water holds less oxygen; coral bleaching; more CO₂ dissolves, lowering pH (ocean acidification), which harms shell-forming organisms.

**Enzymes and Q10** (Edexcel A 5.16). Within limits, a 10 °C rise roughly doubles enzyme-controlled rates (Q10 ≈ 2; file 02). Warming speeds the metabolism of ectotherms and microorganisms (faster decomposition, releasing more CO₂). Above the optimum, enzymes denature. Rising temperature may favour some species and harm others.

**Evolution in response** (Edexcel A 5.17). Climate change is a selection pressure: individuals with alleles that suit the new conditions are more likely to survive and reproduce, so allele frequencies change. Species with short generations and genetic diversity are more likely to adapt; others may not adapt fast enough.

### Practical

**CP13: investigate the effects of temperature on the development of organisms** (such as seedling growth rate, or brine shrimp hatch rate).

1. Place equal numbers of brine shrimp eggs in equal volumes of salt water at a range of temperatures (water baths or incubators, for example 15, 20, 25, 30 °C).
2. Every few hours count the number of hatched larvae (use a light: larvae swim towards it), or measure seedling height each day.
3. Plot percentage hatched (or growth) against time for each temperature; compare rates; calculate Q10.

Control variables: salt concentration, light, volume, number of eggs, pH. Ethics: brine shrimp are invertebrates, but handle humanely.

## Biodiversity, conservation and sustainable management

*Refs: Edexcel A 4.1, 4.16 · Edexcel B 3.3 ii, iii, 10.4 i to iii · OCR A 4.2.1(f) to (i), 6.3.2(c) to (e) · Eduqas C1.6(a) to (d), (f). Level: AS & A (Edexcel A Topic 4, Edexcel B 3.3, OCR A 4.2.1); A-level only (rest).*

### What you need to know

- The factors affecting biodiversity and why species become endangered or extinct.
- The ecological, economic and aesthetic reasons for maintaining biodiversity.
- In situ and ex situ conservation (zoos, seed banks); international and local agreements.
- How ecosystems are managed to balance conservation with human needs; sustainability.

### Explained

**Why biodiversity is falling** (OCR A 4.2.1(f), Eduqas C1.6(a)).

- **Human population growth** increases demand for land, food, water and energy.
- **Habitat loss and fragmentation:** deforestation, farming, urban development, draining wetlands.
- **Monoculture** farming; pesticides and herbicides.
- **Over-exploitation:** overfishing, hunting, logging, trade in animals and plants.
- **Pollution:** fertilisers (eutrophication), plastics, pesticides.
- **Invasive species** introduced by humans outcompete or prey on native species.
- **Climate change.**

Small populations are vulnerable: low genetic diversity, inbreeding, chance events.

**Reasons to maintain biodiversity** (OCR A 4.2.1(g), Edexcel B 3.3 ii).

- *Ecological:* species depend on each other (pollinators, food webs, nutrient cycling); keystone species have large effects; diverse ecosystems are more stable and recover better from change.
- *Economic (ecosystem services):* food, timber, fibres, fuel; medicines (file 41); genes for crop breeding (wild relatives with disease resistance); pollination of crops; soil formation; water purification; flood control; carbon storage; ecotourism.
- *Aesthetic and ethical:* enjoyment of nature and its benefits to mental health; the view that species have a right to exist and that we have a duty to future generations.

**Conservation and preservation.** Conservation is active management, allowing sustainable use; preservation protects an area from use. In situ and ex situ methods, zoos and seed banks are described in file 23. Eduqas C1.6(b): gene pools are conserved in the wild (protected habitats, wildlife corridors joining fragmented habitats) and in captivity (captive breeding with studbooks; frozen gametes and embryos; seed banks).

**Agreements** (OCR A 4.2.1(i), Edexcel B 10.4 ii).

- **CITES** (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species, 1973): bans international trade in the most endangered species (Appendix I) and controls trade in others with permits. It has reduced trade in, for example, ivory and some parrots, but illegal trade continues where enforcement is weak and demand is high.
- **Convention on Biological Diversity** (Rio, 1992): countries agree to conserve biodiversity, use it sustainably, and share the benefits of genetic resources fairly (including medicines from plants found in developing countries).
- **Countryside Stewardship Scheme** (UK; now Environmental Land Management schemes): pays farmers to manage land to benefit wildlife (hedgerows, field margins, wetlands).

**Sustainable management** (OCR A 6.3.2(d), (e); Edexcel B 10.4 iii). Sustainability means meeting present needs without compromising future generations.

- *Timber:* **coppicing** (cutting trees to near ground level so new shoots grow; harvested in rotation) and **pollarding** (cutting higher, out of reach of grazing animals); selective felling; replanting; leaving buffer zones.
- *Fishing:* quotas; minimum mesh sizes so young fish escape; closed seasons and no-take marine reserves; fish farming (with problems of disease, pollution and wild fish used as feed).

**Managed ecosystems** (OCR A 6.3.2(e)). Each balances human needs with conservation:

- **Galápagos Islands:** invasive goats, rats and plants threaten endemic species; tourism and fishing pressure. Management: national park and marine reserve, eradicating goats, controlling visitors and fishing.
- **Masai Mara** (Kenya): conflict between wildlife and the Maasai people's cattle grazing and farming. Conservancies pay local people from tourism; managed grazing.
- **Terai region** (Nepal and India): forest and grassland with tigers and rhinos; deforestation and conflict with farmers. Community forestry; wildlife corridors.
- **Peak District** and **Snowdonia** (UK): national parks with farming, quarrying, tourism and moorland conservation; managed by grazing, burning and footpath management.
- **Antarctica:** fishing and tourism; protected by the Antarctic Treaty, with no mining and controlled fishing.

**Agriculture versus conservation** (Eduqas C1.6(c)). Intensive farming increases food production but reduces biodiversity (file 14). Compromises include wildlife-friendly farming, integrated pest management and setting aside land.

**Planetary boundaries** (Eduqas C1.6(f)). A framework that sets limits for nine Earth systems within which humanity can safely operate: climate change, biodiversity loss (biosphere integrity), nitrogen and phosphorus cycles, land-system change, freshwater use, ocean acidification, ozone depletion, atmospheric aerosols, and novel entities (such as plastics and chemicals). Several boundaries, including climate change, biodiversity and the nitrogen cycle, are already thought to have been crossed. Political decisions should be informed by evidence (Eduqas C1.6(e)).

## Human population, crops and food security

*Refs: OCR B 4.3.1(l), 4.3.2(a) to (d) · Eduqas C1.6(d). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Farms as ecosystems; the conflict between agriculture and conservation.
- The factors that alter birth and death rates; the effect of human population growth on biodiversity.
- The ecological, economic and scientific reasons for conservation.
- Global food security.

### Explained

**Farms as ecosystems** (OCR B 4.3.1(l)). A farm is an ecosystem managed to channel as much energy as possible into crops and livestock: weeds, pests and competitors are removed and nutrients are added. This conflicts with conservation, because hedgerow removal, monocultures, pesticides, fertilisers and drainage reduce biodiversity (file 14). Compromises: keeping hedgerows and field margins, agri-environment payments, integrated pest management, set-aside land and wildlife-friendly farming. The compensation point and crop yields are in file 15; deflected succession through land management in file 23.

**Population growth** (OCR B 4.3.2(a), (b)). Human population grows when birth rate exceeds death rate. Birth rates fall with better education (especially for women), contraception, lower infant mortality and urbanisation; death rates fall with better food, clean water, sanitation, vaccination and medicine. The world population has grown from about 1 billion in 1800 to over 8 billion. This increases demand for land, water and food, leading to habitat loss, pollution and over-exploitation, reducing biodiversity.

**Food security** (OCR B 4.3.2(d)). Food security exists when everyone has access to enough safe, nutritious food. Threats: growing population; climate change; soil degradation; water shortages; pests and disease; conflict; poverty; waste (about a third of food is wasted). Solutions: higher-yield and more resilient crops (including GM); reducing waste; sustainable farming; fair trade; alternative proteins.

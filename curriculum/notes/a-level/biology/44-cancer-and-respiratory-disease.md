# Cancer and respiratory disease: risk, detection and treatment

This file covers what increases the risk of cancer, how epidemiology links risk factors to disease, how cancers are detected and treated, the effects of smoking on the lungs, chronic bronchitis, emphysema, COPD and asthma, how new medicines are tested in clinical trials, and the role of NICE.

## Cancer: risk, evidence, detection and treatment

*Refs: OCR B 3.3.1(a), (b), (e) to (h). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The factors that may increase the risk of developing cancer.
- The cellular basis of cancer (in outline; detail in file 24).
- Evaluating epidemiological evidence linking risk factors and cancer.
- Methods used to detect cancers; the ethical and economic considerations of screening programmes.
- Methods used to treat cancer.

### Explained

**Cancer** is uncontrolled cell division caused by mutations in genes that control the cell cycle (proto-oncogenes, tumour suppressor genes), producing a malignant tumour that can invade and spread (metastasis).

**Risk factors.**

- *Age:* mutations build up over a lifetime.
- *Genes:* inherited alleles such as BRCA1 and BRCA2 (breast and ovarian cancer).
- *Smoking:* tar contains many carcinogens; linked to lung, mouth, throat, bladder and other cancers.
- *Alcohol:* mouth, throat, liver, breast and bowel cancers.
- *Diet and obesity:* processed and red meat and low fibre (bowel cancer); obesity (several cancers).
- *Radiation:* UV light (skin cancer); ionising radiation; radon gas.
- *Infections:* human papillomavirus (HPV, cervical cancer); hepatitis B and C (liver cancer); *Helicobacter pylori* (stomach cancer).
- *Chemicals:* asbestos (mesothelioma), benzene.
- *Hormones:* oestrogen exposure (breast cancer).

**Epidemiological evidence.** Epidemiology studies patterns of disease in populations. Evidence that smoking causes lung cancer came from large cohort studies of thousands of doctors followed for decades and case-control studies, all showing a strong correlation with a dose-response relationship (more cigarettes, higher risk), and from finding carcinogens in tar and mutations in lung tumours. When evaluating: sample size; matching of groups; confounding factors; whether a dose-response exists; whether results are repeated; whether there is a biological mechanism; who funded the study.

**Detecting cancer.**

- *Screening programmes* (UK, for people with no symptoms): breast (mammography: low-dose X-rays, women aged 50 to 71); cervical (testing cells for HPV; abnormal cells then examined); bowel (home faecal immunochemical test for hidden blood).
- *Imaging:* X-rays, CT, MRI, PET and ultrasound scans locate tumours and show whether they have spread.
- *Biopsy:* a sample of tissue examined under the microscope shows whether cells are cancerous (abnormal shape, large nucleus).
- *Blood tests:* tumour markers such as PSA (prostate-specific antigen), though raised levels have other causes.
- *Genetic testing* for inherited high-risk alleles (BRCA).

**Ethics and economics of screening.** Benefits: early detection, when treatment is more successful and cheaper; lives saved. Concerns: false positives (anxiety, unnecessary tests and treatment); false negatives (false reassurance); overdiagnosis (treating slow cancers that would never have caused harm); radiation risk in some tests; the cost of screening millions of people against the number of cancers found; deciding which age groups to screen; informed consent.

**Treatment.**

| Method | How it works | Drawbacks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Surgery | Removes the tumour and nearby tissue or lymph nodes | Not possible if cancer has spread widely or is in a vital organ |
| Radiotherapy | Ionising radiation damages DNA of cancer cells in a targeted area so they cannot divide | Damages nearby healthy tissue; tiredness, skin damage |
| Chemotherapy | Drugs stop cell division (for example by blocking DNA replication or spindle formation, file 05); reaches cells throughout the body | Affects all rapidly dividing cells: hair loss, nausea, low blood cell counts, infection risk |
| Hormone therapy | Blocks hormones some cancers need (tamoxifen blocks oestrogen receptors in breast cancer) | Menopause-like side effects |
| Targeted therapy and immunotherapy | Monoclonal antibodies against proteins on cancer cells (file 07); drugs that help the immune system recognise cancer cells | Expensive; not effective for all cancers |

Treatments are often combined. Palliative care relieves symptoms when cure is not possible.

## Respiratory disease

*Refs: OCR B 3.3.2(a) to (d). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The short-term and long-term effects of smoking on the lungs.
- The causes and symptoms of chronic bronchitis, emphysema and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).
- The difference between acute and chronic diseases.
- The treatment of asthma.

### Explained

**Short-term effects of smoking.** Carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin (forming carboxyhaemoglobin) more strongly than oxygen, reducing oxygen transport; nicotine raises heart rate and blood pressure and narrows blood vessels; smoke paralyses cilia; more mucus is made; airways narrow.

**Long-term effects.** Lung cancer; COPD; increased risk of CVD (file 38); damage to the airway lining; reduced fitness.

**Chronic bronchitis.** Tar irritates the airways, so goblet cells and mucous glands enlarge and make more mucus; cilia are damaged and cannot remove it. Mucus builds up, causing a persistent cough with phlegm, narrowing of the airways and frequent infections.

**Emphysema.** Smoke causes inflammation; phagocytes enter the alveoli and release elastase, an enzyme that breaks down elastin in the alveolar walls (normally a protective protein inhibits elastase, but smoke reduces it). Walls lose elasticity and break down, forming fewer, larger air spaces with a much smaller surface area. Air is trapped because the lungs do not recoil. Symptoms: breathlessness (especially on exertion), wheezing, a barrel chest, tiredness, bluish skin.

**COPD** is the umbrella term for chronic bronchitis and emphysema together, causing long-term obstruction to airflow. Lung damage cannot be reversed; stopping smoking slows decline. Treatment: inhalers to widen airways, steroids, oxygen therapy, pulmonary rehabilitation. Diagnosed with spirometry (low FEV₁ and FEV₁/FVC ratio, where FVC is forced vital capacity).

**Acute and chronic disease** (OCR B 3.3.2(c)). *Acute* diseases develop rapidly and last a short time (influenza, a cold, an asthma attack). *Chronic* diseases develop slowly and last a long time, often for life (COPD, asthma as a condition, diabetes).

**Asthma.** The airways are inflamed and very sensitive. Triggers (pollen, dust mites, cold air, exercise, smoke) cause the smooth muscle around bronchioles to contract, the lining to swell and extra mucus to be made, narrowing the airways: wheezing, tightness, coughing and breathlessness. Asthma has a genetic component and an allergic (IgE, file 07) component in many people.

Treatment:

- **Relievers** (usually blue inhalers): β₂-agonists such as salbutamol bind to receptors on bronchiole smooth muscle and cause it to relax, widening the airways within minutes.
- **Preventers** (usually brown inhalers): inhaled corticosteroids reduce inflammation over time, making attacks less likely.
- Avoiding triggers; spacers to deliver inhaled drugs; peak flow monitoring; tablets or injections (monoclonal antibodies) in severe asthma.

## Clinical trials and NICE

*Refs: OCR B 3.3.2(e) to (g). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The importance of plants as potential sources of new medicines (file 41).
- The design and use of clinical trials to assess the effectiveness of medicines.
- The role of NICE (the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence).

### Explained

**Clinical trials** (detail of phases in file 41). After pre-clinical testing, a medicine passes through phase 1 (safety in healthy volunteers), phase 2 (effectiveness and dose in patients) and phase 3 (large comparison with existing treatment or placebo). Good design: randomised allocation; double-blind; a control group (placebo or existing treatment); large samples; matched groups; informed consent; ethics committee approval; results published whether positive or negative. Phase 4 monitors safety after licensing.

**NICE** decides whether the NHS in England should use a medicine or treatment, based on both clinical effectiveness and cost-effectiveness. It compares the cost of a treatment with the health gain, measured in QALYs (quality-adjusted life years: one year of life in perfect health = 1 QALY). Treatments that cost more than a threshold (about £20 000 to £30 000 per QALY) are usually not recommended, unless there are special reasons (end-of-life care, rare diseases). NICE also publishes guidelines for good practice. This ensures fair, evidence-based use of limited NHS money, but can mean some effective but expensive drugs are not available, which raises ethical debate.

### Worked example

A new drug costs £18 000 per patient and gives an average of 0.5 extra QALYs. Cost per QALY = 18 000 ÷ 0.5 = £36 000, above the usual NICE threshold, so it would probably not be recommended unless the price were reduced.

### Common mistakes

- Saying smoking causes emphysema by "tar blocking the alveoli".
- Saying relievers reduce inflammation (preventers do).
- Saying screening tests diagnose cancer.

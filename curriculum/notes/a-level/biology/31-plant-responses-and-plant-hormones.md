# Plant responses and plant hormones

Plants respond to light, gravity, touch, water stress and attack by changing how they grow. This file covers plant hormones (auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins, abscisic acid, ethene), the evidence for how auxins and gibberellins work, phytochrome and the control of flowering, and the commercial uses of plant hormones.

## Plant responses and hormones

*Refs: OCR A 5.1.5(a) to (f) · Edexcel A 8.6 · Edexcel B 9.3 i to iii, CP14 · OCR B 4.4.1(d) · Eduqas C2.4(g). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Plants respond to abiotic stress and to herbivory.
- The roles of plant hormones in: leaf loss in deciduous plants; seed germination; stomatal closure.
- Experimental evidence for the role of auxins in the control of apical dominance and of gibberellin in the control of stem elongation and seed germination.
- Practical investigations into the effect of plant hormones on growth.
- Commercial uses of plant hormones.
- (Edexcel B) Plant growth substances can interact: synergism and antagonism.

### Explained

**Why plants respond.** Plants cannot move away from danger, so they change their growth and chemistry. Responses include tropisms (growth towards or away from a directional stimulus: phototropism, gravitropism, hydrotropism, thigmotropism to touch, chemotropism) and rapid responses (nastic movements, such as *Mimosa pudica* leaves folding when touched).

**Responses to herbivory.**

- Chemical defences: tannins (bitter, bind proteins, make leaves hard to digest); alkaloids (nicotine, caffeine: bitter and toxic); pheromones; volatile organic compounds released when attacked that attract predators of the herbivore (parasitic wasps) or warn nearby plants.
- Physical: thorns, hairs; folding leaves.

**Responses to abiotic stress.** Closing stomata in drought (ABA); leaf loss in winter (to avoid water loss and frost damage when light is too low for useful photosynthesis); making antifreeze proteins and sugars in cold.

**Plant hormones (growth substances).** Made in many tissues, transported short distances by diffusion and active transport or long distances in xylem and phloem, and act on target cells with specific receptors. Effects depend on concentration and on other hormones.

| Hormone | Main roles |
| --- | --- |
| Auxins (IAA) | Cell elongation in shoots; apical dominance; root growth at low concentrations; inhibit abscission |
| Gibberellins (GA) | Stem elongation (between nodes); seed germination; fruit growth; bolting |
| Cytokinins | Cell division; delay leaf senescence; promote side shoot growth |
| Abscisic acid (ABA) | Stomatal closure; seed dormancy; inhibits growth under stress |
| Ethene | Fruit ripening; promotes abscission (leaf and fruit fall) |

**How auxins cause elongation (acid growth hypothesis).** IAA binds to receptors on cells, stimulating proton pumps that move H⁺ into the cell wall. The low pH activates proteins (expansins) that break bonds between cellulose microfibrils, loosening the wall. Water enters the cell by osmosis and the cell elongates. Later the wall stiffens again.

**Apical dominance.** Auxin made by the shoot tip moves down the stem and inhibits the growth of lateral (side) buds. Evidence:

- Remove the apical bud: side shoots grow.
- Remove the tip and put auxin paste (in lanolin) on the cut surface: side shoots stay suppressed. Lanolin alone does not suppress them (control).
- Apply an auxin transport inhibitor below the tip: side shoots grow.
- Later work showed cytokinins promote side shoot growth and that ABA in side buds keeps them dormant; auxin may act partly by lowering cytokinins. So apical dominance involves several hormones.

Gardeners use this: pruning the tips makes plants bushier.

**Gibberellins and stem elongation.** Evidence:

- Rice infected with the fungus *Gibberella fujikuroi* grew very tall ("foolish seedling disease"); the substance from the fungus (gibberellin) did the same when applied to healthy plants.
- Dwarf varieties of peas or maize have low gibberellin (a mutation in a gene for an enzyme that makes active GA); applying gibberellin makes them grow tall. Tall varieties do not respond as much.
- Measurements show high gibberellin concentrations in young leaves and seeds, where growth is fastest.

**Gibberellins and germination.**

1. The seed absorbs water (imbibition); the embryo is activated and makes gibberellin.
2. Gibberellin diffuses to the aleurone layer (a protein-rich layer around the endosperm in cereals).
3. It switches on genes for amylase (gibberellin causes breakdown of DELLA proteins that otherwise block transcription factors).
4. Amylase hydrolyses starch in the endosperm to maltose and glucose.
5. The sugars are respired by the embryo for energy and used to build new cells, so the embryo grows.

Evidence: seeds of mutant plants that cannot make gibberellin do not germinate unless gibberellin is supplied; inhibitors of gibberellin synthesis stop germination; ABA (which keeps seeds dormant) antagonises gibberellin.

**Leaf loss (abscission).** As days shorten in autumn, auxin from leaves falls. The leaf becomes less sensitive to ethene and more ethene is made. Ethene stimulates genes for cellulases, which break down cell walls in the abscission zone at the base of the leaf stalk. A layer of suberin seals the wound before the leaf falls.

**Stomatal closure.** In drought, roots and leaves make ABA. ABA binds to receptors on guard cells, causing calcium ions to enter; this opens channels so K⁺ (and Cl⁻) leave. The water potential of the guard cells rises, water leaves by osmosis, guard cells become flaccid and stomata close.

**Interactions** (Edexcel B 9.3 iii). *Synergism:* two hormones together have a bigger effect than either alone (auxin and gibberellin together cause more stem growth). *Antagonism:* two hormones have opposite effects (gibberellin promotes germination; ABA inhibits it; auxin inhibits side shoots while cytokinin promotes them).

### Common mistakes

- Saying auxins make cells divide (they make cells elongate).
- Saying gibberellin itself breaks down starch.
- Saying ABA opens stomata.

### Practical

**Gibberellin and amylase in germinating cereals** (Edexcel B CP14; Eduqas starch agar practical).

1. Cut barley grains in half; keep the halves without embryos (so no natural gibberellin). Sterilise their surfaces.
2. Soak in a range of gibberellin concentrations (and water as a control).
3. Place cut side down on starch agar plates; incubate for 48 hours.
4. Flood with iodine solution. Clear zones show where starch was hydrolysed by amylase.
5. Measure the area of the clear zones; larger zones show more amylase.

Halves with embryos act as a positive control. Aseptic technique prevents bacterial amylase giving false results.

**Effect of auxin on growth** (OCR A 5.1.5(e)). Grow coleoptiles, stem segments or cuttings in a range of auxin concentrations and measure growth or root number. Use the same length segments, the same time, the same temperature and light.

## Phytochrome, photomorphogenesis and flowering

*Refs: Edexcel A 8.6 · Edexcel B 9.3 iv · OCR B 4.4.1(a). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- How phytochrome controls flowering, germination and photomorphogenesis.
- (OCR B) Vernalisation and the timing of flowering.

### Explained

**Phytochrome** is a light-sensitive pigment (a protein with a light-absorbing part) found in two interconvertible forms.

- Pr absorbs red light (660 nm) and is converted to Pfr.
- Pfr absorbs far-red light (730 nm) and is converted to Pr.
- In darkness, Pfr slowly reverts to Pr.

Sunlight contains more red than far-red, so in daylight Pr becomes Pfr; at night Pfr slowly becomes Pr. The ratio of Pfr to Pr tells the plant how long the night is.

Pfr is the active form. It moves into the nucleus and binds to transcription factors (it interacts with proteins that otherwise block them), switching genes on or off.

**Flowering.** Plants flower at the right time of year so that pollinators are active and seeds develop in suitable conditions. The critical factor is the length of uninterrupted darkness.

- *Long-day plants* (flower in summer, short nights): flower when Pfr stays high, because short nights do not convert enough back to Pr. A flash of red light in the middle of a long night makes them flower.
- *Short-day plants* (flower in autumn or spring, long nights): flower when Pfr falls low during a long night. A flash of red light in the middle of the night (converting Pr to Pfr) stops flowering; a flash of far-red afterwards reverses that.

Pfr, made in leaves, leads to production of a flowering signal (florigen, a protein) that travels in the phloem to the buds.

**Vernalisation** (OCR B). Some plants (winter wheat, many biennials) flower only after a long period of cold. Cold switches off (epigenetically) a gene that blocks flowering, so the plant flowers in spring, not before winter.

**Germination.** Some small seeds germinate only in light: red light converts Pr to Pfr, which stimulates gibberellin synthesis. This stops small seeds germinating deep in soil where they would use up their food before reaching light. Under a leaf canopy, light is rich in far-red (leaves absorb red), so Pfr stays low and seeds stay dormant until a gap opens.

**Photomorphogenesis.** The development of a plant's form in response to light. A seedling growing in the dark is *etiolated*: long, thin stem; small, yellow leaves; hooked tip. This uses its stored food to reach light quickly. When exposed to red light, Pfr forms and the seedling de-etiolates: stem elongation slows, leaves expand, chlorophyll forms.

**Phototropism and IAA** (Edexcel A 8.6). Blue light, detected by a different pigment (phototropin), causes IAA to move to the shaded side of the shoot (file 18), so the shaded side elongates more.

### Common mistakes

- Saying short-day plants respond to the length of the day; it is the length of the uninterrupted night.
- Saying Pr is the active form.

## Commercial uses of plant hormones

*Refs: OCR A 5.1.5(f). Level: A-level only.*

### Explained

| Hormone | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Auxins | Rooting powders for cuttings; selective weedkillers (synthetic auxins make broad-leaved weeds grow too fast and die, while narrow-leaved cereals are less affected); seedless fruit (parthenocarpy) by spraying unpollinated flowers; preventing fruit drop |
| Gibberellins | Larger, longer seedless grapes; malting in brewing (speeds amylase production in barley); delaying ripening of citrus fruit; more sugar cane yield (longer internodes); seed germination |
| Ethene | Ripening bananas and other fruit picked unripe for transport; promoting fruit drop for harvesting (cherries); promoting lateral growth |
| Cytokinins | Tissue culture (shoot formation, file 29); delaying senescence of lettuce |
| Inhibitors of ethene / gibberellin | Storing fruit longer (low oxygen, high CO₂); dwarf cereals that do not fall over (lodge) in wind and rain |

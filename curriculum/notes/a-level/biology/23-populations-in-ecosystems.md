# Populations in ecosystems, succession and conservation

Populations of different species live together in ecosystems, and their sizes are set by biotic and abiotic factors. This file covers carrying capacity, competition and predation, estimating population size, succession, and conservation, with AQA required practical 12.

## 3.7.4 Populations in ecosystems

*Refs: AQA 3.7.4, RP12 · Edexcel A 4.3, 4.16, 5.1 to 5.4, CP10 · Edexcel B 3.3 ii, iii, 10.1 i, iv, v, 10.3 i, ii, CP15, CP16 · OCR A 4.2.1(b), (h), 6.3.1(a), (d), (e), 6.3.2(a) to (c) · OCR B 4.3.1(m) · Eduqas C1.5(a) to (e), (g), (i), (j), C1.6(b). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Populations of different species form a community. A community and the non-living components of its environment together form an ecosystem. Ecosystems can range in size from the very small to the very large.
- Within a habitat, a species occupies a niche governed by adaptation to both biotic and abiotic conditions.
- An ecosystem supports a certain size of population of a species, called the carrying capacity. This population size can vary as a result of: the effect of abiotic factors; interactions between organisms: interspecific and intraspecific competition and predation.
- The size of a population can be estimated using: randomly placed quadrats, or quadrats along a belt transect, for slow-moving or non-motile organisms; the mark-release-recapture method for motile organisms. The assumptions made when using the mark-release-recapture method.
- Ecosystems are dynamic and are subject to change over time. The concept of succession from pioneer species to climax community. At each stage in succession, certain species may be recognised that change the environment so that it becomes more suitable for other species with different adaptations. The new species may change the environment in such a way that it becomes less suitable for the previous species. Changes that organisms produce in their abiotic environment can result in a less hostile environment and change biodiversity.
- Conservation of habitats frequently involves management of succession.
- Evaluate evidence and data concerning issues relating to the conservation of species and habitats and consider conflicting evidence. Use given data to calculate the size of a population estimated using the mark-release-recapture method.

### Explained

**Terms.**

- *Population:* all the organisms of one species in an area at one time.
- *Community:* all the populations of different species in an area.
- *Ecosystem:* a community plus the abiotic (non-living) parts of its environment.
- *Habitat:* the place where an organism lives.
- *Niche:* the role of a species in its habitat, including its interactions with other species and the abiotic conditions it needs. Two species cannot occupy the same niche in the same habitat indefinitely.

**Carrying capacity.** The largest population an environment can support long term. A population introduced to a new area grows slowly at first (lag phase: few individuals; adjusting), then rapidly (log or exponential phase: plenty of resources), then levels off (stationary phase) as resources, predators or disease limit it around the carrying capacity. (Eduqas, OCR A.)

**Abiotic factors:** temperature, light, pH, water availability, mineral ions, oxygen, wind, soil type. Each species has an optimum range.

**Biotic factors.**

- *Intraspecific competition:* within a species. When resources are short, the population falls; with less competition it rises again; numbers fluctuate around the carrying capacity.
- *Interspecific competition:* between species for the same resource. If niches overlap, one species may outcompete the other (red squirrels replaced by grey squirrels in most of the UK).
- *Predation:* predator and prey populations cycle. Prey increase → more food for predators, so predators increase → prey eaten, so prey decrease → predators starve, so predators decrease → prey recover. Predator peaks lag behind prey peaks. Real cycles are also affected by food supply and disease.

**Density-dependent and density-independent factors** (Eduqas). Density-dependent factors have a bigger effect as the population gets denser: competition, predation, disease, parasitism. Density-independent factors affect a population whatever its density: floods, fire, extreme cold.

**Estimating population size.**

*Quadrats* (for plants and slow-moving animals):

1. Use random coordinates (two tape measures at right angles; random number generator) to place quadrats.
2. Record abundance in each: count individuals (density), estimate percentage cover, or record presence in a point frame or gridded quadrat (frequency).
3. Use enough quadrats that the running mean levels off.
4. Population estimate = mean number per quadrat × (area of habitat ÷ area of one quadrat).

*Transects* (when conditions change across an area, such as from a hedge into a field, or down a seashore):

- Line transect: record species touching the line at regular intervals.
- Belt transect: place quadrats at regular intervals along the line (continuous or interrupted).

*Mark-release-recapture* (for mobile animals):

1. Capture a sample, count them (M), mark them harmlessly (paint dot under the shell), release.
2. After time to mix back in, capture a second sample; count the total (C) and how many are marked (R).
3. Population estimate N = (M × C) ÷ R.

Assumptions: marked individuals mix fully back into the population; the mark does not affect survival (not more visible to predators) and does not rub off; no births, deaths, immigration or emigration between samples; the population is in a defined area.

**Sampling methods and techniques** (Edexcel B 10.1 iv, v, CP15; OCR A 4.2.1(b)). Choose the method for the organism and habitat: kick sampling and nets for freshwater invertebrates; pitfall traps for ground insects; pooters; sweep nets; Tullgren funnels for soil invertebrates; light traps for moths. Measure abiotic factors with light meters, thermometers, pH probes, oxygen probes. Random sampling avoids bias; systematic sampling along a transect shows patterns; stratified sampling covers each part of a habitat in proportion. More samples give a more representative estimate.

**Succession.** The change in a community over time.

*Primary succession* starts on bare ground with no soil (bare rock, sand dunes, cooled lava):

1. **Pioneer species** colonise: lichens, mosses, algae. Adaptations: spores or seeds spread by wind, tolerance of extreme conditions, ability to fix nitrogen, rapid germination, photosynthesis.
2. Pioneers break down rock and, when they die and decompose, form a thin soil with some organic matter (humus).
3. Grasses and small plants can now grow. The soil deepens, holds more water and more nutrients.
4. Shrubs, then trees, colonise. Each stage changes the abiotic environment (more shade, deeper soil, more shelter) in ways that suit new species and may make it less suitable for the previous ones, which are outcompeted.
5. A stable **climax community** forms (in the UK, usually deciduous woodland, such as oak).

As succession proceeds: biomass increases; the number of species (biodiversity) usually increases; conditions become less hostile; food webs become more complex.

*Secondary succession* starts where soil is already present but vegetation has been removed (after fire, ploughing or felling). It is faster.

*Deflected succession / plagioclimax:* human activity (grazing, mowing, burning) stops succession at an earlier stage. Heather moorland is kept by controlled burning; chalk grassland by sheep grazing.

**Conservation.**

- *Conservation* means active management to maintain or increase biodiversity, and allows sustainable human use. *Preservation* (OCR A) means protecting an area from any human interference.
- *Managing succession:* conserving a habitat at an early stage (heathland, meadow) means preventing the climax: grazing, mowing, cutting back scrub, controlled burning.
- *In situ conservation:* protecting species in their natural habitat — national parks, nature reserves, marine conservation zones, controlling invasive species, legal protection.
- *Ex situ conservation:* outside the natural habitat — zoos with captive breeding programmes (keeping studbooks to avoid inbreeding, reintroducing animals to the wild), botanic gardens and seed banks (seeds dried and frozen; tested regularly for viability; such as the Millennium Seed Bank). Advantages: protects species from immediate threats; seeds store many alleles in little space. Disadvantages: small captive populations lose genetic diversity; animals may lose natural behaviours; reintroduction fails if the habitat is still damaged; expensive.

**Conflicting evidence.** Conservation decisions balance biological evidence with economic and social needs. When evaluating data, consider sample size, how the data were collected, whether a correlation shows cause, and who funded or published the study.

### Worked example

1. In a first sample, 40 woodlice were caught and marked. In the second sample, 50 were caught, 8 of them marked. Estimate the population.

N = (40 × 50) ÷ 8 = 250.

2. Ten 0.25 m² quadrats in a 200 m² meadow contained a total of 36 daisies. Estimate the number of daisies in the meadow.

Mean per quadrat = 3.6; per m² = 3.6 ÷ 0.25 = 14.4; total = 14.4 × 200 = 2880.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the climax community "is reached when no more species can live there".
- Saying pioneers "make soil" without saying they break down rock and decompose to add organic matter.
- Mixing up population, community and ecosystem.
- In mark-release-recapture, assuming marking "has no effect" without saying what effects it must not have.
- Placing quadrats where there is "the most" of a plant.

### Practical

**AQA required practical 12** (also Edexcel A CP10, Edexcel B CP16, OCR A PAG3 and 6.3.1(e)): investigation into the effect of a named environmental factor on the distribution of a given species.

Example: the effect of light intensity on the distribution of ivy from the edge of a wood into its interior.

1. Lay a tape measure from the open field into the wood (a belt transect).
2. Place a quadrat at regular intervals (every 2 m).
3. In each, estimate percentage cover of ivy and measure light intensity with a light meter at the same height and time of day.
4. Repeat with several transects.
5. Plot percentage cover against light intensity and use Spearman's rank correlation coefficient to test for a correlation (file 26).

Control variables: same time of day; same weather; same person estimating cover. Risks: uneven ground, stinging plants, ticks; work in pairs; wash hands. Limitations: other factors (soil moisture, pH) also vary along the transect, so a correlation does not show light is the cause.

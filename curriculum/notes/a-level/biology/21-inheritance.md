# Inheritance

Genetic crosses predict the offspring of parents with known genotypes. This file covers monohybrid and dihybrid crosses, codominance, multiple alleles, sex linkage, autosomal linkage, epistasis, polygenic inheritance and the chi-squared test.

## 3.7.1 Inheritance

*Refs: AQA 3.7.1 · Edexcel A 2.13, 3.8, 3.15 · Edexcel B 8.2 i to vi · OCR A 6.1.2(a) to (d) · OCR B 5.1.1(a), (c) to (f) · Eduqas C2.5(a) to (e). Level: A-level only (AS for Edexcel A).*

### What you need to know

- The genotype is the genetic constitution of an organism. The phenotype is the expression of this genetic constitution and its interaction with the environment.
- There may be many alleles of a single gene. Alleles may be dominant, recessive or codominant.
- In a diploid organism, the alleles at a specific locus may be either homozygous or heterozygous.
- The use of fully labelled genetic diagrams to interpret, or predict, the results of: monohybrid and dihybrid crosses involving dominant, recessive and codominant alleles; crosses involving sex-linkage, autosomal linkage, multiple alleles and epistasis.
- The use of the chi-squared (χ²) test to compare the goodness of fit of observed phenotypic ratios with expected ratios.

### Explained

**Terms.**

- *Gene:* a length of DNA at a locus that codes for a polypeptide.
- *Allele:* a different form of the same gene.
- *Dominant:* always expressed in the phenotype when present (capital letter).
- *Recessive:* expressed only when two copies are present (lower case).
- *Codominant:* both alleles are expressed in the heterozygote.
- *Homozygous:* two identical alleles (AA or aa). *Heterozygous:* two different alleles (Aa).

**Monohybrid cross.** One gene. Two heterozygotes (Aa × Aa) give offspring genotypes 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa, and phenotypes 3 dominant : 1 recessive.

A full genetic diagram has: parental phenotypes; parental genotypes; gametes (circled); a Punnett square; offspring genotypes; offspring phenotypes; ratio.

*Test cross:* cross an organism with the dominant phenotype with a homozygous recessive. If any offspring show the recessive phenotype, the parent was heterozygous.

**Codominance.** In snapdragons, C^R C^R gives red, C^W C^W white, C^R C^W pink. A cross of two pink gives 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white. Human example: the sickle cell alleles (Hb^A Hb^S gives sickle cell trait).

**Multiple alleles.** Human ABO blood groups: one gene with three alleles, I^A, I^B and I^O. I^A and I^B are codominant; both are dominant to I^O.

| Genotype | Blood group |
| --- | --- |
| I^A I^A, I^A I^O | A |
| I^B I^B, I^B I^O | B |
| I^A I^B | AB |
| I^O I^O | O |

A parent with group A (I^A I^O) and a parent with group B (I^B I^O) can have children of all four groups.

**Dihybrid cross.** Two genes on different chromosomes. AaBb × AaBb: each parent makes four kinds of gamete (AB, Ab, aB, ab) in equal numbers because of independent segregation. Offspring phenotypes: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.

**Sex linkage.** Genes on the X chromosome. Females are XX; males XY. The Y chromosome is small and carries few of the genes on the X, so males have only one allele of X-linked genes and express it, even if recessive. Example: haemophilia (a recessive allele, X^h, for a missing clotting factor).

- Carrier mother (X^H X^h) × normal father (X^H Y): daughters X^H X^H or X^H X^h (none affected, half carriers); sons X^H Y or X^h Y (half affected).
- Males inherit their X from their mother, so a father cannot pass an X-linked allele to his sons.
- Duchenne muscular dystrophy (Eduqas) and red-green colour blindness are other X-linked recessive conditions.

**Autosomal linkage.** Genes on the same autosome are linked: they tend to be inherited together, because they move together into gametes. A dihybrid cross then does not give 9:3:3:1. Parental combinations appear more often than expected. New (recombinant) combinations appear only when crossing over separates the alleles; the further apart the genes are on the chromosome, the more often crossing over separates them.

*Recombination frequency* = number of recombinant offspring ÷ total offspring.

**Epistasis.** The allele of one gene affects the expression of another gene. Often one gene controls an enzyme early in a pathway:

- *Recessive epistasis:* homozygous recessive at gene A (aa) masks gene B. In some mammals, aa means no pigment at all (white), whatever the colour alleles at B. AaBb × AaBb gives 9 : 3 : 4.
- *Dominant epistasis:* a dominant allele at gene A masks gene B. AaBb × AaBb gives 12 : 3 : 1 (or 13 : 3).

**Polygenic inheritance** (Edexcel A, OCR A). Many genes, each with a small additive effect, affect one characteristic (height, skin colour). With the environment, this gives continuous variation and a normal distribution. Discontinuous variation is usually one gene with distinct phenotypes.

**Environment** (OCR A). Phenotype = genotype + environment. Plants grown in the dark are tall, thin and yellow (etiolation); plants short of magnesium are yellow (chlorosis), even if their genes for chlorophyll are normal.

**Pedigree diagrams** (Edexcel A, Edexcel B). Squares are males, circles females; shaded symbols are affected. If two unaffected parents have an affected child, the condition is recessive and both parents are carriers. If an affected child always has an affected parent, it is likely dominant.

**Model organisms** (OCR B). Fruit flies (*Drosophila*), mice, peas and *Arabidopsis* are used to study inheritance because they breed quickly, produce many offspring, are cheap and easy to keep, have visible variation, and their genomes are known. Results often apply to humans because genes and processes are shared.

**The chi-squared test.** Tests whether the difference between observed (O) and expected (E) results is due to chance.

χ² = Σ (O − E)² ÷ E

1. Null hypothesis: there is no significant difference between observed and expected results.
2. Calculate expected numbers from the ratio and the total.
3. Calculate χ².
4. Degrees of freedom = number of categories − 1.
5. Compare with the critical value at p = 0.05.
6. If χ² is less than the critical value, accept the null hypothesis: any difference is due to chance. If greater, reject it: the difference is significant (perhaps the genes are linked, or there is epistasis).

### Worked example

A dihybrid cross in peas gave: round yellow 296, round green 104, wrinkled yellow 92, wrinkled green 28. Total = 520. Expected ratio 9:3:3:1.

1. Expected: 520 × 9/16 = 292.5; 520 × 3/16 = 97.5; 97.5; 520 × 1/16 = 32.5.
2. (O − E)²/E:
   - (296 − 292.5)² ÷ 292.5 = 12.25 ÷ 292.5 = 0.042
   - (104 − 97.5)² ÷ 97.5 = 42.25 ÷ 97.5 = 0.433
   - (92 − 97.5)² ÷ 97.5 = 30.25 ÷ 97.5 = 0.310
   - (28 − 32.5)² ÷ 32.5 = 20.25 ÷ 32.5 = 0.623
3. χ² = 1.41.
4. Degrees of freedom = 3. Critical value at p = 0.05 is 7.82.
5. 1.41 < 7.82, so accept the null hypothesis. The results fit a 9:3:3:1 ratio; the difference is due to chance.

Sex linkage example: a colour-blind man (X^b Y) and a woman with normal vision whose father was colour-blind (X^B X^b). Probability that their next child is a colour-blind son: gametes X^B, X^b from mother; X^b, Y from father. Children: X^B X^b, X^B Y, X^b X^b, X^b Y. Colour-blind son = X^b Y = 1/4.

### Common mistakes

- Writing gametes with two alleles of the same gene (Aa) or omitting the circle convention.
- Writing sex-linked alleles without the X (just "h") or putting alleles on the Y.
- Saying a 3:1 ratio means "exactly 3 out of every 4". It is a probability.
- Concluding the null hypothesis is "proved". It is accepted or rejected.
- Using the wrong degrees of freedom (number of categories − 1, not number of individuals).
- Saying linked genes "are on different chromosomes".

## Check questions

1. Define the terms genotype and codominant. (2 marks)
2. In cats, the allele for orange fur (X^O) and the allele for black fur (X^o) are codominant and X-linked; heterozygous females are tortoiseshell. Explain why male tortoiseshell cats are very rare. (2 marks)
3. Two parents with blood groups A and B have a child with group O. Give the parents' genotypes and the probability that their next child is group AB. (3 marks)
4. A dihybrid cross between two heterozygotes gave far more offspring with the parental combinations than expected. Suggest and explain why. (3 marks)
5. In a species, gene C controls pigment production (cc = no pigment, white) and gene B gives black (B) or brown (bb). Predict the phenotype ratio from CcBb × CcBb. (3 marks)
6. A monohybrid cross gave 72 dominant and 32 recessive. Use chi-squared to test whether this fits 3:1 (critical value 3.84 at 1 degree of freedom, p = 0.05). (4 marks)
7. Explain why a father cannot pass haemophilia to his son. (2 marks)
8. Give three features that make *Drosophila* a useful model organism in genetics. (3 marks)

1. Genotype: alleles an organism has / genetic constitution (1); codominant: both alleles expressed in the heterozygote (1).
2. Tortoiseshell needs both X^O and X^o / two X chromosomes (1); males have only one X / XY (1).
3. I^A I^O and I^B I^O (1); genetic diagram / four outcomes (1); probability of AB = 1/4 (1).
4. Genes linked / on the same chromosome (1); alleles inherited together (1); recombinants only from crossing over, which is infrequent (1).
5. Diagram or reasoning (1); 9 black (C_B_) : 3 brown (C_bb) (1) : 4 white (cc__) (1).
6. Total 104; expected 78 and 26 (1); χ² = (72 − 78)²/78 + (32 − 26)²/26 = 0.46 + 1.38 = 1.85 (1); less than 3.84 (1); accept null hypothesis: difference due to chance, fits 3:1 (1).
7. Haemophilia allele on X chromosome (1); father gives son a Y chromosome (1).
8. Any three: short life cycle / breed quickly; many offspring; cheap / easy to keep; visible mutations / variation; small genome / sequenced; few chromosomes.

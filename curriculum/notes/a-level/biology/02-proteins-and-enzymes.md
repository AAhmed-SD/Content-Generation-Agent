# Proteins and enzymes

Proteins do most of the work in cells. This file covers amino acids, how they join, the four levels of protein structure, and how enzymes work and what changes their rate. It includes AQA required practical 1.

## 3.1.4.1 General properties of proteins

*Refs: AQA 3.1.4.1 · Edexcel A 2.9 · Edexcel B 1.3 i to v · OCR A 2.1.2(k) to (o), (q), (s) · OCR B 2.1.3(a), (b) · Eduqas CC1(h) to (j). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Amino acids are the monomers of proteins. General structure: a central carbon joined to an amine group (–NH₂), a carboxyl group (–COOH), a hydrogen atom and an R group (side chain).
- The 20 amino acids common to all organisms differ only in their R groups.
- Two amino acids join by condensation to form a peptide bond, making a dipeptide. Many make a polypeptide. A functional protein may contain one or more polypeptides.
- Hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and disulfide bridges hold proteins in shape.
- Primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure, and how structure relates to function.
- The biuret test for proteins.

### Explained

**Peptide bond.** The OH from the carboxyl group of one amino acid and an H from the amine group of the next form water. The C of one joins to the N of the other: –CO–NH–.

**Levels of structure.**

| Level | What it is | Bonds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | The sequence of amino acids in the polypeptide | Peptide bonds |
| Secondary | Local folding into α-helix or β-pleated sheet | Hydrogen bonds between the –NH and –C=O of the backbone |
| Tertiary | The polypeptide folds further into a precise 3D shape | Hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges between R groups (and hydrophobic interactions) |
| Quaternary | More than one polypeptide (sometimes with a prosthetic group) joined together | The same bonds as tertiary |

The primary structure decides everything else. Change one amino acid and the R groups that meet may differ, bonds form in different places, and the 3D shape changes. That is why a mutation can stop a protein working.

Disulfide bridges form between two cysteine R groups and are strong covalent bonds. Ionic and hydrogen bonds are weaker and broken by high temperature and extreme pH.

**Globular and fibrous proteins** (OCR A, OCR B, Edexcel B, Eduqas):

| | Globular | Fibrous |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | Compact, roughly spherical | Long strands |
| Solubility | Soluble: hydrophilic R groups face outwards | Insoluble |
| Role | Metabolic: enzymes, hormones, transport | Structural |
| Examples | Haemoglobin (four polypeptides, each with an iron-containing haem prosthetic group, carries oxygen); insulin (hormone); enzymes | Collagen (three chains wound into a triple helix, cross-linked, high tensile strength in tendons, skin, bone); keratin (many disulfide bridges, in hair and nails); elastin (stretches and recoils in artery walls and alveoli) |

**Biuret test.** Add biuret reagent (sodium hydroxide then dilute copper(II) sulfate). Blue turns lilac/purple if peptide bonds are present.

**Chromatography** (OCR A). Spot the mixture on a thin layer plate, stand in solvent, let the solvent run, then locate spots (ninhydrin for amino acids). Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent. Compare Rf values with known standards run in the same conditions.

### Worked example

An amino acid spot moves 3.2 cm; the solvent front moves 8.0 cm. Find Rf.

Rf = 3.2 ÷ 8.0 = 0.40. (Rf has no units and is always less than 1.)

### Common mistakes

- Saying the primary structure is "the shape". It is the sequence.
- Saying secondary structure is held by bonds between R groups. It is hydrogen bonds in the backbone.
- Saying heat breaks peptide bonds. Denaturing breaks hydrogen and ionic bonds; the primary structure stays.
- Saying biuret detects amino acids. It detects peptide bonds, so free amino acids give no colour.

## 3.1.4.2 Many proteins are enzymes

*Refs: AQA 3.1.4.2, RP1 · Edexcel A 2.10, 5.16, CP4, CP12 · Edexcel B 1.5 i to vii, CP1 · OCR A 2.1.4(a) to (f) · OCR B 2.1.3(c) to (f) · Eduqas CC4(a) to (h). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Each enzyme lowers the activation energy of the reaction it catalyses.
- The induced-fit model of enzyme action. Models have changed over time (lock and key came first).
- An enzyme's properties depend on the tertiary structure of its active site, which is complementary to its substrate(s), so it forms enzyme–substrate complexes. This explains specificity.
- The effects on rate of: enzyme concentration, substrate concentration, competitive and non-competitive inhibitors, pH and temperature.
- Enzymes catalyse intracellular and extracellular reactions that build structures and control functions from cells to whole organisms.
- pH = −log₁₀[H⁺].

### Explained

**Activation energy** is the energy needed to start a reaction. When an enzyme–substrate complex forms, bonds in the substrate are strained (or, for joining reactions, substrates are held close together in the right orientation), so less energy is needed. More molecules have enough energy at body temperature, so the reaction is faster.

**Lock and key** saw the active site as a rigid shape that exactly fits the substrate. **Induced fit** says the active site is not exactly complementary at first. When the substrate binds, the active site changes shape slightly to mould around it. This puts strain on the substrate's bonds. Induced fit explains things lock and key cannot: why some enzymes bind more than one similar substrate, and how binding something elsewhere (an allosteric site) can change the active site. Eduqas uses lysozyme, which bends the bacterial cell-wall polysaccharide as it binds and so breaks a glycosidic bond.

**Factors affecting rate.**

| Factor | Effect | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature (rising, below optimum) | Rate increases | Molecules have more kinetic energy, so more successful collisions and more E–S complexes form |
| Temperature (above optimum) | Rate falls fast | Vibrations break hydrogen and ionic bonds; tertiary structure and active site change shape; substrate no longer fits; enzyme is denatured (permanent) |
| pH away from optimum | Rate falls | H⁺ or OH⁻ ions affect the charges on R groups; ionic and hydrogen bonds break; active site changes shape |
| Substrate concentration | Rate rises then levels off | More collisions until all active sites are occupied at any moment; enzyme concentration then limits |
| Enzyme concentration | Rate rises, levels off if substrate becomes limiting | More active sites available |
| Competitive inhibitor | Rate lower, but the same maximum can be reached by adding more substrate | Similar shape to the substrate; binds the active site; substrate and inhibitor compete |
| Non-competitive inhibitor | Rate lower, maximum lower, adding substrate does not help | Binds elsewhere (allosteric site); changes the shape of the active site |

**Q10** (OCR A, Edexcel A). The temperature coefficient: Q10 = rate at (T + 10 °C) ÷ rate at T. For most enzymes below their optimum it is about 2: the rate doubles for every 10 °C rise.

**Cofactors** (OCR A). Some enzymes need a non-protein helper. Inorganic ion cofactors: Cl⁻ for amylase. Coenzymes are organic and often made from vitamins: NAD, FAD, coenzyme A. A prosthetic group is a cofactor permanently bound: Zn²⁺ in carbonic anhydrase.

**Inhibition in metabolism** (OCR A, Edexcel B). In end-product inhibition, the last product of a pathway inhibits the first enzyme non-competitively, so production slows when the product builds up. This is negative feedback. Some inhibitors are reversible (bind weakly); others bind permanently and are poisons, such as cyanide (inhibits cytochrome oxidase in respiration). Many medicines are inhibitors: some antivirals inhibit viral enzymes such as reverse transcriptase; ACE inhibitors lower blood pressure.

**Intracellular and extracellular.** Catalase works inside cells, breaking down hydrogen peroxide. Amylase and trypsin are made in cells and secreted to work outside them in the gut.

### Worked example

1. A solution has [H⁺] = 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. Find the pH.

pH = −log₁₀(3.2 × 10⁻⁵) = 4.49 (to 3 s.f., 4.49).

2. An enzyme releases 12 cm³ of oxygen per minute at 25 °C and 21 cm³ per minute at 35 °C. Calculate Q10.

Q10 = 21 ÷ 12 = 1.75.

3. Finding an initial rate from a curved graph: draw a tangent to the curve at time 0 and find its gradient (change in y ÷ change in x). If the tangent passes through (0, 0) and (40 s, 30 cm³), the initial rate = 30 ÷ 40 = 0.75 cm³ s⁻¹.

### Common mistakes

- Saying enzymes are "killed" by heat. They are proteins, not alive: they are denatured.
- Saying the active site and substrate are "the same shape". They are complementary.
- Saying low temperature denatures enzymes. Low temperature just lowers kinetic energy.
- Saying a non-competitive inhibitor "blocks" the active site.
- Measuring the rate at the end of a reaction instead of the initial rate. As substrate is used up the rate falls, so compare initial rates.

### Practical

**AQA required practical 1** (also Edexcel A CP4 and CP12, Edexcel B CP1, OCR A PAG4): investigate the effect of a named variable on the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction.

A typical method: catalase (from potato or yeast) breaking down hydrogen peroxide, collecting oxygen in an inverted measuring cylinder or gas syringe; or amylase digesting starch, sampling every 30 s into iodine on a spotting tile and timing how long until no blue-black colour.

- Independent variable: for example temperature (water baths at 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 °C) or pH (buffer solutions).
- Control variables: enzyme concentration and volume, substrate concentration and volume, pH (use a buffer) or temperature (water bath), time allowed to equilibrate before mixing.
- Control: boiled (denatured) enzyme to show any change is due to the enzyme.
- Repeat each condition at least three times and calculate a mean; identify anomalies.
- Rate = 1 ÷ time, or volume ÷ time; use a tangent at time 0 for the initial rate.
- Uncertainty: half the smallest division on the measuring instrument; percentage uncertainty = uncertainty ÷ reading × 100.
- Risks: hydrogen peroxide is an irritant (wear eye protection); hot water.

# Biological molecules: monomers, carbohydrates and lipids

All living things are built from the same few kinds of carbon-based molecule. This file covers how small units join into large molecules, the structure and jobs of carbohydrates and lipids, and the food tests used to find them.

## 3.1.1 Monomers and polymers

*Refs: AQA 3.1.1 · Edexcel B 1.1 iii · OCR A 2.1.2(b), (c) · OCR B 2.1.2(d) · Eduqas CC1(c). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The chemistry of life is the same in all organisms, which is indirect evidence for a common ancestor.
- A monomer is a small unit that can join with others to make a larger molecule. Monosaccharides, amino acids and nucleotides are monomers.
- A polymer is a large molecule made of many monomers joined together.
- A condensation reaction joins two molecules with a new chemical bond and releases a molecule of water.
- A hydrolysis reaction breaks a chemical bond between two molecules using a molecule of water.
- (OCR A) Carbohydrates contain C, H and O; lipids contain C, H and O; proteins contain C, H, O, N and S; nucleic acids contain C, H, O, N and P.

### Explained

Think of condensation as "making, with water out" and hydrolysis as "splitting, with water in" (hydro = water, lysis = splitting). Digestion is hydrolysis. Building starch in a leaf or glycogen in the liver is condensation. Each needs its own enzyme.

Not every large molecule is a polymer. A triglyceride is large but it is made of one glycerol and three fatty acids, which are not repeating identical units, so it is not a polymer.

### Common mistakes

- Calling lipids polymers.
- Saying hydrolysis "releases" water. It uses water.
- Saying condensation "removes water from the molecule" without saying a bond forms.

## 3.1.2 Carbohydrates

*Refs: AQA 3.1.2 · Edexcel A 1.12, 1.13, 4.9 · Edexcel B 1.1 i to iv · OCR A 2.1.2(d) to (g), (q), (r) · OCR B 2.1.2(b), (c), (e) · Eduqas CC1(c) to (e). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Monosaccharides are the monomers of carbohydrates. Glucose, galactose and fructose are common ones (all hexoses, C₆H₁₂O₆). Ribose and deoxyribose are pentoses.
- Two monosaccharides join by condensation to form a glycosidic bond, making a disaccharide:
  - maltose = glucose + glucose
  - sucrose = glucose + fructose
  - lactose = glucose + galactose
- Glucose has two isomers. In α-glucose the OH on carbon 1 is below the ring; in β-glucose it is above the ring.
- Polysaccharides are made by condensation of many glucose units:
  - starch and glycogen are made from α-glucose
  - cellulose is made from β-glucose.
- Tests: Benedict's test for reducing sugars; a non-reducing sugar test (hydrolyse with acid first); iodine in potassium iodide for starch.

### Explained

**Glucose ring.** Six carbons; carbons 1 to 5 and an oxygen form the ring, carbon 6 sticks out. The only difference between α and β is which way the OH on carbon 1 points. This small difference changes everything about the polymers they make.

**Starch** is the energy store of plants. It is a mix of two polymers of α-glucose:

| | Amylose | Amylopectin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bonds | 1,4-glycosidic only | 1,4 with 1,6 branches |
| Shape | Unbranched chain that coils into a helix | Branched |
| Why it helps | Compact: lots of glucose in a small space | Many ends where enzymes can hydrolyse glucose off quickly |

Starch is insoluble, so it does not lower the water potential of the cell and water does not move in by osmosis. It is large, so it cannot leave the cell.

**Glycogen** is the energy store of animals and fungi. It is like amylopectin but more branched, with shorter chains. More branches means more ends, so glucose can be released fast, which suits animals that move and have a high metabolic rate. It is also insoluble and compact.

**Cellulose** is the main part of plant cell walls. In a chain of β-glucose every other glucose is turned upside down. This gives straight, unbranched chains. Many parallel chains are held together by hydrogen bonds to form microfibrils, and microfibrils group into fibres. Each hydrogen bond is weak, but there are so many that cellulose is very strong. The strength lets the wall resist the pressure of water entering by osmosis, keeping the cell turgid.

**Benedict's test.** All monosaccharides and some disaccharides (maltose, lactose) are reducing sugars. Heat the sample with Benedict's reagent in a water bath. A reducing sugar gives a colour change from blue through green, yellow and orange to brick red, depending on how much sugar there is.

**Non-reducing sugar test.** Sucrose gives no change with Benedict's. If the first test is negative: boil a fresh sample with dilute hydrochloric acid (hydrolyses the glycosidic bond), cool, neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate (Benedict's does not work in acid), then do the Benedict's test again. A red-brown precipitate now shows a non-reducing sugar was present.

**Starch test.** Add iodine in potassium iodide solution. Orange-brown turns blue-black.

**Making the tests quantitative.** The colour of the Benedict's result is only a rough guide. Better methods:

- Filter and dry the precipitate, then weigh it.
- Use a colorimeter. Make a dilution series of glucose solutions of known concentration, test each, measure absorbance (or transmission) and plot a calibration curve. Read the unknown's concentration off the curve. (Required practical 11 in AQA uses this for urine-style glucose samples: file 20.)
- (OCR A) A biosensor uses an immobilised enzyme, such as glucose oxidase, and a transducer that turns the reaction into an electrical signal read as a concentration.

### Worked example

A student makes glucose solutions of 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 mmol dm⁻³ from a 10 mmol dm⁻³ stock, each with a final volume of 10 cm³. What volumes of stock and water make the 4 mmol dm⁻³ solution?

1. Fraction needed = 4 ÷ 10 = 0.4.
2. Stock volume = 0.4 × 10 = 4 cm³.
3. Water = 10 − 4 = 6 cm³.

Answer: 4 cm³ of stock and 6 cm³ of distilled water.

### Common mistakes

- Saying starch is made of β-glucose or cellulose of α-glucose.
- Saying cellulose is branched. It is unbranched and straight.
- Saying "cellulose forms hydrogen bonds with other glucose" instead of "between parallel chains".
- Forgetting to neutralise after acid hydrolysis in the non-reducing sugar test.
- Saying "Benedict's turns red" for a reducing sugar without mentioning heating.
- Saying glycogen is found in plants.

## 3.1.3 Lipids

*Refs: AQA 3.1.3 · Edexcel A 1.14 · Edexcel B 1.2 i to iv · OCR A 2.1.2(h) to (j), (q) · OCR B 2.1.2(f) · Eduqas CC1(f), (g). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Triglycerides and phospholipids are two groups of lipid.
- A triglyceride is made by condensation of one glycerol and three fatty acids. Each condensation forms an ester bond and releases a water molecule (three in total).
- A fatty acid is RCOOH. The R group (hydrocarbon chain) may be saturated (no C=C double bonds) or unsaturated (one or more C=C double bonds).
- In a phospholipid, one fatty acid is replaced by a phosphate-containing group.
- You should explain the different properties of triglycerides and phospholipids from their structures, and recognise saturated and unsaturated fatty acids in diagrams.
- The emulsion test for lipids.

### Explained

**Triglycerides** are the main energy store in animals and many seeds.

| Property | Why | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High ratio of C–H bonds to C atoms | Many bonds to break in respiration | Release about twice as much energy per gram as carbohydrate |
| Low mass for the energy stored | As above | Good store for animals that move |
| Insoluble in water | Fatty acid tails are non-polar | Do not affect water potential |
| High ratio of hydrogen to oxygen | Oxidation releases water | Metabolic water source (desert animals) |
| Poor conductor of heat (OCR, Edexcel B) | | Insulation under the skin; waterproofing in waxy cuticles |

**Saturated and unsaturated.** A double bond puts a kink in the chain, so unsaturated chains cannot pack tightly. That is why unsaturated fats (oils) are liquid at room temperature and saturated fats are more often solid.

**Phospholipids** have a hydrophilic "head" (the phosphate group, which is charged and attracts water) and two hydrophobic "tails" (the fatty acids, which repel water). In water they form a bilayer with heads facing the water on both sides and tails pointing inwards. This is the basis of every cell membrane (file 06). The hydrophobic core stops water-soluble substances passing freely.

**Cholesterol** (OCR A, OCR B) is a lipid with a four-ring structure. It sits between phospholipids in animal membranes and regulates fluidity. It is also used to make steroid hormones and vitamin D.

**Emulsion test.** Shake the sample with ethanol to dissolve any lipid, then pour into water and shake. A milky-white emulsion shows lipid: the lipid comes out of solution as tiny droplets that scatter light.

### Worked example

How many water molecules are released when one triglyceride is made, and how many are needed to hydrolyse two triglycerides fully?

1. Three ester bonds form, each releasing one water: 3 water molecules.
2. Hydrolysing one triglyceride uses 3 water; two use 6.

Answer: 3 released; 6 needed.

### Common mistakes

- Calling the bond in a triglyceride a glycosidic or peptide bond. It is an ester bond.
- Saying phospholipids have "a hydrophobic head".
- Saying unsaturated means "no double bonds". It is the other way round.
- Saying lipids store "more energy" without "per gram".
- In the emulsion test, adding water first. Dissolve in ethanol first.

## Mark scheme

1. Condensation (1); (1,4) glycosidic bond (1).
2. Any three: highly branched, so many ends for enzymes to hydrolyse, so glucose released quickly (1); insoluble, so no effect on water potential / no osmosis into cell (1); compact, so lots stored in small space (1); large, so cannot leave cell (1).
3. β-glucose gives straight, unbranched chains (1); chains lie parallel and are joined by many hydrogen bonds (1); forming microfibrils / fibres, giving strength (1).
4. Benedict's test on sample, heated, stays blue, so no reducing sugar (1); boil fresh sample with dilute hydrochloric acid (1); neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate (1); heat with Benedict's; red/orange/green precipitate shows non-reducing sugar (1).
5. Triglyceride has three fatty acids; phospholipid has two fatty acids and a phosphate-containing group (1); both have glycerol and ester bonds (1).
6. More energy released per gram (1); so lower mass to carry during flight (1). Accept: also releases more metabolic water.
7. Colour depends on volume of reagent and time / temperature as well as glucose concentration (1); so only glucose concentration varies, and the curve applies to the unknowns (1).
8. Heads are hydrophilic and face the water (1); tails are hydrophobic and point away from water, towards each other (1).

# Animal behaviour

Behaviour is what an animal does in response to its environment. This file covers innate behaviour (reflexes, kineses, taxes), learned behaviour (habituation, imprinting, conditioning, latent learning and insight), social behaviour in insects and vertebrates, territory and courtship, sexual selection, and Edexcel A core practical 18.

## Innate behaviour

*Refs: Eduqas OC.3(a). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Innate behaviour and the advantage to organisms of escape reflexes, kineses and taxes.

### Explained

**Innate behaviour** is inherited: it is controlled by genes, is the same in all members of a species, and is performed correctly the first time without learning. It allows a rapid, appropriate response, and is especially important in animals with short lives and little or no parental care.

- **Escape reflexes:** rapid, automatic responses that move an animal away from danger. An earthworm withdraws into its burrow when touched (giant axons conduct the impulse fast, file 19); a startled fish darts away. Advantage: avoiding predators without delay.
- **Kineses:** non-directional changes in the speed of movement or rate of turning in response to the intensity of a stimulus (file 18). Woodlice move faster and turn less in dry conditions, so they end up spending more time in damp places, which reduces water loss.
- **Taxes:** directional movements towards or away from a stimulus. Positive phototaxis in single-celled algae (more light for photosynthesis); negative phototaxis in woodlice and blowfly larvae (avoiding predators and drying out); chemotaxis of bacteria towards food.

## Learned behaviour

*Refs: Edexcel A 8.13, CP18 · Eduqas OC.3(b). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Learned behaviours: habituation, imprinting, classical and operant conditioning, latent learning and insight learning.
- How animals, including humans, learn by habituation (Edexcel A).

### Explained

**Learned behaviour** is changed by experience. It lets animals adapt to changing or unpredictable environments. It is most important in animals with long lives and long periods of parental care.

**Habituation.** The response to a repeated stimulus that is neither harmful nor beneficial gradually decreases and stops. Example: birds learn to ignore a scarecrow; a snail stops withdrawing its tentacles when gently touched many times; people stop noticing a ticking clock. Advantage: the animal does not waste time and energy responding to harmless stimuli, and can respond to stimuli that matter.

Mechanism (Edexcel A): with repeated stimulation, fewer calcium ion channels open in the presynaptic membrane of the sensory neurone, so less neurotransmitter is released into the synapse with the motor neurone; the postsynaptic neurone is depolarised less and fewer action potentials pass, so the response weakens. Habituation is specific to that stimulus: a different or stronger stimulus restores the response (dishabituation).

**Imprinting.** In a short sensitive (critical) period early in life, a young animal learns to recognise and follow a moving object, normally its parent. Example: goslings and ducklings follow the first large moving object they see after hatching (Lorenz showed geese could imprint on him). Advantages: young stay close to a parent for food, warmth and protection; learn to recognise their own species (later mate choice).

**Classical conditioning.** An animal learns to link a new (neutral) stimulus with a stimulus that already causes a response, so the new stimulus alone comes to cause the response. Pavlov's dogs salivated (unconditioned response) to food (unconditioned stimulus); after a bell was rung each time food was given, the bell alone caused salivation (conditioned response).

**Operant conditioning (trial and error).** An animal learns to link one of its own actions with a reward or punishment, so that rewarded actions are repeated and punished actions avoided. Skinner's rats learned to press a lever for food. Used in training animals.

**Latent learning.** An animal explores its surroundings and learns information without any immediate reward; it uses it later. Example: a rat that has explored a maze without a reward finds its way through faster than a new rat when food is later placed at the end. Wild animals learn the layout of their territory, which helps them escape predators.

**Insight learning.** Solving a new problem by thinking it through, using past experience, without trial and error. Example: chimpanzees stacking boxes or joining sticks to reach bananas; some birds using tools. Found mostly in primates, some birds (crows) and possibly other mammals.

### Practical

**Edexcel A CP18: investigate habituation to a stimulus.**

1. Place a giant African land snail on a clean, damp surface; let it settle until its tentacles are extended.
2. Touch the snail gently between the eye stalks with a damp cotton bud.
3. Time how long it takes for the tentacles to extend fully again.
4. Repeat the touch as soon as the tentacles are extended, for 10 to 15 touches; record the time for each.
5. The time to re-emerge decreases as the snail habituates.

Control variables: same snail size and species; same force and place of touch (use a fixed method, for example a cotton bud on a set drop); same temperature, humidity and light. Use several snails and calculate means. Ethics: handle gently; keep snails moist; return them to their tank; avoid causing stress or harm.

## Social behaviour

*Refs: Eduqas OC.3(c) to (f). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Primates, including humans, live in very complex societies and show complex social behaviour.
- The advantages and disadvantages of living in social groups.
- The social structure of some insects, based on a caste system.
- Social structure in vertebrates achieved through dominance hierarchies.

### Explained

**Advantages of living in groups:** protection from predators (more eyes to spot danger; safety in numbers; group defence); cooperative hunting (wolves, lions); finding food (information shared); care of young by several adults; learning from others; division of labour; keeping warm (penguins huddling).

**Disadvantages:** competition for food and mates within the group; disease and parasites spread more easily; groups are more conspicuous to predators; energy used on social interactions; risk of infanticide or aggression; lower-ranking individuals may get little food or few chances to breed.

**Social insects** (bees, ants, termites, wasps). Colonies have a **caste** system: individuals are specialised for different jobs and many do not reproduce. In a honeybee colony:

- One **queen**: lays all the eggs; produces pheromones that control the colony and stop workers developing ovaries.
- **Drones** (males): develop from unfertilised eggs (haploid); mate with new queens.
- **Workers** (sterile females): build comb, feed larvae, clean, guard, collect nectar and pollen. Their job changes with age.

Caste is determined largely by diet as larvae (larvae fed royal jelly throughout become queens), which changes gene expression. Communication by pheromones and by the waggle dance (shows direction and distance of food). Workers share many alleles with the queen's offspring (their sisters), which helps explain why helping the colony spreads their own alleles (kin selection).

**Primate societies.** Primates (including humans) live in complex groups, form long-lasting relationships, recognise individuals, cooperate, form alliances, groom each other (which strengthens bonds and removes parasites), communicate with calls, facial expressions and gestures (language in humans), have long periods of learning in childhood, and pass on learned behaviour (culture, such as tool use in chimpanzees).

**Dominance hierarchies.** In many vertebrate groups (chickens' pecking order, wolves, gorillas, baboons), individuals have a rank. Higher-ranking animals get priority access to food, shelter and mates. Rank is set by displays and occasional fights and is then maintained mainly by signals (posture, threat displays), so less energy is wasted on fighting and fewer animals are injured. Dominant individuals pass on their alleles more; subordinates may still gain protection and may rise in rank later.

## Territory, courtship and sexual selection

*Refs: Eduqas OC.3(g), (h). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The advantage of territorial and courtship behaviours in increasing reproductive success.
- The role of sexual selection in the evolution of territorial and courtship behaviour.

### Explained

**Territory.** A territory is an area an animal (or group) defends against others of the same species. Marked with scent, song or displays; defended by threat displays and sometimes fighting. Advantages: secure supply of food for the owner and its young; nesting sites; access to mates; spreads the population out, reducing competition and disease. Animals without territories may not breed, which regulates population size. Defending a territory costs energy and risks injury, so territories are only held where the benefit is greater than the cost.

**Courtship** (file 14). Species-specific displays (songs, dances, colours, gifts) that: allow recognition of the same species; show that a mate is sexually mature and receptive; synchronise mating; form a pair bond in species where both parents care for young; let one sex judge the quality of the other. Example: the great crested grebe's elaborate head-shaking and "weed dance"; the zig-zag dance of the male stickleback.

**Sexual selection** is natural selection acting on characteristics that increase mating success.

- *Intrasexual selection:* competition between members of one sex (usually males) for access to the other: larger size, antlers, horns and fighting ability (red deer stags).
- *Intersexual selection (mate choice):* one sex (usually females, who invest more in each offspring) chooses mates with particular features, such as bright plumage, long tails or elaborate songs (the peacock's tail). These features may signal good health and good genes (only a healthy male can afford to grow and carry them).

Over generations, alleles for these features and for the preference become more common, even if the features make survival harder (more visible to predators). This explains the evolution of exaggerated courtship displays and territorial behaviour.

### Common mistakes

- Saying habituation is "getting tired" or due to muscle fatigue; it is a change at synapses.
- Confusing imprinting with classical conditioning.
- Saying workers in a bee colony are male.
- Saying innate behaviour can be changed by experience.

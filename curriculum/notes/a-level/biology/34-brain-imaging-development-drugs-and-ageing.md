# The brain: imaging, development, drugs, damage and ageing

This file covers how the brain is studied without surgery, how it develops (critical periods, neuroplasticity, nature and nurture), the cortex and language areas, how imbalances in brain chemicals and drugs affect synapses, drug dependency, brain and spinal cord injury, and Alzheimer's disease.

## The cortex and language

*Refs: Eduqas OC.1(e) to (g). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The sensory and motor areas of the cortex; the relationship between the size of these areas and the sensitivity or dexterity of body parts.
- The areas of the cerebrum involved in language: Broca's and Wernicke's areas.

### Explained

**Motor cortex** (a strip at the back of the frontal lobe) sends impulses to skeletal muscles to start voluntary movement. **Somatosensory cortex** (a strip just behind it, in the parietal lobe) receives impulses from touch, pressure, temperature and pain receptors. Each part of the body has a matching area of cortex. Areas that are very sensitive (lips, fingertips) or need fine control (hands, tongue) have much larger areas of cortex than their size suggests. Drawn to scale, this gives a distorted figure called a **homunculus**. Other areas: visual cortex (occipital lobe), auditory cortex (temporal lobe).

**Language areas** (usually in the left hemisphere).

- **Broca's area** (frontal lobe): producing speech, forming sentences. Damage (Broca's aphasia): speech is slow and halting, but understanding is mostly kept.
- **Wernicke's area** (temporal lobe): understanding language. Damage (Wernicke's aphasia): speech is fluent but makes little sense, and understanding is poor.
- They are joined by a bundle of nerve fibres.

## Studying the brain: imaging

*Refs: Edexcel A 8.9 · OCR B 5.2.1(g) · Eduqas OC.2(a). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- How magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), functional MRI (fMRI) and computed tomography (CT) scans are used in medical diagnosis and to study the brain.

### Explained

| Method | How it works | Shows | Uses and limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CT | X-rays from many angles; a computer builds cross-sections | Structure; denser tissue appears lighter | Quick; detects bleeding, tumours, skull fractures after injury. Uses ionising radiation; less detail of soft tissue than MRI |
| MRI | Strong magnetic field and radio waves; hydrogen nuclei in water give signals that differ between tissues | Detailed structure of soft tissue, distinguishing grey and white matter | Tumours, multiple sclerosis, stroke damage; no radiation. Slow, noisy, expensive; not for people with metal implants |
| fMRI | MRI tuned to detect oxygenated haemoglobin; active areas receive more oxygenated blood | Activity in real time | Mapping which areas are active during tasks (research, planning surgery). Shows blood flow, not neurone activity directly; low time resolution |
| PET (Eduqas, OCR B) | Radioactive tracer (glucose labelled with a positron emitter) injected; active areas take up more | Metabolic activity | Alzheimer's, epilepsy, tumours. Uses radioactivity |
| EEG (Eduqas) | Electrodes on the scalp record electrical activity | Brain waves; timing of activity | Epilepsy, sleep studies. Poor spatial detail |

## Brain development, critical periods and plasticity

*Refs: Edexcel A 8.10, 8.11, 8.12, 8.19 · Eduqas OC.2(b) to (e). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- What happens during the critical period so that mammals can develop normal visual capacities.
- The role animal models have played in research into human brain development and function; the moral and ethical issues of using animals in medical research.
- Neuroplasticity.
- How gene expression affects brain development; how altered gene expression in childhood may predispose adults to disease.
- The methods used to investigate the contributions of nature and nurture to brain development.

### Explained

**The critical period in vision.** At birth, the visual cortex has many synapses. Neurones from each eye connect to columns (ocular dominance columns) of the visual cortex. During a critical period in early life, visual stimulation is needed: active synapses are strengthened and kept; synapses that receive little stimulation are lost ("use it or lose it").

Evidence (Hubel and Wiesel, 1960s): one eye of newborn kittens or monkeys was kept closed for the first few months. Afterwards the animal was blind in that eye, even though the eye itself was normal, because the cortex columns for that eye had been taken over by the open eye. Closing an eye in an adult animal had no such effect. In humans, cataracts or a squint in young children must be treated early to avoid permanent loss of vision (amblyopia).

**Neuroplasticity** (Eduqas). The brain can change its structure and connections through life: forming new synapses, strengthening or weakening existing ones, and sometimes forming new neurones (in the hippocampus). This underlies learning and memory, and recovery after damage, when other areas take over functions (for example after a stroke, with physiotherapy and practice). Plasticity is greatest in childhood.

**Gene expression and development** (Eduqas). Genes control the migration of neurones, their connections and the release of neurotransmitters. The environment changes which genes are expressed through epigenetic marks (file 24). Stress, poor nutrition or abuse in childhood can change the methylation of genes (for example genes for stress hormone receptors), and these changes can last, increasing the risk of depression, anxiety and other conditions in adulthood.

**Animal models** (Edexcel A 8.11, 8.12). Animals share many genes and brain structures with humans and are used to study development and disease (mice, rats, primates). Knockout mice show what a gene does. Advantages: control over genes and environment; procedures impossible in humans. Issues: animals' welfare and pain; whether results apply to humans; the number of animals used. The 3Rs guide use: **replace** animals where possible (cell cultures, computer models), **reduce** numbers, **refine** procedures to reduce suffering. Ethical positions differ: some argue human benefit justifies careful use; others that animals have rights not to be used. Research is licensed in the UK under the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act.

**Nature and nurture** (Edexcel A 8.19). How much do genes and environment each contribute?

- *Twin studies:* compare identical (monozygotic) twins, who share all their alleles, with non-identical (dizygotic) twins, who share about half. A higher concordance in identical twins suggests a genetic influence. Twins raised apart separate the effects of shared environment.
- *Adoption studies:* compare children with their biological parents (genes) and adoptive parents (environment).
- *Cross-cultural studies:* compare people with similar genes in different cultures, or different populations in similar environments.
- *Newborn studies:* abilities present at birth are more likely to be innate.
- *Animal experiments:* raising animals in deprived or enriched environments.
- *Brain damage studies:* which abilities are lost or recovered.

Most characteristics depend on both.

## Brain chemicals and drugs

*Refs: Edexcel A 8.14, 8.15 · Edexcel B 9.6 i · OCR B 5.2.1(i), (j) · Eduqas C3.5(l). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- How imbalances in naturally occurring brain chemicals can contribute to ill health (Parkinson's disease, depression) and to the development of new drugs.
- The effects of drugs on synaptic transmission, including L-dopa in Parkinson's disease and MDMA (Edexcel A).
- Psychological and physical drug dependency (OCR B).

### Explained

**How drugs act at synapses** (Edexcel B 9.6). A drug can:

- increase neurotransmitter release, or mimic it (agonist) → more stimulation
- block receptors (antagonist) or reduce release → less stimulation
- inhibit the enzyme that breaks the neurotransmitter down → more stays in the cleft
- block reuptake into the presynaptic neurone → more stays in the cleft.

**Parkinson's disease.** Dopamine-producing neurones in the substantia nigra (part of the basal ganglia system) die. Less dopamine is released, so movement control is impaired: tremor, stiff muscles, slow movement, poor balance. Treatments:

- **L-dopa:** a precursor of dopamine that can cross the blood–brain barrier (dopamine cannot) and is converted into dopamine in the brain, increasing dopamine release. Effects lessen over time; side effects include involuntary movements.
- Dopamine agonists (mimic dopamine at receptors); MAO-B inhibitors (stop dopamine being broken down).
- Deep brain stimulation; possible future: gene therapy, stem cell transplants.

**Depression.** Linked to low levels of serotonin (and other monoamines) in the brain, although causes are complex. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (**SSRIs**, such as fluoxetine) block the reuptake transporter, so serotonin stays in the synaptic cleft longer, stimulating postsynaptic neurones more. Effects take weeks, suggesting longer-term changes (receptor numbers, neuroplasticity) are involved.

**MDMA (ecstasy).** Binds to serotonin reuptake transporters and reverses them, so serotonin is released into the cleft and not reabsorbed. Serotonin levels rise sharply, causing euphoria and energy; afterwards serotonin is depleted, causing low mood. Risks: overheating, dehydration or drinking too much water (MDMA increases ADH, so hyponatraemia), possible long-term damage to serotonin neurones.

**Other psychoactive drugs** (Eduqas C3.5(l), OCR B 5.2.1(i)): nicotine (mimics acetylcholine at nicotinic receptors; stimulates dopamine release in reward pathways); cocaine (blocks dopamine reuptake); alcohol (enhances GABA, an inhibitory neurotransmitter; depressant); opioids such as morphine (bind to endorphin receptors, reducing pain); benzodiazepines (enhance GABA, reducing anxiety). Organophosphates are in file 19.

**Drug dependency** (OCR B 5.2.1(j)).

- *Physical dependency:* the body adapts to the drug (for example by making fewer receptors or less neurotransmitter), so more drug is needed for the same effect (**tolerance**), and stopping causes physical **withdrawal symptoms** (shaking, sweating, nausea).
- *Psychological dependency:* a craving for the drug's effects (reward pathways involving dopamine), so the person feels unable to cope without it, even without physical withdrawal.

## Damage and ageing of the nervous system

*Refs: OCR B 5.2.1(h), 5.2.3(a), (b). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The consequences of brain and spinal cord injuries.
- The symptoms and possible causes of Alzheimer's disease.
- Age-related loss of vision and hearing (vision detail in file 35).

### Explained

**Brain injury.** Caused by head trauma, stroke (a blocked or burst blood vessel stops blood supply to part of the brain) or tumours. Effects depend on the area: loss of movement or feeling on the opposite side of the body; speech problems (Broca's or Wernicke's areas); memory loss (hippocampus); changes in personality (frontal lobe); coordination problems (cerebellum). Scans (CT, MRI) locate the damage. Rehabilitation uses plasticity.

**Spinal cord injury.** Neurones in the CNS do not regrow well. Damage cuts communication between the brain and the body below the injury: loss of sensation and movement (paralysis). Injury in the neck can cause quadriplegia (all four limbs) and affect breathing; lower down, paraplegia (legs). Autonomic functions below the injury (bladder control) may also be lost. Research: stem cells, nerve growth factors, electrical stimulation.

**Alzheimer's disease.** The most common form of dementia. Symptoms: progressive memory loss (recent memories first), confusion, difficulty with language and planning, mood changes. Changes in the brain: plaques of beta-amyloid protein between neurones; tangles of tau protein inside neurones; loss of neurones and synapses, especially in the hippocampus and cortex; reduced acetylcholine. The brain shrinks. Possible causes and risk factors: age; inherited alleles (such as APOE4); head injury; cardiovascular risk factors. Treatment: drugs that inhibit acetylcholinesterase raise acetylcholine and slow symptoms for a time; newer antibodies that clear amyloid; no cure.

**Age-related hearing loss** (presbycusis): hair cells in the cochlea are damaged and lost over a lifetime (especially by loud noise) and do not regrow, so high-frequency sounds are lost first. Hearing aids amplify sound; cochlear implants stimulate the auditory nerve directly.

### Common mistakes

- Saying dopamine is given as a drug for Parkinson's disease (it cannot cross the blood–brain barrier; L-dopa can).
- Saying SSRIs increase serotonin release.
- Saying fMRI measures electrical activity directly.

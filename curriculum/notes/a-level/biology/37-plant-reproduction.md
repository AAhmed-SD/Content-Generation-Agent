# Sexual reproduction in flowering plants

Flowering plants reproduce sexually by pollination and fertilisation, forming seeds inside fruits. This file covers flower structure, wind and insect pollination, how pollen and embryo sacs form, pollen tube growth, double fertilisation, seeds and fruits, germination and the importance of cereals.

## Overview

Meiosis is in file 13. Control of flowering by phytochrome and gibberellin in germination are in file 31. AQA does not have this content.

## Flowers and pollination

*Refs: OCR B 4.4.1(b) · Eduqas C2.4(a), (c). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The structure of flowers; comparing wind-pollinated and insect-pollinated flowers.
- Cross-pollination and self-pollination.

### Explained

**Flower parts.**

- **Sepals:** protect the bud.
- **Petals:** attract insects (in insect-pollinated flowers).
- **Stamens** (male): **anther** (makes pollen) on a **filament**.
- **Carpels** (female): **stigma** (receives pollen), **style**, **ovary** containing one or more **ovules**.
- **Nectaries** (in insect-pollinated flowers): make sugary nectar.

**Pollination** is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma.

| Feature | Insect-pollinated (e.g. legumes, such as peas and beans) | Wind-pollinated (e.g. grasses and cereals) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Petals | Large, brightly coloured, often with guide lines | Small, green or absent |
| Scent and nectar | Usually present | Absent |
| Anthers | Inside the flower, so insects brush against them | Hang outside the flower on long filaments |
| Stigmas | Inside, sticky | Large, feathery, hang outside to catch pollen |
| Pollen | Larger, sticky or spiky, smaller amounts | Small, light, smooth, very large amounts |

**Self-pollination:** pollen reaches the stigma of the same flower or another flower on the same plant. Reliable, but offspring have less genetic variation (inbreeding).

**Cross-pollination:** pollen from another plant of the same species. More genetic variation, so populations adapt better; but relies on wind or animals and wastes pollen. Mechanisms that favour cross-pollination: anthers and stigmas mature at different times; separate male and female flowers or plants; self-incompatibility (pollen tubes from the same plant cannot grow).

## Gamete formation, pollen tubes and double fertilisation

*Refs: Edexcel A 3.7 · Edexcel B 2.5 i to iii, CP4 · OCR B 4.4.1(c) · Eduqas C2.4(b), (d). Level: AS & A (Edexcel); A-level only (OCR B, Eduqas).*

### What you need to know

- How a pollen grain forms in the anther and the embryo sac forms in the ovule.
- How the pollen tube grows and delivers male nuclei to the embryo sac.
- Double fertilisation to form a diploid zygote and a triploid endosperm.

### Explained

**Pollen formation (in the anther).** The anther has four pollen sacs. Inside, diploid pollen mother cells divide by meiosis, each giving four haploid cells (a tetrad), which separate and develop into pollen grains. Each pollen grain's nucleus divides by mitosis into a **tube nucleus** and a **generative nucleus**. The grain has a tough outer wall (exine) that protects it. The tapetum (a nutritive layer lining the pollen sac) feeds developing pollen. When mature, the anther wall dries and splits (dehisces), releasing pollen.

**Embryo sac formation (in the ovule).** Inside the ovule, a diploid megaspore mother cell divides by meiosis to give four haploid cells; three degenerate. The surviving megaspore divides by mitosis three times, giving eight haploid nuclei in the **embryo sac**. These include the **egg cell** (female gamete), two **polar nuclei** in the centre, and other cells (synergids and antipodal cells). The ovule is covered by integuments, with a small gap called the micropyle.

**Pollen tube growth.**

1. A compatible pollen grain lands on the stigma and absorbs water and sugar.
2. A **pollen tube** grows out through a pore, down through the style towards the ovary, digesting a path with enzymes. The tube nucleus is at the tip and directs growth (it grows towards chemicals from the ovule).
3. The generative nucleus divides by mitosis to form **two male nuclei** (male gametes), which travel down the tube.
4. The tube enters the ovule through the micropyle and the tip bursts, releasing the male nuclei into the embryo sac.

**Double fertilisation.**

- One male nucleus fuses with the egg cell nucleus, forming a **diploid zygote**, which develops into the embryo.
- The other male nucleus fuses with the two polar nuclei, forming a **triploid (3n) endosperm nucleus**, which develops into the endosperm, a food store for the embryo.

This is unique to flowering plants.

### Practical

**Pollen tube growth** (Edexcel B CP4).

1. Make a series of sucrose solutions (for example 0, 5, 10, 15, 20%) on cavity slides or in dishes.
2. Dust pollen (from a freshly opened flower) onto each.
3. Incubate at a constant temperature; after a set time (30 to 60 minutes), view under the microscope.
4. Count the percentage of grains that have germinated, or measure tube length with an eyepiece graticule.

Sucrose supplies a respiratory substrate and affects water potential: too dilute and the pollen may burst; too concentrated and water is drawn out. Control variables: same species, temperature, time, volume. Draw anther sections (Eduqas): a low power plan of a transverse section, showing pollen sacs, tapetum and pollen grains.

## Seeds, fruits and germination

*Refs: OCR B 4.4.1(c) to (e) · Eduqas C2.4(e) to (g). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- How seeds and fruits form; the structure of seeds and fruits of broad bean (a dicotyledon) and maize (a monocotyledon).
- The germination of broad bean.
- The importance of cereals as staple foods (OCR B).

### Explained

**After fertilisation.** The zygote divides by mitosis to form the **embryo** (radicle, which becomes the root; plumule, which becomes the shoot; and one or two cotyledons). The endosperm grows. The integuments harden into the **testa** (seed coat). The ovule becomes the **seed**. The ovary wall develops into the **fruit** (pericarp), which protects seeds and helps disperse them. Water is removed from the seed so it becomes dormant.

| | Broad bean (*Vicia faba*, dicot) | Maize (*Zea mays*, monocot) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cotyledons | Two, large, store the food (endosperm absorbed during development) | One, thin (scutellum), absorbs food from the endosperm |
| Endosperm | Little or none in the mature seed | Large; main food store (starch) |
| Fruit | Pod (the ovary wall) containing several seeds | Each grain is a single-seeded fruit; fruit wall fused with seed coat |
| Other | Testa, micropyle, hilum (scar where seed was attached) | Aleurone layer (protein-rich, makes amylase) |

**Germination** needs water, oxygen and a suitable temperature (and sometimes light, file 31).

1. The seed absorbs water through the micropyle (imbibition); the seed swells and the testa splits.
2. Enzymes are activated (gibberellin switches on amylase genes in cereals, file 31): starch → maltose → glucose; proteins → amino acids; lipids → fatty acids and glycerol.
3. These are transported to the embryo, respired for energy, and used to build new cells.
4. The radicle grows out first and down; then the plumule grows up. In broad bean, the cotyledons stay underground (hypogeal germination) and the plumule is hooked to protect its tip as it pushes through soil.

**Cereals as staple foods** (OCR B 4.4.1(e)). Wheat, rice and maize provide most of the energy in the human diet worldwide. Their grains store much starch in the endosperm, plus protein and some oils; they store well when dry; plants are wind-pollinated, grow fast and give high yields per hectare. Selective breeding (file 22) has produced dwarf, high-yield varieties. Concerns: relying on a few varieties makes crops vulnerable to disease; climate change threatens yields (file 42).

### Common mistakes

- Confusing pollination (transfer of pollen) with fertilisation (fusion of nuclei).
- Saying the pollen grain itself travels down the style.
- Saying the endosperm is diploid.
- Saying the fruit develops from the ovule (it develops from the ovary; the ovule becomes the seed).

## Mark scheme

1. Any four: petals small vs large and coloured; no scent / nectar vs present; anthers outside vs inside; feathery vs sticky stigmas; light, smooth, abundant pollen vs sticky / spiky, less pollen.
2. Greater genetic variation (1); population more able to adapt / survive changes (1).
3. Pollen mother cells divide by meiosis (1); four haploid cells / tetrad (1); develop into pollen grains with tube and generative nuclei / tough wall (1).
4. Pollen tube grows from grain down style to ovary (1); tube nucleus at tip / grows towards ovule (1); generative nucleus divides by mitosis to form two male nuclei (1); one fuses with egg to form diploid zygote (1); other fuses with two polar nuclei to form triploid endosperm (1).
5. Zygote diploid (1); endosperm triploid (1); pollen nucleus haploid (1).
6. Water potential of water higher than inside pollen (1); water enters by osmosis and grain bursts (1).
7. Water absorbed through micropyle; testa splits (1); enzymes activated / hydrolyse stored food in cotyledons (1); products used in respiration and growth of embryo (1); radicle emerges first, then plumule (1).
8. Any three: high starch / energy; also protein; store well / dry; high yields; grow in many climates; fast growing.

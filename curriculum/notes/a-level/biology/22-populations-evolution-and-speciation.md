# Populations, evolution and speciation

Evolution is a change in allele frequencies in a population over time. This file covers gene pools, the Hardy–Weinberg principle, the forces that change allele frequencies, and how new species form.

## 3.7.2 Populations

*Refs: AQA 3.7.2 · Edexcel A 4.5 · Edexcel B 8.3 iv · OCR A 6.1.2(f) · OCR B 5.1.2(c) · Eduqas C2.6(e), (g), (h). Level: A-level only (AS for Edexcel A).*

### What you need to know

- Species exist as one or more populations.
- A population as a group of organisms of the same species occupying a particular space at a particular time that can potentially interbreed.
- The concepts of gene pool and allele frequency.
- The Hardy–Weinberg principle provides a mathematical model, which predicts that allele frequencies will not change from generation to generation. The conditions under which the principle applies.
- Calculate allele, genotype and phenotype frequencies from appropriate data and from the Hardy–Weinberg equation, p² + 2pq + q² = 1, where p is the frequency of one (dominant) allele of the gene and q is the frequency of the other (recessive) allele of the gene.
- Understand and use the expression p + q = 1.

### Explained

**Gene pool:** all the alleles of all the genes in a population at a given time. **Allele frequency:** the proportion of all copies of a gene that are one particular allele.

**Hardy–Weinberg.** For a gene with two alleles:

- p + q = 1 (allele frequencies)
- p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (genotype frequencies): p² = homozygous dominant, 2pq = heterozygous, q² = homozygous recessive.

The principle predicts allele frequencies stay the same from one generation to the next, if:

- the population is large
- mating is random
- there is no selection (all genotypes equally likely to survive and reproduce)
- there is no mutation
- there is no migration into or out of the population (no gene flow).

In reality these are rarely all true. If observed frequencies change over generations, or differ from predictions, one of the conditions has been broken, such as selection acting. That is how the equation is used (Edexcel B: to monitor change).

The usual starting point is q², because the homozygous recessive phenotype is the only one whose genotype you can identify by looking.

### Worked example

1 in 2500 babies in a population is born with a recessive condition. Calculate the frequency of carriers.

1. q² = 1/2500 = 0.0004.
2. q = √0.0004 = 0.02.
3. p = 1 − 0.02 = 0.98.
4. Carriers = 2pq = 2 × 0.98 × 0.02 = 0.0392, about 1 in 26 people (3.9%).

From genotype counts: in 200 snails, 98 are AA, 84 Aa, 18 aa. Allele A count = (2 × 98) + 84 = 280 out of 400 alleles; p = 0.7, q = 0.3. Expected under Hardy–Weinberg: AA = 0.49 × 200 = 98; Aa = 0.42 × 200 = 84; aa = 0.09 × 200 = 18. The population fits.

### Common mistakes

- Taking the square root of the dominant phenotype frequency to get p. The dominant phenotype is p² + 2pq.
- Forgetting the 2 in 2pq.
- Saying Hardy–Weinberg "proves" that a population is not evolving.

## 3.7.3 Evolution may lead to speciation

*Refs: AQA 3.7.3 · Edexcel A 4.4, 5.17, 5.19 · Edexcel B 3.2 i, iii, 8.3 i to iii · OCR A 4.2.2(e), 6.1.2(e), (g), (h) · OCR B 3.1.3(e), 5.1.2(a), (b), (d), (e) · Eduqas C2.6(c), (d), (f), (i) to (k). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Individuals within a population of a species may show a wide range of variation in phenotype. This is due to genetic factors and, to some extent, the environment.
- The primary source of genetic variation is mutation. Meiosis and the random fertilisation of gametes during sexual reproduction produce further genetic variation.
- Predation, disease and competition for the means of survival result in differential survival and reproduction, ie natural selection.
- Those organisms with phenotypes providing selective advantages are likely to produce more offspring and pass on their favourable alleles to the next generation. The effect of this differential reproductive success on the allele frequencies within a gene pool.
- The effects of stabilising, directional and disruptive selection.
- Evolution as a change in the allele frequencies in a population.
- Reproductive separation of two populations can result in accumulation of differences in their gene pools. New species arise when these genetic differences lead to an inability of members of the populations to interbreed and produce fertile offspring. In this way, new species arise from existing species.
- Allopatric and sympatric speciation.
- The importance of genetic drift in causing changes in allele frequency in small populations.
- Explain why individuals within a population of a species may show a wide range of variation in phenotype.

### Explained

**Sources of variation.** Mutation creates new alleles. Meiosis (independent segregation, crossing over) and random fertilisation reshuffle them. The environment adds non-heritable variation.

**Selection pressures.** Predation, disease and competition for food, mates and territory mean more offspring are produced than can survive. Individuals with advantageous phenotypes are more likely to survive and reproduce (differential reproductive success), so their alleles become more frequent in the gene pool.

- *Intraspecific competition* (Eduqas): between members of the same species, for food, territory and mates; it drives selection within a species.
- *Interspecific competition:* between different species for the same resources.

**Three types of selection.**

| Type | Favoured | Effect on distribution | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Directional | One extreme | Mean shifts | Antibiotic resistance; longer necks; darker moths in polluted areas |
| Stabilising | The mean | Range narrows; mean the same | Human birth weight; clutch size in birds |
| Disruptive | Both extremes | Two peaks; can lead to speciation | Beak sizes in a finch population where only large and small seeds are available |

**Genetic drift.** Random changes in allele frequency from one generation to the next, because which individuals happen to reproduce is partly chance. In small populations chance has a much bigger effect: an allele can be lost or fixed even if it gives no advantage. Drift reduces genetic diversity.

- *Founder effect* (Edexcel B, OCR A, OCR B): a few individuals start a new population; their allele frequencies are by chance unlike those of the original population (for example a high frequency of a rare disease allele in an isolated community).
- *Genetic bottleneck:* a population is greatly reduced (by disaster or hunting); the survivors carry only a fraction of the original alleles. Northern elephant seals and cheetahs have very low genetic diversity after bottlenecks.
- *Gene flow* (migration) brings alleles in or takes them out.

**Speciation.**

1. Two populations become reproductively isolated, so no gene flow between them.
2. Different mutations arise in each.
3. Different selection pressures act (different environments), and drift acts differently.
4. Allele frequencies in the two gene pools change differently.
5. Eventually the populations can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring: they are separate species.

*Allopatric speciation:* populations are separated geographically (by a mountain range, a river, the sea). Darwin's finches on the Galápagos Islands are an example.

*Sympatric speciation:* populations live in the same area but become reproductively isolated because of:

- mutations that change courtship behaviour, flowering time or the shape of genitalia
- a mutation that causes polyploidy in plants (offspring cannot breed with the parent population)
- different feeding preferences, so groups mate in different places (apple maggot flies).

**Isolating mechanisms** (OCR A, Eduqas).

- *Prezygotic* (stop fertilisation): geographical; ecological (different habitats); temporal (breeding at different times); behavioural (different courtship); mechanical (genitalia or flowers incompatible); gametic (gametes cannot fuse).
- *Postzygotic* (after fertilisation): hybrid inviability (embryo dies); hybrid sterility (mule); hybrid breakdown (second generation infertile).

**Evidence for evolution** (OCR A, OCR B, Eduqas).

- *Fossils:* show change over time; simpler organisms in older rock layers; transitional forms. Limits: few organisms fossilise; the record has gaps.
- *Comparative anatomy:* homologous structures, such as the pentadactyl limb, show common ancestry.
- *Molecular evidence:* DNA base sequences and protein amino acid sequences (cytochrome c, haemoglobin) are more similar in closely related species. The number of differences increases with time since the species diverged, so differences act as a "molecular clock" (OCR B 5.1.2(b)).
- *Darwin and Wallace* (Eduqas C2.6(k)): observed variation, over-production of offspring, struggle for existence, survival of the fittest, inheritance of advantageous characteristics; the theory explains the origin of species by natural selection.

**Artificial selection** (OCR A 6.1.2(h)). Humans choose individuals with desired characteristics to breed, over many generations: high-yield wheat, dairy cattle with high milk yield, dogs. It reduces genetic diversity, can cause health problems from inbreeding (breathing problems in flat-faced dog breeds), and makes populations vulnerable to new diseases. Gene banks of wild types and old varieties keep a resource of alleles for future breeding.

### Worked example

Before an antibiotic was used, 2% of a bacterial population carried a resistance allele. After a course of treatment, 60% did. Explain the change.

The antibiotic was a selection pressure. Bacteria without the allele were killed; those with it survived and reproduced. Their offspring inherited the allele, so its frequency rose. This is directional selection; it took place over many generations because bacteria reproduce quickly.

### Common mistakes

- Saying mutations happen "in order to" help an organism survive.
- Saying "the population adapts by mutating".
- Saying geographical isolation alone makes a new species. The populations must also change genetically until they can no longer interbreed.
- Saying genetic drift is the same as natural selection. Drift is random; selection is not.
- Saying sympatric speciation needs a physical barrier.

## Mark scheme

1. Gene pool: all the alleles of all genes in a population (1); allele frequency: proportion of an allele of all alleles of that gene in a population (1).
2. Any three: large population; random mating; no selection; no mutation; no migration / gene flow.
3. q² = 0.16, q = 0.4 (1); p = 0.6 (1); 2pq = 0.48, so 48% (1).
4. Populations geographically separated (1); no gene flow / reproductive isolation (1); different mutations (1); different selection pressures change allele frequencies differently (1); eventually cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring (1).
5. Mutation changes courtship / mating time / food preference (1); some individuals become reproductively isolated from others in the same area (1); gene pools diverge until new species form (1).
6. Chance events affect allele frequencies more when there are few individuals (1); an allele can be lost or fixed regardless of advantage (1).
7. Both extremes favoured, intermediates selected against (1); two distinct groups form (1); if they stop interbreeding, gene pools diverge, leading to new species (1).
8. Artificial selection reduces genetic diversity (1); fewer alleles, so less chance that some individuals have resistance (1); whole crop may be susceptible (1).

# Adaptations for nutrition

Organisms get their food in very different ways. This file covers autotrophic and heterotrophic nutrition, saprotrophs, holozoic feeding, nutrition in single-celled organisms, the increasing specialisation of guts, the human gut as an omnivore's gut, herbivores and carnivores, and parasites.

## Types of nutrition

*Refs: Eduqas C3.3(a) to (e). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The terms autotrophic and heterotrophic; autotrophs can be photosynthetic or chemosynthetic.
- The terms saprotrophic (saprobiotic), holozoic and parasitic.
- Saprotrophic nutrition: secretion of enzymes, external digestion and absorption.
- Holozoic nutrition: internal digestion.
- Nutrition in single-celled organisms such as *Amoeba*.

### Explained

**Autotrophs** make their own organic molecules from simple inorganic ones (CO₂, water, mineral ions).

- *Photoautotrophs* use light energy (plants, algae, cyanobacteria): photosynthesis (file 15).
- *Chemoautotrophs* use energy from oxidising inorganic chemicals (nitrifying bacteria oxidising ammonium; bacteria at deep-sea vents oxidising hydrogen sulfide).

**Heterotrophs** obtain organic molecules by feeding on other organisms or their remains.

| Type | How | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Saprotrophic (saprobiotic) | Secrete enzymes onto dead organic matter; digest it outside the body (extracellular digestion); absorb the soluble products | Fungi (bread mould *Rhizopus*), many bacteria |
| Holozoic | Take food into the body (ingestion); digest it internally; absorb; assimilate; egest undigested material | Most animals, *Amoeba* |
| Parasitic | Live on or in a living host, obtaining nutrients from it and harming it | Tapeworm, head louse |

**Saprotrophs** in detail. Fungal hyphae grow over and into the food, giving a large surface area. Enzymes (amylases, proteases, lipases, cellulases) are secreted from the hyphal tips. Products (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, glycerol) are absorbed by diffusion and active transport. Saprotrophs recycle nutrients in ecosystems.

**Holozoic nutrition stages:** ingestion (taking in food); digestion (hydrolysis into small soluble molecules); absorption (into cells or blood); assimilation (using the products to build or respire); egestion (removing undigested waste).

**Single-celled organisms: *Amoeba*.**

1. *Amoeba* moves towards food (a smaller protoctist) and surrounds it with extensions of its cytoplasm (pseudopodia).
2. The food is taken in by phagocytosis into a food vacuole.
3. Lysosomes fuse with the vacuole and release enzymes; the food is digested inside the vacuole.
4. Soluble products diffuse into the cytoplasm.
5. Undigested remains are released from the cell by exocytosis.

A single cell has a large surface area to volume ratio, so no specialised gut is needed.

## Specialised guts

*Refs: Eduqas C3.3(f), (g), (i). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The adaptation of multicellular organisms for nutrition, showing increasing specialisation of the gut, from a single opening to a tube with specialised regions.
- The adaptations of the human gut to a mixed, omnivorous diet.
- The adaptations of herbivore guts and dentition (particularly ruminants) compared with carnivores.

### Explained

**From simple to specialised.**

- *Hydra* and other cnidarians: a sac-like gut with a single opening, used both to take food in and to get rid of waste. Cells lining the cavity secrete enzymes and also engulf food particles. Food cannot be processed continuously.
- *Earthworms* and more complex animals: a tube with a mouth at one end and an anus at the other. Food moves one way, so different regions can be specialised for different jobs (crop for storage; gizzard for grinding; intestine for digestion and absorption), and an animal can feed while still digesting.

**The human gut: an omnivore's gut.** Humans eat both plant and animal food.

- Teeth: incisors (cutting), canines (tearing), premolars and molars (grinding).
- A moderately long small intestine (about 6 m) with villi and microvilli for absorption.
- Enzymes for carbohydrates, proteins and lipids (file 09), but no cellulase; plant cellulose passes through as dietary fibre, which helps peristalsis.
- The stomach stores food and starts protein digestion; the colon absorbs water; a small appendix (reduced) at the junction of the small and large intestine.
- Gut layers (mucosa, submucosa, muscle layers, serosa) are in file 09.

**Herbivores.** Plant material is high in cellulose, which is hard to digest, and low in protein. Mammals cannot make cellulase, so herbivores rely on mutualistic microorganisms (bacteria and protoctists) that do.

*Dentition:* incisors (often only on the lower jaw in ruminants, cutting against a horny pad); no canines, leaving a gap (diastema) where the tongue moves food; large, ridged premolars and molars that grind across one another with a sideways jaw movement; teeth keep growing as they wear down.

*Ruminants* (cows, sheep): a four-chambered stomach.

1. **Rumen:** a large fermentation chamber. Microorganisms digest cellulose to sugars and ferment them to fatty acids (absorbed by the cow, which uses them in respiration), producing CO₂ and methane (belched out).
2. **Reticulum:** forms the cud, which is brought back to the mouth and chewed again (chewing the cud), breaking down plant material further and increasing the surface area for microorganisms.
3. **Omasum:** absorbs water.
4. **Abomasum:** the "true" stomach, secreting acid and proteases; it digests the microorganisms that pass on from the rumen, a source of protein.

*Non-ruminant herbivores* (rabbits, horses): fermentation in a large caecum and appendix after the small intestine; rabbits eat their soft faeces (refection) to pass food through the gut twice.

Herbivores have a long gut relative to body size, giving time for slow digestion.

**Carnivores.** Food (meat) is high in protein and fat and easy to digest.

- *Dentition:* sharp incisors for gripping; large, pointed canines for killing and tearing; carnassial teeth (modified premolar and molar) that slice like scissors; strong jaw muscles with an up-and-down jaw movement; jaw joint that prevents sideways movement and dislocation.
- *Gut:* short, with a small caecum, because meat does not need long fermentation.

## Parasites

*Refs: Eduqas C3.3(j). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Parasites as highly specialised organisms that obtain nutrition at the expense of their host, illustrated by the pork tapeworm (*Taenia solium*), an endoparasite, and the head louse (*Pediculus humanus capitis*), an ectoparasite.

### Explained

**Endoparasites** live inside the host; **ectoparasites** live on the outside.

**Pork tapeworm (*Taenia solium*).** Lives in the human small intestine; pigs are the secondary (intermediate) host.

| Adaptation | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Scolex (head) with hooks and suckers | Attaches firmly to the gut wall so it is not moved along by peristalsis |
| Thick cuticle; secretes enzyme inhibitors | Protects against the host's digestive enzymes and immune response |
| No gut; very large surface area (long, flat body); microvilli-like projections on the surface | Absorbs digested food directly from the host's intestine |
| Few sense organs and a simple nervous system | Lives in a stable environment |
| Body of many segments (proglottids), each with male and female organs; can self-fertilise | Huge numbers of eggs (tens of thousands per segment) increase the chance that some reach a new host |
| Eggs with resistant shells; secondary host | Eggs survive outside the body; larvae form cysts in pig muscle, and people are infected by eating undercooked pork |

Effects on the host: competition for food, malnutrition, abdominal discomfort; larvae can form cysts in human tissues (cysticercosis), including the brain. Control: cooking pork thoroughly, meat inspection, sanitation, drugs.

**Head louse (*Pediculus humanus capitis*).** An ectoparasite living among hair on the human head.

- Mouthparts adapted for piercing skin and sucking blood; saliva contains an anticoagulant.
- Claws on legs grip hair firmly.
- Flattened body and no wings; small size.
- Eggs (nits) glued firmly to the base of hairs.
- Spread by head-to-head contact.

Effects: itching (an allergic reaction to saliva); scratching may lead to infection. Control: combing with fine-toothed combs; insecticidal lotions (resistance has developed to some).

### Common mistakes

- Saying fungi "eat" food; they secrete enzymes and absorb products.
- Saying cows make cellulase.
- Saying tapeworms digest food themselves.

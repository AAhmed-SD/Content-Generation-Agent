# Mass transport in plants: xylem and phloem

Plants move water and mineral ions up from the roots in xylem, and sugars from leaves to where they are needed in phloem. This file covers both, with transpiration, the potometer and adaptations to water availability.

## 3.3.4.2 Mass transport in plants

*Refs: AQA 3.3.4.2 · Edexcel A 4.11 · Edexcel B 4.7 i to v, CP8 · OCR A 3.1.3(a) to (f) · OCR B 2.2.4(a) to (f) · Eduqas C3.2(i) to (r). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Xylem as the tissue that transports water in the stem and leaves of plants. The cohesion-tension theory of water transport in the xylem.
- Phloem as the tissue that transports organic substances in plants. The mass flow hypothesis for the mechanism of translocation.
- Recognise xylem vessels, sieve tube elements and companion cells in photographs and diagrams.
- Interpret evidence from tracer and ringing experiments and evaluate evidence for and against the mass flow hypothesis.

### Explained

**Xylem** vessels are long tubes made from dead cells joined end to end. The end walls have broken down, so there is a continuous column. Their walls are thickened with lignin (in spiral, ring or net patterns), which stops them collapsing under tension and makes them waterproof. Pits in the walls let water move sideways between vessels.

**Water uptake in the root** (OCR A, OCR B, Edexcel B, Eduqas).

1. Root hair cells have a large surface area. Mineral ions are actively transported into them, so their water potential is lower than the soil's; water enters by osmosis.
2. Water crosses the cortex by two pathways:
   - Apoplast pathway: through the cellulose cell walls and spaces between cells. Fast, with little resistance; water is pulled along by cohesion.
   - Symplast pathway: through the cytoplasm of cells, passing from cell to cell through plasmodesmata. (Eduqas also names the vacuolar pathway: through vacuoles as well as cytoplasm.)
3. At the endodermis, the Casparian strip (a band of waterproof suberin in the cell walls) blocks the apoplast. Water must enter the cytoplasm, so the plant controls which ions pass.
4. Endodermis cells actively pump ions into the xylem, lowering its water potential, so water follows by osmosis. This creates **root pressure**, which pushes water a short way up the xylem (it explains guttation and why cut stems "bleed").

**Cohesion-tension theory.**

1. Water evaporates from the moist walls of mesophyll cells into air spaces and diffuses out through the stomata: transpiration.
2. This lowers the water potential of the mesophyll cells, so water moves in from neighbouring cells and ultimately from the xylem.
3. Water molecules are held together by hydrogen bonds (**cohesion**), so as water leaves the top of the xylem, the whole column is pulled up.
4. Water molecules also stick to the lignified xylem walls (**adhesion**), helping the column rise (capillarity).
5. The pull creates **tension** (negative pressure) in the xylem.

Evidence for cohesion-tension: tree trunks get measurably narrower during the day, when transpiration is fastest, because tension pulls the xylem walls inwards; if a xylem vessel is broken, air is drawn in rather than water leaking out, and the plant can no longer pull water up that vessel (the column is broken).

**Transpiration** is the loss of water vapour from the leaves, mainly through stomata. Factors:

| Factor | Effect on rate | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Light intensity | Increases | Stomata open in light |
| Temperature | Increases | Water molecules have more kinetic energy, evaporate faster; air holds more water vapour so gradient is steeper |
| Humidity | Decreases | Smaller water potential gradient between the leaf and the air |
| Air movement (wind) | Increases | Moist air around the stomata is blown away, keeping the gradient steep |

**Phloem** is made of living cells.

- Sieve tube elements: joined end to end; perforated end walls called sieve plates; no nucleus and very few organelles, so little obstruction to flow.
- Companion cells: next to each sieve tube element, linked by many plasmodesmata; have a nucleus and many mitochondria; they provide ATP for active loading of sucrose and keep the sieve tube element alive.

**Translocation** moves organic substances (mainly sucrose, also amino acids) from **sources** (where they are made, such as leaves) to **sinks** (where they are used or stored, such as roots, growing tips and fruits). It can go up or down.

**Mass flow hypothesis.**

1. At the source, sucrose is actively loaded into sieve tubes (companion cells pump H⁺ out using ATP; H⁺ diffuses back in with sucrose through co-transporter proteins).
2. This lowers the water potential in the sieve tube, so water enters from the xylem by osmosis.
3. Hydrostatic pressure rises at the source end.
4. At the sink, sucrose is removed (used in respiration or converted to starch), so the water potential there is higher; water leaves the sieve tube by osmosis, lowering the pressure.
5. The pressure difference pushes the solution from source to sink: mass flow.

**Evidence.**

*Ringing experiments.* Removing a ring of bark (which contains the phloem but not the xylem) from a tree trunk makes the bark above the ring swell with sugary fluid, and tissue below dies. This shows phloem carries sugars down the trunk; the xylem still carries water because leaves above stay alive for a while.

*Tracer experiments.* Leaves supplied with ¹⁴CO₂ make radioactive sugars. Autoradiographs of stem sections show the radioactivity in the phloem. Radioactivity later appears in sinks.

*Aphids.* An aphid's mouthparts (stylet) pierce a single sieve tube; cutting off the aphid leaves the stylet behind, and sap flows out under pressure. Sap closer to the leaves has a higher sucrose concentration.

*For mass flow:* sap is under pressure; there is a sucrose concentration gradient from source to sink; metabolic poisons that stop ATP production stop translocation (needed for loading).

*Against mass flow:* sieve plates would slow flow a lot; different solutes move at different speeds and in different directions in the same phloem, which a simple pressure-driven flow cannot explain; sucrose reaches all sinks at a similar rate, not faster to the lower-concentration ones.

**Adaptations to water availability** (OCR A, OCR B, Eduqas).

- *Xerophytes* (marram grass, cacti): thick waxy cuticle; rolled leaves with stomata on the inner surface; sunken stomata in pits; hairs trapping moist air; fewer stomata; leaves reduced to spines; succulent tissue storing water; long or wide shallow roots; low water potential in cells.
- *Hydrophytes* (water lilies): stomata on the upper surface of floating leaves; little or no cuticle; air spaces (aerenchyma) that keep leaves afloat and supply oxygen to submerged parts; little xylem or lignin, because water supports the plant.
- *Mesophytes* (Eduqas): plants of moderate water supply, such as most crop plants; they close stomata and may wilt in drought.

**Distribution of vascular tissue** (OCR A). In a dicot root the xylem forms a central star (X shape) with phloem between its arms, helping the root resist pulling forces. In a stem, vascular bundles form a ring near the outside, with xylem on the inside and phloem on the outside, resisting bending. In a leaf, they form the midrib and veins, with xylem on the upper side.

### Worked example

A potometer has a capillary tube with an internal radius of 0.5 mm. An air bubble moves 24 mm in 4 minutes. Calculate the rate of water uptake in mm³ min⁻¹.

1. Volume = πr² × length = π × 0.5² × 24 = π × 0.25 × 24 = 18.85 mm³.
2. Rate = 18.85 ÷ 4 = 4.7 mm³ min⁻¹ (2 s.f.).

### Common mistakes

- Saying a potometer measures transpiration. It measures water uptake, which is close to transpiration but some water is used in photosynthesis and to keep cells turgid.
- Saying water moves up the xylem by osmosis or by "capillary action" alone.
- Saying sucrose diffuses into sieve tubes. It is actively loaded.
- Saying phloem cells are dead (xylem vessels are dead).
- Saying translocation only goes downward.
- Saying humidity increases transpiration.

### Practical

**Measuring water uptake with a potometer** (OCR A PAG5, Edexcel B CP8, OCR B 2.2.4(c); AQA skills opportunity).

1. Cut a shoot under water, at a slant, so no air enters the xylem.
2. Assemble the potometer under water; fit the shoot with a tight seal; seal joints with petroleum jelly.
3. Dry the leaves; let the shoot adjust to the conditions for a few minutes.
4. Introduce one air bubble; measure how far it moves in a set time. Reset with the reservoir tap.
5. Change one factor (light distance, a fan, temperature, a plastic bag for humidity) and repeat.

Control variables: same shoot or shoots of equal leaf area, same time, same other conditions. To compare plants, divide by leaf area.

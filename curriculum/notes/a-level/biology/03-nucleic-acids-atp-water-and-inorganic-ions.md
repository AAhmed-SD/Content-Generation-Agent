# Nucleic acids, DNA replication, ATP, water and inorganic ions

DNA and RNA carry genetic information, ATP carries energy, and water and ions make life's chemistry possible. This file covers their structures and properties and how DNA copies itself.

## 3.1.5.1 Structure of DNA and RNA

*Refs: AQA 3.1.5.1 · Edexcel A 2.5 · Edexcel B 1.4 i · OCR A 2.1.3(a), (b), (d) · OCR B 2.1.4(a), (c) · Eduqas CC5(a), (e) to (g). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- DNA holds genetic information; RNA transfers it from DNA to the ribosomes. Ribosomes are made of RNA and protein.
- DNA and RNA are polymers of nucleotides. A nucleotide = a pentose sugar + a nitrogen-containing base + a phosphate group.
- DNA nucleotide: deoxyribose, phosphate, and adenine, cytosine, guanine or thymine.
- RNA nucleotide: ribose, phosphate, and adenine, cytosine, guanine or uracil.
- Nucleotides join by condensation, forming phosphodiester bonds.
- DNA is a double helix of two polynucleotide strands held by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs. RNA is a relatively short single strand.
- DNA's simplicity made many scientists doubt it could carry the genetic code.

### Explained

**The backbone.** The phosphate of one nucleotide bonds to the sugar of the next, giving a sugar–phosphate backbone with the bases sticking out.

**Pairing.** A pairs with T (two hydrogen bonds); C pairs with G (three hydrogen bonds). Adenine and guanine are purines (two rings); cytosine, thymine and uracil are pyrimidines (one ring). Each pair is one purine plus one pyrimidine, so the rungs are all the same width and the helix is regular.

**Antiparallel strands.** The two strands run in opposite directions (one 5′ to 3′, the other 3′ to 5′).

**Why DNA suits its job.**

- Very long, so it stores a lot of information.
- Two strands, each a template, so it can be copied accurately.
- Bases protected inside the helix by the backbone; many hydrogen bonds make it stable.
- Base sequence can vary without limit, so it can code for any protein.

**DNA compared with RNA** (Eduqas):

| | DNA | RNA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sugar | Deoxyribose | Ribose |
| Bases | A, T, C, G | A, U, C, G |
| Strands | Two, double helix | One |
| Length | Very long | Short |

**Why scientists doubted DNA.** It has only four kinds of nucleotide, while proteins have 20 amino acids. Proteins seemed the more likely carrier of complex information until experiments in the 1940s and 1950s showed DNA was the genetic material.

**Purifying DNA** (OCR A practical). Grind tissue (onion or kiwi) with a solution of detergent and salt; detergent breaks membranes, salt helps the DNA clump. Add protease to break down histone proteins. Filter, then pour ice-cold ethanol gently down the side. DNA is insoluble in cold ethanol and precipitates as white strands at the boundary.

### Worked example

In a DNA sample, 21% of bases are thymine. What percentage are guanine?

1. A pairs with T, so A = 21%. A + T = 42%.
2. C + G = 100 − 42 = 58%.
3. C = G, so G = 29%.

Answer: 29%.

### Common mistakes

- Saying DNA contains ribose or uracil.
- Saying the two strands are held by "phosphodiester bonds". Strands are held together by hydrogen bonds; phosphodiester bonds are within each strand.
- Using base pairing rules for RNA in a single strand. In single-stranded RNA there is no rule that A = U.

## 3.1.5.2 DNA replication

*Refs: AQA 3.1.5.2 · Edexcel A 2.11 · Edexcel B 1.4 ii · OCR A 2.1.3(e) · OCR B 2.1.4(d) · Eduqas CC5(h). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Semi-conservative replication keeps genetic information the same between generations of cells.
- Steps: DNA helicase unwinds the double helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds between bases; free DNA nucleotides are attracted to exposed complementary bases on both template strands; DNA polymerase joins adjacent nucleotides by condensation, forming phosphodiester bonds.
- Evaluate the work that confirmed the Watson–Crick model.

### Explained

Semi-conservative means each new molecule has one original strand and one new strand.

DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides in one direction (to the 3′ end of the growing strand). Because the strands are antiparallel, one new strand is made continuously and the other in short fragments that are later joined by ligase. (Only OCR and Edexcel B mention this; it is useful for explaining why the active site of DNA polymerase is specific to one orientation.)

**Meselson and Stahl (1958).** Two models were possible: conservative (the original molecule stays whole; the copy is entirely new) and semi-conservative.

1. Bacteria were grown for many generations on a medium with heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N), so all their DNA was heavy.
2. They were moved to light nitrogen (¹⁴N) and allowed to divide once. DNA was extracted and centrifuged in a solution that forms a density gradient.
3. After one generation, there was one band at intermediate density. This ruled out conservative replication, which would give one heavy and one light band.
4. After two generations, there were two bands, intermediate and light, in equal amounts. This is exactly what semi-conservative replication predicts.

Why it convinced people: a clear prediction from each model, a single variable changed, a result that matched only one model, and others could repeat it.

### Worked example

Bacteria with all-¹⁵N DNA are moved to ¹⁴N and divide three times. What fraction of DNA molecules are hybrid (¹⁵N/¹⁴N)?

1. One molecule becomes 2, then 4, then 8.
2. The two original heavy strands are always in separate molecules: 2 hybrid molecules.
3. Fraction = 2 ÷ 8 = 1/4. The other 3/4 are light.

### Common mistakes

- Saying DNA polymerase breaks hydrogen bonds or pairs bases. Helicase separates strands; bases pair by themselves; polymerase forms phosphodiester bonds.
- Saying DNA polymerase forms hydrogen bonds.
- Saying "half the DNA is new" instead of "each molecule has one old and one new strand".

## 3.1.6 ATP

*Refs: AQA 3.1.6 · Edexcel A 5.6 · Edexcel B 4.2 vi, 5.4 iv · OCR A 2.1.3(c) · OCR B 2.1.4(b) · Eduqas CC5(b) to (d), C1.1(a). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is a nucleotide derivative: ribose + adenine + three phosphate groups.
- ATP hydrolase catalyses ATP + water → ADP + Pi (inorganic phosphate), releasing energy.
- Hydrolysis of ATP can be coupled to energy-requiring reactions.
- The Pi released can phosphorylate other compounds, often making them more reactive.
- ATP is remade by condensation of ADP and Pi, catalysed by ATP synthase, during photosynthesis and respiration.

### Explained

ATP is the cell's immediate energy source, not a long-term store. Why it suits this:

- Energy is released in small, manageable amounts, so little is wasted as heat.
- Hydrolysis is a single, fast reaction.
- ATP is soluble and moves easily within the cell.
- It is quickly remade.
- It can phosphorylate substances, such as glucose at the start of glycolysis, making them more reactive.

Uses: active transport (changing the shape of carrier proteins), muscle contraction, DNA and protein synthesis, nerve impulse recovery, secretion by exocytosis.

ATP cannot pass out of cells, so every cell makes its own. A person turns over roughly their own body mass in ATP each day, which shows it is constantly recycled.

### Common mistakes

- Saying "breaking the bond releases energy". Bond breaking needs energy; the hydrolysis reaction as a whole releases energy.
- Saying ATP is "a store of energy" like glycogen.
- Calling ATP synthase "ATP hydrolase" or the reverse.

## 3.1.7 Water

*Refs: AQA 3.1.7 · Edexcel A 1.2 · Edexcel B 1.7 i · OCR A 2.1.2(a) · OCR B 2.1.2(a) · Eduqas CC1(b). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

Water is a major component of cells. It:

- is a metabolite in many reactions, including condensation and hydrolysis
- is an important solvent in which metabolic reactions occur
- has a relatively high specific heat capacity, buffering temperature changes
- has a relatively large latent heat of vaporisation, giving cooling with little water lost
- has strong cohesion between molecules, which supports columns of water in xylem and gives surface tension.

### Explained

All of these come from water being polar. Oxygen pulls electrons more strongly than hydrogen, so O is slightly negative (δ−) and each H slightly positive (δ+). Hydrogen bonds form between the O of one molecule and the H of another.

| Property | Cause | Importance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solvent | Polar molecules and ions dissolve as water clusters around them | Reactions happen in solution; transport in blood, xylem and phloem |
| High specific heat capacity | Hydrogen bonds absorb a lot of energy before molecules speed up | Stable temperature in cells and aquatic habitats |
| High latent heat of vaporisation | Many hydrogen bonds must break to evaporate water | Sweating and transpiration cool organisms well |
| Cohesion | Hydrogen bonds between water molecules | Continuous columns in xylem; surface tension lets small insects walk on ponds |
| Ice is less dense than liquid water (OCR, Edexcel B) | Hydrogen bonds hold molecules apart in a lattice | Ice floats and insulates water below |
| Metabolite | | Hydrolysis, photosynthesis (photolysis) |

### Common mistakes

- Saying water molecules are held by "hydrogen bonds within the molecule". Hydrogen bonds are between molecules.
- Saying high specific heat capacity means water "heats up quickly". It is the opposite.

## 3.1.8 Inorganic ions

*Refs: AQA 3.1.8 · Edexcel B 1.6 i · OCR A 2.1.2(p) · Eduqas CC1(a). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Inorganic ions are in solution in the cytoplasm and body fluids, some in high and some in very low concentrations. Each has a specific role.
- Recognise their roles in: hydrogen ions and pH; iron ions in haemoglobin; sodium ions in co-transport of glucose and amino acids; phosphate ions in DNA and ATP.

### Explained

| Ion | Role |
| --- | --- |
| H⁺ | Determines pH, which affects enzyme shape; proton gradients drive ATP synthesis; Bohr effect |
| Fe²⁺ | In haem, binds oxygen in haemoglobin |
| Na⁺ | Co-transport of glucose and amino acids into epithelial cells; nerve impulses |
| PO₄³⁻ | Part of nucleotides, DNA, RNA, ATP and phospholipids |
| Ca²⁺ (OCR A, Eduqas, Edexcel B) | Neurotransmitter release; muscle contraction; blood clotting; bone; calcium pectate in plant middle lamella |
| K⁺ (OCR A) | Nerve impulses; stomatal opening |
| Cl⁻ (OCR A) | Chloride shift; cofactor for amylase |
| HCO₃⁻ (OCR A) | Carries CO₂ in blood; buffers pH |
| NO₃⁻, NH₄⁺ (OCR A, Edexcel B) | Nitrogen source for amino acids and nucleotides in plants |
| Mg²⁺ (Eduqas, Edexcel B) | Part of chlorophyll |
| OH⁻ (OCR A) | Affects pH; involved in bonding in proteins |

### Common mistakes

- Saying iron is in the globin chains. It is in the haem group.
- Writing "sodium" where "sodium ions" is needed; ions carry the charge that makes them work.

1. Any two (1 each): deoxyribose vs ribose; thymine vs uracil; (accept: DNA nucleotides form double-stranded polymer).
2. C = G = 20%, so A + T = 60%, A = 30% (1); 0.30 × 1800 = 540 (1).
3. Helicase unwinds the helix and breaks hydrogen bonds between bases (1); free nucleotides pair with complementary bases on each template strand (1); DNA polymerase joins adjacent nucleotides by condensation / phosphodiester bonds (1).

6. Water evaporates using latent heat from the skin (1); high latent heat of vaporisation, so a lot of heat is removed for little water lost (1).

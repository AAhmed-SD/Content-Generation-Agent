# Practical skills, data handling and statistical tests

Every A-level Biology course tests practical skills in the written papers and through a practical endorsement. This file covers planning, carrying out, analysing and evaluating experiments, the statistical tests the boards use, and a list of each board's required practicals with the file that covers each one.

## Overview

AS and A-level, all boards. OCR A Module 1 and OCR B Module 1 are written out as spec content; the other boards list the same skills in an appendix. Chi-squared is worked through in file 21 and diversity indices in file 14.

### Board differences

- OCR A 1.1.1 to 1.1.4 (planning, implementing, analysis, evaluation) and 1.2.1, 1.2.2 (practical endorsement and the practical activity groups, PAG1 to PAG12). OCR B 1.1 and 1.2 (the same structure).
- AQA has 12 required practicals (RP1 to RP12), Edexcel A 18 core practicals (CP1 to CP18), Edexcel B 16 (CP1 to CP16), and Eduqas a list of specified practical work in each topic. All boards share the Common Practical Assessment Criteria (CPAC) for the practical endorsement.
- Statistical tests named by boards: Student's t-test (OCR A 4.2.2(f), Edexcel B 10.1 vi), Spearman's rank correlation (Edexcel B 10.1 vi, OCR A), chi-squared (all boards), standard deviation (all). AQA lists the tests in its mathematical requirements and expects students to choose the right test.
- Edexcel A 1.8 and 1.9 ask how to interpret health data and evaluate study design (correlation and cause); Edexcel A 5.18 and Edexcel B 3.1 vi and 10.4 iv ask how the scientific community validates findings (journals, peer review, conferences).

## Planning, implementing, analysing and evaluating

*Refs: OCR A 1.1.1 to 1.1.4, 1.2.1, 1.2.2 · OCR B 1.1, 1.2 · Edexcel A 1.8, 5.18 · Edexcel B 3.1 vi, 10.4 iv. Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- Plan an experiment: identify independent, dependent and control variables; choose apparatus and techniques; write a hypothesis; consider risks and ethics.
- Carry out experiments safely using a wide range of apparatus and techniques; record results in suitable tables.
- Process and analyse data: calculate means, rates, percentages, percentage change; plot graphs; find gradients and intercepts; use statistical tests.
- Evaluate: identify anomalies; consider accuracy, precision, repeatability, reproducibility and validity; discuss limitations and improvements; calculate uncertainty.
- Recognise how scientific findings are validated by the scientific community.

### Explained

**Variables.**

- *Independent:* the one you change.
- *Dependent:* the one you measure.
- *Control variables:* others that could affect the result, kept the same.
- *Control experiment:* a set-up without the independent variable (or with it set to zero), to show that the effect is due to it. For example, boiled enzyme; tube with no organisms.

**Key terms.**

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Accurate | Close to the true value |
| Precise | Repeated readings are close together (small spread) |
| Repeatable | The same person, method and equipment give similar results |
| Reproducible | Different people or methods give similar results |
| Valid | The experiment measures what it claims to, with control variables kept constant |
| Anomaly | A result that does not fit the pattern; investigate it and exclude it from the mean if there is a reason |
| Uncertainty | The range within which the true value probably lies; usually ± half the smallest division of the instrument |

**Percentage uncertainty** = (uncertainty ÷ measured value) × 100. A burette read to ±0.05 cm³ for a 10.00 cm³ reading: 0.5%.

**Tables:** independent variable in the first column; headings with units in the heading, not in the cells; consistent decimal places matching the instrument's resolution.

**Graphs:** independent variable on x; dependent on y; label both axes with units; use more than half the grid; plot points accurately with small crosses; draw a line or curve of best fit (or join points with straight lines if intermediate values cannot be assumed). Bar charts for categories; histograms for continuous grouped data; scatter graphs for correlations.

**Rates.** Rate = change ÷ time, or 1 ÷ time. For a curve, draw a tangent and find its gradient.

**Correlation and cause** (Edexcel A 1.8, 1.9). A correlation shows that two variables change together. It does not show that one causes the other: a third factor may cause both. Causation needs a plausible mechanism and controlled experiments. Good studies have large samples, matched groups, controls, and blinding (the people involved do not know who had the treatment); they are repeated by others.

**Validation** (Edexcel A 5.18, Edexcel B 3.1 vi, 10.4 iv). Scientists publish in peer-reviewed journals (other experts check methods, analysis and conclusions before publication), present at conferences, and other scientists try to repeat the work. Conflicting evidence is resolved by further investigation. This process makes accepted knowledge more reliable but takes time.

**Ethics and safety.** Do a risk assessment: hazard, risk, how to reduce it. Treat living organisms with respect: use the minimum number, return animals to their habitat, use material from food animals for dissection. Human volunteers must give informed consent.

### Common mistakes

- Confusing accurate and precise.
- Calling a set of results "reliable" because the line is straight.
- Writing units in the cells of a table.
- Saying "repeat to make it more accurate". Repeats improve repeatability and let you spot anomalies; accuracy depends on the method and instruments.

## Statistical tests

*Refs: Edexcel B 8.2 vi, 10.1 vi · OCR A 4.2.2(f), 6.1.2(c). Level: AS & A (OCR A 4.2.2(f) AS); A-level (Edexcel B).*

### What you need to know

- Calculate the mean, range, standard deviation and (for AQA and others) the standard error and 95% confidence interval.
- Choose the right test: chi-squared for comparing observed and expected frequencies in categories; Student's t-test for comparing the means of two sets of continuous data; Spearman's rank correlation coefficient for a correlation between two variables.
- Write a null hypothesis; compare the test statistic with the critical value at p = 0.05; draw a conclusion.

### Explained

**Standard deviation** measures spread around the mean:

s = √[ Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1) ]

About 68% of data lie within ±1 s of the mean, 95% within ±2 s (for normally distributed data).

**Standard error** of the mean: SE = s ÷ √n. The 95% confidence interval is about mean ± 2 × SE. If the 95% confidence intervals of two means do not overlap, the difference is likely to be significant. Error bars on graphs may show SD, SE or 95% confidence intervals; always say which.

**Choosing a test.**

| Data and question | Test |
| --- | --- |
| Counts in categories; do observed numbers fit an expected ratio? | Chi-squared (χ²) |
| Two sets of measurements; is there a significant difference between their means? (Data roughly normal) | Student's t-test |
| Paired measurements of two variables; is there a significant correlation? | Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (rs) |

**Null hypothesis.** States there is no difference (t-test, χ²) or no correlation (Spearman's). The probability level used is p = 0.05: if the test statistic is greater than the critical value at p = 0.05, there is less than a 5% probability that the result is due to chance, so reject the null hypothesis.

**Student's t-test (unpaired).**

t = |x̄₁ − x̄₂| ÷ √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂)

Degrees of freedom = n₁ + n₂ − 2.

**Spearman's rank correlation coefficient.**

rs = 1 − (6Σd²) ÷ (n(n² − 1))

1. Rank each variable separately (1 = smallest). Tied values share the mean of their ranks.
2. Find d, the difference in ranks for each pair; square; sum.
3. rs ranges from −1 (perfect negative) to +1 (perfect positive).
4. Compare |rs| with the critical value for n pairs at p = 0.05. You need at least about 7 pairs.

### Worked example

**Standard deviation and standard error.** Data: 4, 7, 5, 6, 8. Mean = 6. Deviations² = 4, 1, 1, 0, 4; Σ = 10. s = √(10 ÷ 4) = 1.58. SE = 1.58 ÷ √5 = 0.71. 95% CI ≈ 6 ± 1.4.

**t-test.** Leaf lengths (cm) in sun: 12, 14, 15, 13, 16 (mean 14.0, s = 1.58). In shade: 10, 11, 12, 11, 11 (mean 11.0, s = 0.71).

1. Null hypothesis: there is no significant difference between the mean lengths.
2. t = 3.0 ÷ √(1.58²/5 + 0.71²/5) = 3.0 ÷ √(0.5 + 0.1) = 3.0 ÷ 0.775 = 3.87.
3. Degrees of freedom = 8; critical value at p = 0.05 = 2.31.
4. 3.87 > 2.31, so reject the null hypothesis: the difference is significant (p < 0.05).

**Spearman's.** Light intensity ranks (1 to 8) and moss cover ranks: 1, 3, 2, 5, 4, 6, 8, 7.

1. d values: 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 1, 1; Σd² = 6.
2. rs = 1 − (6 × 6) ÷ (8 × 63) = 1 − 36 ÷ 504 = 0.93.
3. Critical value for n = 8 at p = 0.05 is 0.74 (two-tailed, from tables). 0.93 > 0.74, so there is a significant positive correlation.

### Common mistakes

- Using a t-test on counts in categories, or chi-squared on means.
- Saying a significant correlation proves cause.
- Forgetting n − 1 in the standard deviation of a sample.
- Saying the null hypothesis is "proved true".
- Using the wrong degrees of freedom.

## Check questions

1. Explain the difference between accuracy and precision. (2 marks)
2. A student measured the time for a colour change with a stopwatch reading to 0.01 s. Suggest why the uncertainty is larger than 0.01 s. (2 marks)
3. State which statistical test you would use for each: (a) whether woodlice prefer damp or dry conditions in a choice chamber; (b) whether the mean mass of limpets differs between two shores; (c) whether there is a correlation between soil pH and the number of earthworms. (3 marks)
4. Calculate the standard deviation of 3, 5, 7, 5. (2 marks)
5. Two means have 95% confidence intervals of 12.0 ± 1.5 and 16.0 ± 1.8. What can you conclude? (2 marks)
6. A t-test gave t = 1.9 with 10 degrees of freedom; the critical value at p = 0.05 is 2.23. State the conclusion. (2 marks)
7. Explain why peer review is important before a finding is published. (2 marks)
8. Calculate Spearman's rs for 7 pairs with Σd² = 12, and state whether there is a significant correlation (critical value 0.79). (3 marks)

## Mark scheme

1. Accurate: close to the true value (1); precise: repeated values close together / small spread (1).
2. Human reaction time when starting / stopping (1); judging the end point of the colour change (1).
3. (a) chi-squared (1); (b) t-test (1); (c) Spearman's rank (1).
4. Mean 5; Σ(x − x̄)² = 4 + 0 + 4 + 0 = 8 (1); s = √(8/3) = 1.63 (1).
5. Intervals do not overlap (12.0 to 13.5 and 14.2 to 17.8) (1); difference is likely to be significant / not due to chance (1).
6. 1.9 < 2.23, so accept null hypothesis (1); no significant difference between the means; difference due to chance (1).
7. Other experts check methods, data and conclusions (1); reduces errors / fraud / unsupported claims; findings more reliable (1).
8. rs = 1 − (6 × 12) ÷ (7 × 48) = 1 − 72/336 = 0.79 (2); equal to critical value, so (just) significant at p = 0.05 / borderline; accept either with reasoning that rs must be at least the critical value (1).

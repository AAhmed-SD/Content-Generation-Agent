# DNA, genes, chromosomes and protein synthesis

A gene is a length of DNA that codes for a polypeptide. This file covers how DNA is packaged in different cells, the genetic code, and how proteins are made by transcription and translation.

## 3.4.1 DNA, genes and chromosomes

*Refs: AQA 3.4.1 · Edexcel A 2.7, 2.8 · Edexcel B 1.4 iii, vii · OCR A 2.1.3(f) · OCR B 2.1.4(e) · Eduqas CC5(i), (j), (k), (n). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- In prokaryotic cells, DNA molecules are short, circular and not associated with proteins.
- In the nucleus of eukaryotic cells, DNA molecules are very long, linear and associated with proteins called histones. Together a DNA molecule and its associated proteins form a chromosome.
- The mitochondria and chloroplasts of eukaryotic cells also contain DNA which, like prokaryotic DNA, is short, circular and not associated with protein.
- A gene is a base sequence of DNA that codes for the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide, or a functional RNA (including ribosomal RNA and tRNAs).
- A gene occupies a fixed position, called a locus, on a particular DNA molecule.
- A sequence of three DNA bases, called a triplet, codes for a specific amino acid. The genetic code is universal, non-overlapping and degenerate.
- In eukaryotes, much of the nuclear DNA does not code for polypeptides. There are, for example, non-coding multiple repeats of base sequences between genes. Even within a gene only some sequences, called exons, code for amino acid sequences. Within the gene, these exons are separated by one or more non-coding sequences, called introns.

### Explained

**Packaging.** Each human nucleus holds about 2 m of DNA. It is wound around histone proteins (like thread round a bobbin) and coiled again and again into a compact chromosome. In prokaryotes there are no histones; the circular DNA is just looped and folded.

**The genetic code.**

- *Triplet:* three bases code for one amino acid. Four bases taken three at a time give 4³ = 64 combinations, more than enough for 20 amino acids.
- *Degenerate:* most amino acids have more than one triplet. This means some base substitutions do not change the amino acid.
- *Non-overlapping:* each base is read once, as part of only one triplet.
- *Universal:* the same triplet codes for the same amino acid in (nearly) all organisms. This is evidence of common ancestry and is why a human gene can be expressed in a bacterium.
- *Start and stop:* a start codon (AUG on mRNA, coding for methionine) begins translation; stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA) code for no amino acid and end translation.

**Non-coding DNA.** Only about 1.5% of human DNA codes for proteins. The rest includes introns, regulatory sequences, genes for functional RNAs, and repeated sequences between genes (useful in genetic fingerprinting, file 25).

### Common mistakes

- Saying a gene codes for "a protein" or "a characteristic". It codes for a polypeptide (or a functional RNA).
- Saying the genetic code is "universal" means all organisms have the same DNA. It means the same triplets code for the same amino acids.
- Saying introns are removed from DNA. They are removed from pre-mRNA.

## 3.4.2 DNA and protein synthesis

*Refs: AQA 3.4.2 · Edexcel A 2.6, 2.7 · Edexcel B 1.4 iv to vi · OCR A 2.1.3(g) · OCR B 2.1.4(f), (g) · Eduqas CC5(k) to (n). Level: AS & A.*

### What you need to know

- The concept of the genome as the complete set of genes in a cell and of the proteome as the full range of proteins that a cell is able to produce.
- The structure of molecules of messenger RNA (mRNA) and of transfer RNA (tRNA).
- Transcription as the production of mRNA from DNA. The role of RNA polymerase in joining mRNA nucleotides. In prokaryotes, transcription results directly in the production of mRNA from DNA. In eukaryotes, transcription results in the production of pre-mRNA; this is then spliced to form mRNA.
- Translation as the production of polypeptides from the sequence of codons carried by mRNA. The role of ribosomes and tRNA, and the role of ATP in translation.
- Relate the base sequence of nucleic acids to the amino acid sequence of polypeptides, when provided with suitable data about the genetic code.
- Interpret data from experimental work investigating the role of nucleic acids.

### Explained

**Genome and proteome.** Every body cell has the same genome, but each cell uses only some of its genes, so different cells have different proteomes. One gene can give several proteins by alternative splicing (file 24), so the proteome can be larger than the number of genes.

**mRNA and tRNA.**

| | mRNA | tRNA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | Single, linear strand | Single strand folded into a clover-leaf shape |
| Hydrogen bonds | None | Yes, between paired bases within the molecule |
| Key part | Codons (triplets of bases complementary to the DNA template) | Anticodon (three bases complementary to an mRNA codon); amino acid binding site at one end |
| Length | Varies with gene | Short, about 80 nucleotides |

**Transcription** (in the nucleus).

1. DNA helicase unwinds the gene and breaks the hydrogen bonds between bases, exposing the template strand.
2. Free RNA nucleotides pair with complementary bases on the template strand (A with U, T with A, C with G, G with C).
3. RNA polymerase moves along the template, joining RNA nucleotides with phosphodiester bonds (condensation).
4. At a stop sequence, RNA polymerase detaches; pre-mRNA is released; the DNA rewinds.
5. In eukaryotes, the pre-mRNA is **spliced**: introns are removed and exons are joined to make mature mRNA.
6. mRNA leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore.

In prokaryotes there are no introns, so transcription makes mRNA directly, and translation can start before transcription finishes.

**Translation** (in the cytoplasm, at ribosomes).

1. A ribosome binds to the mRNA at the start codon.
2. Each tRNA carries a specific amino acid; attaching the amino acid to its tRNA uses ATP.
3. A tRNA with an anticodon complementary to the first codon pairs with it (hydrogen bonds). A second tRNA pairs with the next codon. The ribosome holds two tRNAs at once.
4. A peptide bond forms between the two amino acids (condensation, catalysed by an enzyme in the ribosome, using ATP).
5. The ribosome moves along one codon; the first tRNA is released and goes off to collect another amino acid.
6. This continues until a stop codon is reached; the polypeptide is released.
7. Several ribosomes can translate one mRNA at the same time (a polysome), making many copies quickly.

The polypeptide then folds into its secondary and tertiary structure and may be modified in the Golgi apparatus (file 04).

**Evidence for the role of nucleic acids.** Typical data: when mRNA and ribosomes are added to a cell-free mixture of amino acids, tRNA and ATP, polypeptides are made; if RNase (which hydrolyses RNA) is added, no polypeptide is made. Synthetic mRNA made only of U nucleotides (UUU...) produced a polypeptide of only phenylalanine, showing UUU codes for phenylalanine. This was how the code was cracked.

### Worked example

A section of DNA template strand reads: TAC GGA TTC CGT.

1. mRNA (complementary, U for A): AUG CCU AAG GCA.
2. tRNA anticodons (complementary to mRNA): UAC GGA UUC CGU.
3. Using a code table: AUG = Met, CCU = Pro, AAG = Lys, GCA = Ala.

The polypeptide begins Met–Pro–Lys–Ala.

Note that the anticodons have the same sequence as the DNA template, with U in place of T.

If a gene has 1200 bases in its exons and no stop codon is counted, how many amino acids does its polypeptide have? 1200 ÷ 3 = 400 (minus one if the last triplet is a stop codon).

### Common mistakes

- Using T in mRNA or tRNA.
- Saying RNA polymerase forms hydrogen bonds between the mRNA and DNA.
- Saying "the tRNA reads the codon" without mentioning complementary anticodon.
- Saying the ribosome makes the amino acids.
- Saying introns are translated.
- Writing "translation happens in the nucleus".

## Mark scheme

1. Any two: eukaryotic DNA long, linear, associated with histones / in chromosomes; prokaryotic DNA short, circular, not associated with proteins.
2. More than one triplet / codon codes for the same amino acid (1); a substitution may change the triplet into another for the same amino acid (1); so primary structure unchanged (1).
3. Joins RNA nucleotides together (1); by phosphodiester bonds / condensation, forming pre-mRNA (1).
4. Pre-mRNA contains introns (1); introns are removed by splicing (1).
5. Carries a specific amino acid (1); anticodon complementary to mRNA codon (1); brings amino acid to ribosome so that amino acids are joined in the right order (1).
6. CGA ACC TTT (1).
7. Different genes are expressed / switched on (1); so different proteins made (1).
8. Any two: tRNA clover-leaf shape vs mRNA linear; tRNA has hydrogen bonds between bases; tRNA has an anticodon / amino acid binding site; mRNA longer and has codons.

# Genome projects and gene technologies

Scientists can read whole genomes, copy and cut DNA, move genes between organisms, and compare people's DNA. This file covers genome sequencing, recombinant DNA technology (in vivo and in vitro cloning), gene therapy, DNA probes and screening, and genetic fingerprinting.

## 3.8.3 Using genome projects

*Refs: AQA 3.8.3 · Edexcel A 8.16 · Edexcel B 7.1 i · OCR A 6.1.3(a), (b) · OCR B 5.1.3(e), (f) · Eduqas C2.7(a) to (c), (h). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Sequencing projects have read the genomes of a wide range of organisms, including humans.
- Determining the genome of simpler organisms allows the sequences of the proteins that derive from the genetic code (the proteome) of the organism to be determined. This may have many applications, including the identification of potential antigens for use in vaccine production.
- In more complex organisms, the presence of non-coding DNA and of regulatory genes means that knowledge of the genome cannot easily be translated into the proteome.
- Sequencing methods are continuously updated and have become automated.

### Explained

**Genome:** all the DNA of an organism. The Human Genome Project (completed 2003) read the human genome of about 3 billion base pairs and found about 20,000 protein-coding genes. The 100,000 Genomes Project (England, completed 2018) sequenced whole genomes of patients with rare diseases and cancers, linking DNA to medical records (Eduqas).

**Simple organisms.** Bacteria have little non-coding DNA and no introns, so the proteome can be predicted directly from the genome. This helps identify antigens on pathogens for vaccines, and find genes for antibiotic resistance.

**Complex organisms.** Introns, non-coding regions, regulatory genes and alternative splicing mean the genome cannot simply be "translated" into the proteome.

**Sequencing methods** (OCR A).

- *Chain termination (Sanger):* DNA is copied using normal nucleotides plus a few modified, fluorescently labelled terminator nucleotides that stop the chain. Fragments of every length are made, separated by size in a capillary gel, and the colour of the last base of each read in order.
- *Next-generation (high-throughput):* millions of fragments are sequenced at the same time on a flow cell; automated, faster and much cheaper. A human genome can now be read in a day.

**Uses** (OCR A 6.1.3(b), Edexcel A 8.16, OCR B 5.1.3(e), Eduqas C2.7(h)).

- *Bioinformatics:* computer software and databases to store and compare sequences.
- *Comparing genomes:* between species to work out evolutionary relationships; between individuals to find alleles linked to disease.
- *Epidemiology:* tracking how pathogens spread and mutate (as during the COVID-19 pandemic).
- *Personalised medicine:* choosing the drug and dose that suits a person's genotype (some people metabolise drugs fast or slowly).
- *SNPs and haplotypes* (OCR B): a single nucleotide polymorphism is a single base that varies between people; a haplotype is a set of SNPs inherited together on one chromosome. SNP profiles can show disease risk and response to drugs.
- *Synthetic biology* (OCR A): designing and building new biological parts and systems, such as bacteria that make drugs.

**Ethics** (Eduqas C2.7(b)). Who owns genetic data; insurers or employers discriminating on genetic risk; consent; finding unexpected results (risk of a disease with no cure); privacy.

### Common mistakes

- Saying sequencing the genome tells you all the proteins in a complex organism.
- Confusing genome (DNA) with proteome (proteins).

## 3.8.4.1 Recombinant DNA technology

*Refs: AQA 3.8.4.1 · Edexcel A 6.4, 8.17, 8.18 · Edexcel B 7.1 ii, 7.4 i to vi · OCR A 6.1.3(d), (f) to (h) · OCR B 5.1.3(b), (c), (g) · Eduqas C2.7(d) to (g). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Recombinant DNA technology involves the transfer of fragments of DNA from one organism, or species, to another. Since the genetic code is universal, as are transcription and translation mechanisms, the transferred DNA can be translated within cells of the recipient (transgenic) organism.
- Fragments of DNA can be produced by several methods, including: conversion of mRNA to complementary DNA (cDNA), using reverse transcriptase; using restriction enzymes to cut a fragment containing the desired gene from DNA; creating the gene in a gene machine.
- Fragments of DNA can be amplified by in vitro and in vivo techniques.
- The principles of the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) as an in vitro method to amplify DNA fragments.
- The culture of transformed host cells as an in vivo method to amplify DNA fragments: the addition of promoter and terminator regions to the fragments of DNA; the use of restriction endonucleases and ligases to insert fragments of DNA into vectors; transformation of host cells using these vectors; the use of marker genes to detect genetically modified (GM) cells or organisms. (Students will not be required to recall specific marker genes.)
- Interpret information relating to the use of recombinant DNA technology; evaluate the ethical, financial and social issues associated with the use and ownership of recombinant DNA technology in agriculture, in industry and in medicine; balance the humanitarian aspects of recombinant DNA technology with the opposition from environmentalists and anti-globalisation activists; relate recombinant DNA technology to gene therapy.

### Explained

**Getting the gene.**

1. *Reverse transcriptase:* take mRNA from a cell that makes the protein (it has many copies, and no introns). Reverse transcriptase makes single-stranded cDNA; DNA polymerase makes it double-stranded.
2. *Restriction endonucleases:* enzymes from bacteria that cut DNA at specific recognition sequences (often palindromic, such as GAATTC). Some cut straight across (blunt ends); others make staggered cuts leaving single-stranded **sticky ends**, which can pair with complementary sticky ends.
3. *Gene machine:* the protein's amino acid sequence is used to work out a DNA sequence; a computer designs it and makes it from nucleotides in short overlapping pieces (oligonucleotides) that are joined. No introns; sequences can be adjusted.

**PCR (in vitro).**

1. Mix the DNA with free nucleotides, primers (short single-stranded DNA complementary to the start of each target strand) and a heat-stable DNA polymerase (Taq polymerase), in a buffer.
2. Heat to 95 °C: hydrogen bonds break; strands separate.
3. Cool to about 55 °C: primers anneal (bind) to the ends of the target sequence.
4. Heat to 72 °C: Taq polymerase adds nucleotides from the primers, copying each strand.
5. Repeat. Each cycle doubles the number of copies: after n cycles, 2ⁿ copies.

Advantages: very fast, automated, needs only a tiny sample, no living cells. Limitations: very sensitive to contamination; copies only short fragments; needs the sequence to make primers.

**Cloning in host cells (in vivo).**

1. Add a **promoter** (so RNA polymerase can bind and transcription starts) and a **terminator** (so it stops) to the gene.
2. Cut a **vector** (usually a bacterial plasmid, or a virus) with the same restriction enzyme, giving complementary sticky ends.
3. Mix the gene and the vector; their sticky ends pair; **DNA ligase** joins the sugar-phosphate backbones. This is recombinant DNA.
4. **Transformation:** mix plasmids with host bacteria and calcium ions, then heat-shock (or use electroporation) to make the membrane more permeable, so some bacteria take up the plasmid.
5. Only some bacteria take up the plasmid, and only some plasmids contain the gene. **Marker genes** identify them:
   - An antibiotic resistance gene on the plasmid: grow on agar with that antibiotic; only transformed bacteria survive.
   - The gene inserted into a second marker gene, disrupting it. Example: inserting into a second antibiotic resistance gene (replica plating, Edexcel B 7.4 iii: bacteria that grow on the first antibiotic but not the second have the recombinant plasmid); or into the *lacZ* gene (blue/white screening); or using a fluorescent protein gene (GFP) that glows under UV.
6. Culture the transformed bacteria in a fermenter; they make the protein.

In vivo cloning is more accurate (fewer copying errors), can copy long genes and makes the product as well; but it is slower and needs living cells.

**GM organisms** (Edexcel A 8.17, 8.18, Edexcel B 7.4, OCR A 6.1.3(f), (g), Eduqas C2.7(f)).

- *Microorganisms:* bacteria making human insulin; yeast making vaccines; bacteria making enzymes for industry.
- *Plants:* golden rice (genes for β-carotene, which the body converts to vitamin A); herbicide-resistant soya (Edexcel B 7.4 v: a gene from a soil bacterium makes an enzyme not affected by glyphosate, so farmers can spray weeds without harming the crop; more crop yield, but concern about herbicide-resistant weeds and reduced biodiversity); insect-resistant (Bt) crops. Plants are often transformed with *Agrobacterium tumefaciens*, a bacterium whose Ti plasmid enters plant cells.
- *Animals:* sheep or goats making human proteins (such as antithrombin) in their milk ("pharming"); knockout mice (Edexcel B 7.4 iv), in which a gene is deliberately inactivated to study its function or model a human disease.

**Issues.**

| For | Against |
| --- | --- |
| Higher crop yields, less pesticide; more food in poorer countries | Genes may spread to wild relatives (superweeds) |
| Nutritionally improved foods (golden rice) | Reduced biodiversity from monocultures |
| Cheap, plentiful medicines (insulin) | Large companies own patents; farmers may have to buy seed each year; poorer farmers disadvantaged (anti-globalisation concerns) |
| Disease-resistant crops and animals | Unknown long-term effects on health and ecosystems |
| Animal models for research | Animal welfare in GM and knockout animals |

**Gene therapy.** Inserting a functional allele into a person's cells to treat a genetic disorder.

- Recessive disorders (such as cystic fibrosis): add a working allele.
- Dominant disorders: silence the faulty allele (for example with RNAi or by inserting DNA into it).
- Vectors: modified viruses, liposomes (lipid spheres), or direct injection.
- *Somatic* gene therapy alters body cells only; it is not passed on; must be repeated because cells die.
- *Germ line* gene therapy alters gametes or embryos, so all cells and future offspring carry the change. Currently illegal in humans in the UK because of ethical concerns (unknown effects on future generations; consent; "designer babies").
- Problems: immune response to the vector; the allele may insert into another gene and cause cancer; the effect is short-lived; expensive.

### Worked example

Starting with one DNA molecule, how many copies are there after 20 PCR cycles? 2²⁰ = 1,048,576 (about a million).

If each cycle takes 4 minutes, how long do 30 cycles take? 30 × 4 = 120 minutes = 2 hours.

### Common mistakes

- Saying restriction enzymes "join" DNA. Ligase joins; restriction enzymes cut.
- Saying primers are "to start the PCR". They give DNA polymerase a starting point and define which section is copied.
- Saying human DNA polymerase is used in PCR. It would denature at 95 °C.
- Forgetting the promoter and terminator.
- Saying marker genes "make the gene work".

## 3.8.4.2 Differences in DNA between individuals of the same species can be exploited for identification and diagnosis of heritable conditions

*Refs: AQA 3.8.4.2. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- The use of labelled DNA probes and DNA hybridisation to locate specific alleles of genes.
- The use of labelled DNA probes that can be used to screen patients for heritable conditions, drug responses or health risks.
- The use of this information in genetic counselling and personalised medicine.
- Evaluate information relating to screening individuals for genetically determined conditions and drug responses.

### Explained

A **DNA probe** is a short, single-stranded piece of DNA with a base sequence complementary to part of the allele being looked for. It is labelled with a radioactive isotope (detected by X-ray film) or a fluorescent dye (detected under UV light).

1. The patient's DNA is extracted, cut into fragments and made single-stranded (heated).
2. The probe is added. If the allele is present, the probe binds to it by complementary base pairing: **hybridisation**.
3. Unbound probe is washed away. The label shows whether, and where, the allele is present.

**DNA microarrays** hold thousands of different probes on a slide, so many alleles can be tested at once.

**Uses.**

- Screening for heritable conditions (BRCA1 mutations, cystic fibrosis, Huntington's disease).
- Drug responses: choosing the drug that works for that person and avoiding ones that cause harmful reactions (personalised medicine).
- Health risks: alleles that increase the risk of heart disease or some cancers.
- Genetic counselling: explaining to people what results mean, the risks of passing on an allele, and the options (file 39).

**Evaluating screening.** Benefits: early diagnosis and treatment; informed decisions about having children; personalised drugs reduce side effects and wasted prescriptions. Concerns: anxiety from results, especially for conditions with no treatment; false positives and negatives; pressure on decisions about pregnancy; risk of discrimination; cost.

### Common mistakes

- Saying probes "attach to the protein".
- Saying a positive result means a person will definitely develop a disease (for risk alleles it shows increased probability).

## 3.8.4.3 Genetic fingerprinting

*Refs: AQA 3.8.4.3 · Edexcel A 6.3, CP14 · Edexcel B 3.1 iv, 7.1 ii · OCR A 6.1.3(c), (e) · OCR B 5.1.3(d) · Eduqas C2.7(d). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- An organism's genome contains many variable number tandem repeats (VNTRs). The probability of two individuals having the same VNTRs is very low.
- The technique of genetic fingerprinting in analysing DNA fragments that have been cloned by PCR, and its use in determining genetic relationships and in determining the genetic variability within a population.
- The use of genetic fingerprinting in the fields of forensic science, medical diagnosis, animal and plant breeding.

### Explained

**VNTRs** (also called short tandem repeats or minisatellites) are short base sequences in non-coding DNA repeated many times in a row. The number of repeats at each locus varies between people. Everyone inherits one set of repeats from each parent.

**Making a genetic fingerprint (DNA profile).**

1. Extract DNA from the sample (blood, hair root, saliva).
2. Amplify the VNTR regions by PCR.
3. Cut with restriction enzymes that cut just outside the repeats, so fragment length depends on the number of repeats. (In modern profiling, PCR primers flank the repeat, so cutting is not needed.)
4. **Gel electrophoresis:** load the fragments into wells in an agarose gel; apply an electric current. DNA is negatively charged (phosphate groups), so it moves towards the positive electrode. Smaller fragments move faster and further. A DNA ladder of known fragment sizes runs alongside.
5. Make the DNA visible: transfer to a nylon membrane and add labelled probes complementary to the VNTRs; or use fluorescent tags added during PCR; then view under UV or with X-ray film.
6. The pattern of bands is the genetic fingerprint. Compare patterns.

**Uses.**

- *Forensics:* matching DNA at a crime scene to suspects; excluding the innocent.
- *Paternity and relationships:* every band in a child must come from either the mother or the father.
- *Medical diagnosis:* identifying alleles linked to disease (for example, the number of repeats in the Huntington's gene).
- *Breeding:* checking pedigrees; avoiding inbreeding by choosing unrelated animals or plants.
- *Genetic variability:* comparing profiles across a population (more varied bands mean more genetic diversity; Eduqas C2.1(l)).
- *Classification* (Edexcel B 3.1 iv): comparing species.

### Worked example

A child's DNA profile has six bands. The mother's profile shares three of them. Of two possible fathers, man X shares two of the remaining three bands, and man Y shares all three. Which man is more likely to be the father? Man Y: every band not from the mother must come from the biological father.

On a gel, a ladder band of 500 base pairs moved 30 mm and one of 1000 bp moved 18 mm. A sample band moved 24 mm. Estimate its size. It lies between: roughly 700 bp (fragment size is not linear with distance; a plot of log size against distance is used for accuracy).

### Common mistakes

- Saying genetic fingerprinting uses genes. It uses non-coding VNTRs.
- Saying DNA moves to the negative electrode.
- Saying larger fragments move further.
- Saying identical twins have different fingerprints (they have the same).

### Practical

**Gel electrophoresis** (Edexcel A CP14; OCR A 6.1.3(e) and PAG6).

1. Pour molten agarose into a casting tray with a comb to form wells; let it set; place in the tank and cover with buffer.
2. Load DNA samples (pre-cut with restriction enzymes, mixed with loading dye) into the wells with a micropipette, with a DNA ladder in one well. Wells at the negative (cathode) end.
3. Run at a set voltage until the dye has moved most of the way.
4. Stain (for example with a safe DNA stain or methylene blue) and view.

Control: run a known DNA ladder; keep voltage and time the same. Safety: electrical hazard; some stains are toxic.

Organisms detect changes and respond to them. This file covers plant tropisms and IAA in outline, taxes, kineses and reflexes, receptors (the Pacinian corpuscle, rods and cones), and how the heartbeat is started and its rate controlled, with AQA required practical 10.

## 3.6.1.1 Survival and response

*Refs: AQA 3.6.1.1, RP10. Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Organisms increase their chance of survival by responding to changes in their environment.
- In flowering plants, specific growth factors move from growing regions to other tissues, where they regulate growth in response to directional stimuli.
- The effect of different concentrations of indoleacetic acid (IAA) on cell elongation in the roots and shoots of flowering plants as an explanation of gravitropism and phototropism in flowering plants.
- Taxes and kineses as simple responses that can maintain a mobile organism in a favourable environment.
- The protective effect of a simple reflex, exemplified by a three-neurone simple reflex in a mammal. The importance of simple reflexes in avoiding damage to the body.

### Explained

**Tropisms** are growth responses of plants towards or away from a directional stimulus. IAA (an auxin) is made in shoot and root tips and moves to the cells behind them.

- *Phototropism in shoots:* light from one side causes IAA to move to the shaded side. IAA makes shoot cells elongate (by loosening cell walls, in an acid-growth process). The shaded side grows faster, so the shoot bends towards light: positive phototropism.
- *Phototropism in roots:* IAA also moves to the shaded side, but in roots high IAA concentrations inhibit elongation. The shaded side grows less, so the root bends away from light: negative phototropism.
- *Gravitropism:* IAA gathers on the lower side. In shoots the lower side elongates more, so the shoot grows upwards (negative gravitropism). In roots the lower side elongates less, so the root grows downwards (positive gravitropism).

**Taxes and kineses.**

- A *taxis* is a directional movement towards (positive) or away from (negative) a stimulus. Examples: woodlice move away from light (negative phototaxis), keeping them hidden from predators and in damp places; single-celled algae move towards light.
- A *kinesis* is a non-directional response where the rate of movement or turning changes with the intensity of a stimulus. Example: woodlice in dry air move faster and turn less, so they are more likely to leave; in humid air they slow down and turn more, so they stay.

**Simple reflexes.** A reflex is a rapid, automatic response to a stimulus. The three-neurone reflex arc: receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone (in the spinal cord) → motor neurone → effector (muscle). Example: withdrawing a hand from a hot object. Because the pathway does not go through the brain's conscious areas and has few synapses, it is fast and protects the body from damage before the brain even becomes aware.

### Common mistakes

- Saying light "destroys" IAA on the lit side. The accepted explanation for AQA is that IAA moves to the shaded side.
- Saying IAA "inhibits growth" in all tissues. It promotes elongation in shoots and, at high concentration, inhibits it in roots.
- Calling a kinesis a "directional response".

### Practical

**AQA required practical 10**: investigation into the effect of an environmental variable on the movement of an animal using either a choice chamber or a maze.

*Choice chamber.* A dish divided into sections with a gauze platform. Put a drying agent (silica gel or anhydrous calcium chloride) under one side and water under the other to compare humidity; or cover half with black card to compare light. Place ten woodlice in the centre; after 10 minutes count how many are on each side. Repeat with fresh woodlice.

*Maze.* Record which way an animal (maggot, woodlouse) turns at a T-junction when one arm is dark or damp.

Controls: the same number and species of animal; the same time; only one factor changed (cover the whole chamber in the humidity experiment so light is the same); return animals after each trial. Analysis: a chi-squared test compares the observed numbers with those expected if there were no preference (file 21, file 26). Ethics: handle animals gently with a soft brush, keep them for a short time, return them to their habitat.

## 3.6.1.2 Receptors

*Refs: AQA 3.6.1.2 · Edexcel A 8.5 · Edexcel B 9.7 ii, iii · OCR A 5.1.3(a). Level: A-level only.*

### What you need to know

- Receptors respond only to specific stimuli. Stimulation of a receptor leads to the establishment of a generator potential.
- The basic structure of a Pacinian corpuscle as an example of a receptor. How a Pacinian corpuscle produces a generator potential as a result of a pressure stimulus, by the opening of stretch-mediated sodium ion channels.
- Differences in sensitivity to light, sensitivity to colour and visual acuity as explained by differences in the optic pigments of rods and cones and the connections rods and cones make in the optic nerve.

### Explained

Receptors are **transducers**: they convert energy from a stimulus (pressure, light, chemicals) into electrical energy, as a generator potential.

**Pacinian corpuscle.** Found deep in the skin (fingertips, soles) and joints; detects pressure. It is a sensory neurone ending wrapped in many layers of connective tissue (lamellae) separated by gel.

1. At rest, the stretch-mediated sodium ion channels in the neurone membrane are too narrow for Na⁺ to pass, so there is a resting potential.
2. Pressure deforms the lamellae, which press on the neurone ending and stretch its membrane.
3. The stretch-mediated sodium channels widen and Na⁺ diffuses in.
4. This depolarises the membrane: a generator potential.
5. If the generator potential reaches the threshold, an action potential is sent along the sensory neurone.

Stronger pressure opens more channels, giving a larger generator potential and a higher frequency of action potentials.

**Rods and cones.** Light-sensitive cells in the retina.

| | Rods | Cones |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pigment | Rhodopsin | Iodopsin (three types, each most sensitive to red, green or blue light) |
| Sensitivity to light | High: work in dim light | Low: need bright light |
| Colour | Black and white only | Colour vision |
| Connections | Many rods share one bipolar neurone (retinal convergence) | Each cone has its own bipolar neurone |
| Visual acuity | Low | High |
| Distribution | Mostly at the edges (periphery) of the retina | Concentrated in the fovea |

*Why rods are sensitive:* rhodopsin is broken down by low light intensity; several rods connect to one bipolar cell, so their small generator potentials add together (summation) to reach the threshold.

*Why rods give low acuity:* because several rods feed one neurone, the brain cannot tell which rod was stimulated, so two close points of light are seen as one.

*Why cones give high acuity:* each cone has its own route to the brain, so separate points are distinguished. But iodopsin needs high light intensity to be broken down, and each cone alone must reach threshold.

*Colour:* the brain compares how much each of the three cone types is stimulated.

**Rhodopsin** (Edexcel B, Edexcel A). Rhodopsin is retinal (a light-absorbing molecule) bound to opsin (a protein). Light changes retinal from *cis* to *trans*, so it no longer fits opsin and rhodopsin splits ("bleaching"). In the dark, sodium ions continually enter the rod and it releases an inhibitory neurotransmitter (glutamate) that stops the bipolar cell firing. When rhodopsin bleaches, a cascade closes sodium channels; the rod hyperpolarises and stops releasing glutamate, so the bipolar cell depolarises and an impulse is sent along the optic nerve. In bright light rhodopsin is bleached faster than it is remade, which is why it takes time to see after going into a dark room.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the Pacinian corpuscle detects "touch" without saying pressure deforms the membrane.
- Saying cones are found at the edge of the retina.
- Saying rods are more sensitive "because they have more pigment" rather than because of convergence.
- Saying generator potentials are "all or nothing". Action potentials are; generator potentials vary in size.

## 3.6.1.3 Control of heart rate

*Refs: AQA 3.6.1.3 · Edexcel A 7.8, 7.9 · Edexcel B 4.4 iv, v, 9.8 i · OCR A 3.1.2(g), (h), 5.1.5(k) · OCR B 2.2.1(d), (e), (h), 5.3.1(b) · Eduqas C3.2(c). Level: A-level only (AS for OCR A, OCR B and Edexcel B on the initiation of the heartbeat and ECGs).*

### What you need to know

- Myogenic stimulation of the heart and transmission of a subsequent wave of electrical activity. Roles of the sinoatrial node (SAN), atrioventricular node (AVN) and bundle of His.
- The roles and locations of chemoreceptors and pressure receptors and the roles of the autonomic nervous system and effectors in controlling heart rate.

### Explained

**Myogenic.** Cardiac muscle contracts on its own, without nerve impulses. The nervous system only changes the rate.

**The sequence.**

1. The SAN, in the wall of the right atrium (the pacemaker), sends out a wave of electrical activity (excitation) at regular intervals.
2. The wave spreads across both atria, which contract (atrial systole).
3. A layer of non-conducting tissue between the atria and ventricles stops the wave passing directly to the ventricles.
4. The AVN picks up the wave and, after a short delay (about 0.1 s), passes it on. The delay lets the atria empty fully before the ventricles contract.
5. The wave passes down the bundle of His (in the septum) to the base (apex) of the ventricles.
6. It spreads up through Purkyne (Purkinje) fibres in the ventricle walls, so the ventricles contract from the bottom up, pushing blood up into the arteries.

**Changing the rate.** The cardiac control centre is in the medulla oblongata of the brain. It sends impulses along two parts of the autonomic nervous system:

- Sympathetic nerves (accelerator) to the SAN increase the rate (and force).
- Parasympathetic nerves (the vagus nerve, inhibitory) to the SAN decrease the rate.

Receptors:

| Receptor | Location | Detects | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chemoreceptors | Aorta, carotid arteries, medulla | Rise in CO₂ concentration (fall in blood pH), as during exercise | More impulses to the medulla, more sympathetic impulses to the SAN, heart rate rises; extra CO₂ is removed faster in the lungs |
| Pressure receptors (baroreceptors) | Aorta and carotid arteries | Rise in blood pressure | More parasympathetic impulses to the SAN, heart rate falls |
| | | Fall in blood pressure | More sympathetic impulses, heart rate rises |

Adrenaline from the adrenal glands also raises heart rate (OCR A 5.1.5(k), Edexcel B 9.8 ii; file 32).

**Electrocardiograms** (OCR A, OCR B, Edexcel A, Edexcel B). Electrodes on the skin record the electrical activity of the heart.

- P wave: depolarisation (excitation) of the atria, leading to atrial systole.
- QRS complex: depolarisation of the ventricles, leading to ventricular systole. Large, because the ventricles have more muscle.
- T wave: repolarisation of the ventricles (diastole).
- The P–R interval includes the AVN delay.

Abnormal traces:

- *Tachycardia:* heart rate over 100 beats per minute at rest.
- *Bradycardia:* under 60 beats per minute (normal in trained athletes).
- *Ectopic heartbeat:* an early, extra beat, followed by a pause.
- *Atrial fibrillation:* atria twitch irregularly instead of contracting; no clear P waves and an irregular rhythm of QRS complexes.
- A raised or depressed S–T segment can show a heart attack (damage to ventricle muscle).

### Worked example

On an ECG the paper moves at 25 mm s⁻¹ and the distance between two R peaks is 20 mm. Calculate the heart rate.

1. Time per beat = 20 ÷ 25 = 0.8 s.
2. Heart rate = 60 ÷ 0.8 = 75 beats min⁻¹.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the brain starts each heartbeat.
- Saying the AVN "slows the heart rate". It delays the wave so atria empty first.
- Saying the SAN is in the left atrium, or that the bundle of His carries blood.
- Saying chemoreceptors detect oxygen in the AQA answer: the main stimulus at A-level is CO₂ / pH.
- Mixing up which branch of the autonomic system speeds the heart.

# OCR practical activity groups (PAGs)

OCR does not set fixed required practicals like AQA. Instead it groups the apparatus and techniques every student must use into **practical activity groups (PAGs)**. Each separate science has eight PAGs; Combined Science has sixteen. Schools choose the activities, but written papers can ask about any of the techniques, and about the practicals suggested in the specification. About 15% of exam marks test practical skills.

Most PAGs use the same techniques as an AQA required practical, and the method, risks and analysis are in the AQA notes (each PAG below links to them through the OCR map). This file says what each PAG covers, gives a typical activity, and writes out in full the practicals that AQA does not have.

The PAG names are the same in Gateway (A) and Twenty First Century (B). Gateway writes them with the subject letter (PAG B1, PAG C1, PAG P1); the separate Twenty First Century specs write PAG1 to PAG8 inside each subject. The Combined Science specs (J250 and J260) use their own sixteen groups.

### Board differences

- AQA lists fixed required practicals; OCR lists apparatus and techniques and suggests activities. The AQA required practical linked from each OCR map row uses the same techniques.
- OCR B outcome B4.1.5 (J257 and J260) asks students to describe investigations into how different substrates affect the rate of respiration in yeast, which is written out in Biology PAG 6 below.
- The OCR combined groups B2 to B5 and C1 to C5 are numbered differently from the separate groups, so always check which list a question means. This file uses the separate-science names, with the combined code in brackets.

### Explained

**PAG 6: Respiration in yeast with different substrates (OCR B B4.1.5).** Yeast respires sugars and releases carbon dioxide. The rate of carbon dioxide release shows the rate of respiration.

1. Make up a yeast suspension and equal-concentration solutions of different sugars (for example glucose, sucrose, fructose, lactose).
2. Mix equal volumes of yeast suspension and one sugar solution in a boiling tube. Stand it in a water bath at a fixed temperature (about 35 °C) for a few minutes so it reaches that temperature.
3. Fit a bung and delivery tube. Either collect the gas in an upturned measuring cylinder or gas syringe, or count bubbles through water in a set time.
4. Measure the volume of gas in a fixed time (for example 5 minutes). Repeat for each sugar, and do a control with water instead of sugar.

Control: the same mass of yeast, volume and concentration of sugar, temperature and time. Yeast usually respires glucose and fructose fastest, sucrose more slowly (it must first be broken down) and lactose hardly at all (yeast lacks the enzyme to digest it).

**PAG 6: Respirometers.** A respirometer measures oxygen uptake by small organisms such as woodlice or germinating seeds. The organisms sit on a gauze above a chemical that absorbs carbon dioxide (such as soda lime or potassium hydroxide). As they use oxygen, the gas volume in the tube falls, and a drop of coloured liquid in a capillary tube moves towards them. Distance moved per minute (or volume, from the tube's cross-section) gives the rate. A second tube with glass beads instead of organisms is the control, to correct for temperature and pressure changes.

**PAG 6: Exercise and pulse rate.** Measure resting pulse (beats in 15 seconds × 4), exercise for a fixed time (such as 2 minutes of step-ups), then measure pulse every minute until it returns to resting. The recovery time is a measure of fitness.

**Ethics with living organisms.** Use the smallest number of animals needed, keep them in their habitat conditions, handle them gently, return them to where they were found, and never use vertebrates for experiments that harm them. Students' own data (pulse, reaction time) are used only with their consent.

### Common mistakes

- Not letting the tubes reach the water-bath temperature before starting timing.
- Forgetting the control tube in a respirometer, so temperature changes are mistaken for oxygen uptake.
- In yeast experiments, changing the mass of sugar instead of keeping the concentration the same.
- Counting bubbles when bubbles are different sizes; a gas syringe gives more accurate volumes.

### Explained

**PAG 1: Reactivity trend.** Two common ways to put metals in order of reactivity:

- *Temperature rise with acid.* Add the same mass of each metal powder to the same volume and concentration of dilute hydrochloric acid in an insulated cup, and record the maximum temperature rise. A more reactive metal gives a larger rise. Use powders of similar particle size, and only metals that react safely (magnesium, zinc, iron, copper; never sodium or potassium with acid).
- *Displacement.* Put a piece of each metal into solutions of the other metals' salts. If the metal is coated or the solution changes colour (or the temperature rises), the added metal is more reactive and has displaced the other. Record results in a grid and count the reactions for each metal.

Halogen displacement (chlorine, bromine and iodine solutions with halide solutions) is another reactivity trend: a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from its salt, shown by a colour change.

**PAG 4: Distillation.**

- *Simple distillation* separates a liquid from a dissolved solid (water from salty water). Heat the flask; the water boils, the vapour is cooled in a Liebig condenser (cold water in at the bottom, out at the top, so the jacket stays full), and the pure liquid drips into a beaker. The salt stays in the flask. The thermometer bulb sits at the side arm, to read the temperature of the vapour that is distilling.
- *Fractional distillation* separates liquids with close boiling points, such as ethanol (78 °C) and water (100 °C). A fractionating column packed with glass beads sits on the flask. Vapour condenses and re-boils many times up the column, so the vapour at the top is mostly the lower-boiling liquid. Collect the liquid while the thermometer reads near 78 °C; change the beaker when the temperature rises.
- Safety: use anti-bumping granules for smooth boiling, never heat a sealed apparatus, and use an electric heater or water bath for flammable liquids like ethanol.

**PAG 5: Identification of species.** Use clean nichrome wire (dipped in acid and heated until there is no colour) for flame tests; add sodium hydroxide solution drop by drop for metal hydroxide precipitates; acidify with dilute nitric acid before adding silver nitrate (halides) or with hydrochloric acid before adding barium chloride (sulfate), so carbonate ions do not give a false precipitate. Details are in Chemistry 4.8.3.

**PAG 6: Titration.** Rinse the burette with the solution it will hold, fill it with the funnel removed, and read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level. Add the alkali from a pipette (using a pipette filler) to a conical flask on a white tile, add a few drops of indicator, then add acid quickly for a rough titre, and then slowly, drop by drop near the end point, for accurate titres. Repeat until two titres agree within 0.10 cm³ (concordant) and take their mean.

### Common mistakes

- Placing the thermometer bulb in the boiling liquid during distillation instead of at the side arm.
- Running condenser water in at the top, which leaves air in the jacket and poor cooling.
- Using a dirty flame-test wire, so colours from earlier tests contaminate the result.
- Including the rough titre in the mean.

### Explained

**PAG 3: Motion with light gates.** A light gate is a beam of light and a sensor. When a card of known length (an "interrupt card") on a trolley passes through, the data logger times how long the beam is blocked.

speed = card length ÷ time the beam is blocked

With two light gates (or a double interrupt card), the logger finds two speeds and the time between them, and so calculates acceleration. Light gates remove human reaction time, which is the main error when using a stopwatch over short times.

**PAG 7: Series and parallel circuits.** Build each circuit from a circuit diagram and check it before switching on. In series, put ammeters at different points to show the current is the same everywhere, and voltmeters across each component to show the p.d.s add up to the supply p.d. In parallel, show that the currents in the branches add up to the current from the supply and that the p.d. across each branch is the same.

**PAG 8: Interactions of waves.**

- *Reflection.* Shine a ray from a ray box at a plane mirror. Mark the incident and reflected rays, draw the normal, and measure the angles of incidence and reflection. They are equal.
- *Refraction.* Trace round a rectangular glass block, shine a ray in at an angle, and mark where it enters and leaves. Join the points to show the ray inside the block. Measure the angle of incidence and the angle of refraction from the normal. The ray bends towards the normal entering the glass (it slows down) and away from the normal on leaving.
- *Infrared.* Fill a Leslie cube with hot water and measure the infrared from each face with an infrared detector at the same distance; matt black radiates most, shiny silver least (Physics 4.6.3.1).

Use a narrow ray, a darkened room, and a sharp pencil to mark ray positions, because wide rays and thick lines are the main source of uncertainty in the angles.

### Common mistakes

- Measuring angles from the surface of the block or mirror instead of from the normal.
- Counting a light gate's card length wrongly (use the length that actually blocks the beam).
- Leaving a circuit switched on between readings, so the wire heats up and its resistance rises.

## Check questions

1. A student investigates how different sugars affect the respiration of yeast. Name two variables that must be controlled, and describe how the rate of respiration is measured. [4 marks]
2. Explain why a respirometer has a control tube containing glass beads. [2 marks]
3. Describe how fractional distillation separates a mixture of ethanol and water. [4 marks]
4. A student adds equal masses of four metal powders to equal volumes of the same acid. The temperature rises are: zinc 9 °C, magnesium 31 °C, copper 0 °C, iron 5 °C. Put the metals in order of reactivity and explain why the powders should be of similar size. [3 marks]
5. In a titration a student records titres of 24.60, 23.85, 23.95 and 23.90 cm³. Which results should be used, and what is the mean titre? [2 marks]
6. A 5.0 cm card on a trolley blocks a light gate for 0.125 s. Calculate the speed of the trolley, and give one advantage of a light gate over a stopwatch. [3 marks]
7. Describe how to measure the angle of refraction for a ray of light entering a glass block. [4 marks]

## Mark scheme

1. Any two controlled: mass or volume of yeast; concentration of sugar; volume of solution; temperature; time (2). Rate measured by volume of carbon dioxide collected in a gas syringe or measuring cylinder in a fixed time / bubbles counted per minute (2).
2. To show any change in volume caused by temperature or pressure changes (1), so these can be allowed for / the volume change in the test tube is due only to the organisms (1).
3. Mixture heated in a flask with a fractionating column (1). Ethanol has the lower boiling point (78 °C) (1). Vapour repeatedly condenses and re-boils up the column, so mostly ethanol reaches the top (1). Ethanol is condensed in the condenser and collected while the temperature stays near 78 °C (1).
4. Magnesium > zinc > iron > copper (1). Larger temperature rise means faster / more vigorous reaction (1). Surface area affects rate, so a fair test needs similar particle size (1).
5. 23.85, 23.95 and 23.90 (concordant; the 24.60 rough titre is left out) (1). Mean = 23.90 cm³ (1).
6. Speed = 0.050 ÷ 0.125 (1) = 0.40 m/s (1). Light gate removes human reaction time / more precise timing (1).
7. Trace round the block on paper (1). Shine a narrow ray from a ray box at an angle and mark the ray entering and leaving (1). Join the points to show the ray inside the block and draw the normal where it enters (1). Measure the angle between the refracted ray and the normal with a protractor (1).

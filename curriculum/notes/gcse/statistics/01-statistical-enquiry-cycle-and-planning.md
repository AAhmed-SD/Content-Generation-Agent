# The statistical enquiry cycle and planning

This file covers the five stages of the statistical enquiry cycle (SEC), writing a hypothesis, the constraints on an investigation, and how to plan ahead for things that go wrong. Every exam paper on both boards has questions set inside the SEC, often a longer question that walks through several stages of one investigation.

## Overview

All of this file is on both tiers.

### Board differences

- AQA 8382: planning is Section A (3.1.1 A1 to 3.1.3 A3). The SEC is 3.6, split into 3.6.1 to 3.6.5. Each AQA paper ends with an SEC question.
- Edexcel 1ST0: planning is 1a.01 to 1a.03. The SEC is a separate numbered list of five stages before the content tables (written SEC 1 to SEC 5 here). Edexcel orders its whole content list to follow the cycle.
- Neither board needs a formal null hypothesis.

## The statistical enquiry cycle

*Refs: AQA 3.6, 3.6.1 to 3.6.5 · Edexcel SEC 1 to SEC 5. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- The cycle has five stages:
  1. Initial planning: choose a question or hypothesis; decide what data to collect, how to collect and record it, and how to process and show it, with reasons.
  2. Data collection: know the limits on getting primary data and on finding secondary data (from reference books, the internet and the media); acknowledge sources; know that sensitive topics can make data hard to get.
  3. Processing and presentation: organise the data (often with technology), draw diagrams, and calculate measures to compare; know that software can automate this.
  4. Interpretation: read the diagrams and measures in context; reach conclusions that answer the hypothesis; make inferences or predictions; discuss how reliable the findings are; communicate clearly for the audience.
  5. Evaluation and review: find weaknesses in the method or presentation; suggest improvements; refine the process to test the hypothesis further.
- The cycle is a loop: what you learn at stage 5 feeds a new or sharper question at stage 1.

### Explained

A statistical investigation is never just "do a calculation". It starts with a question worth asking and ends by asking whether the answer can be trusted. The cycle makes this a routine.

Think of a student who wonders whether people who sleep more do better in tests.

- Planning: hypothesis "Students who sleep more hours score higher in maths tests." Data needed: hours of sleep (continuous) and test score (discrete) for each student. Plan: a scatter diagram and a correlation coefficient.
- Collection: a questionnaire for sleep (primary) and the school's test records (secondary, needs permission because they are confidential).
- Processing: enter both into a spreadsheet, remove impossible entries (25 hours of sleep), draw the scatter diagram.
- Interpretation: "There is weak positive correlation, so there is some evidence that students who sleep more score higher, but sleep was self-reported."
- Evaluation: "The sample was one class. Next time, sample from every year group and use a sleep diary instead of memory."

Interpretation must use the context. "The median is higher" earns little; "the median time for Year 11 is 4 minutes higher, so Year 11 students typically took longer" earns the mark.

Reliability of findings depends on the sample size, how the sample was chosen and how the data was measured. A conclusion should be no stronger than the data allows: "suggests" not "proves".

Communication: a report for the public might use a simple bar chart; one for a statistician might use box plots and standard deviations.

### Common mistakes

- Conclusions that do not refer back to the hypothesis ("The mean is 6.2" with no "so the hypothesis is supported").
- Evaluations that just say "use a bigger sample" with no reason. Say why: a larger sample reduces the effect of unusual values and makes results more reliable.
- Forgetting that stage 5 leads back to stage 1.

## 3.1.1 / 1a.01 Hypotheses

*Refs: AQA 3.1.1 (A1) · Edexcel 1a.01. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- A hypothesis is a statement that can be tested, for example "older cars are worth less".
- It can only be tested by collecting suitable data and analysing it.
- A formal null hypothesis is not needed.

### Explained

A question ("Do older cars cost less?") becomes a hypothesis when it is written as a statement that data can support or not support. A good hypothesis:

- names the variables (age of car, price);
- says the direction of the effect (as age goes up, price goes down);
- can be measured (age in years, price in pounds).

"Cars are nicer when new" is not testable because "nicer" is not measured.

The data you collect must match the hypothesis. To test "boys have faster reaction times than girls" you need reaction times for both boys and girls, collected the same way.

You never "prove" a hypothesis with a sample. You say the data supports it or does not support it.

### Worked example

A student wants to find out whether taller people have longer arm spans. Write a hypothesis and say what data is needed.

1. Hypothesis: "The taller a person is, the longer their arm span."
2. Data: height and arm span for each person in a sample, both in cm.
3. Analysis: a scatter diagram, with height on the x-axis, and a description of the correlation.

### Common mistakes

- Writing a question instead of a statement.
- A hypothesis with no direction ("height affects arm span" is weaker than "taller people have longer arm spans").
- Saying the data "proves" the hypothesis.

## 3.1.2 / 1a.02 Constraints on an investigation

*Refs: AQA 3.1.2 (A2) · Edexcel 1a.02. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- Constraints can include time, cost, ethical issues, confidentiality and convenience.
- Give examples of each in context.

### Explained

- Time: a survey of every school in the country would take too long, so you sample.
- Cost: paying interviewers, printing questionnaires or buying data.
- Ethics: you must not harm people or animals, must get consent, and must not pressure people. Testing a drug on people without telling them is unethical.
- Confidentiality: some data (salaries, medical records, exam results) is private and may not be released, or must be kept anonymous.
- Convenience: the easiest data to get is not always the right data; data may only be available in a form that does not quite match the question.

Other practical constraints: data you need may not exist, may be out of date, or may be measured differently from what you want.

### Worked example

A company wants to test the hypothesis "staff who work from home earn more". Give two constraints.

1. Confidentiality: salaries are private, so staff may refuse to give them or the company may not be allowed to share them.
2. Time or cost: collecting data from every member of staff takes time, so a sample might be needed.

### Common mistakes

- Naming a constraint without linking it to the context of the question.

## 3.1.3 / 1a.03 Planning for problems

*Refs: AQA 3.1.3 (A3) · Edexcel 1a.03. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- Plan ahead for problems such as: the population is hard to identify, people do not respond, or results are unexpected.
- Suggest a practical strategy for each.

### Explained

| Problem | Strategy |
| --- | --- |
| No list of the population (for example, all dog owners in a town) | Find a sampling frame that is close (vet registers), or use quota sampling. |
| Non-response to a questionnaire | Follow up with reminders, make it short, offer a reward, use face-to-face interviews, or select a larger sample at the start. |
| Sensitive questions | Make the questionnaire anonymous; use the random response method (Higher, see file 04). |
| Unexpected outcomes (strange values) | Check for recording errors; repeat the measurement; run a pilot first. |
| Not enough time | Pilot first so the main survey runs smoothly; use a sample not a census. |

A pilot survey is a small trial run. It finds unclear questions, tests how long things take and gives an idea of likely answers before the full survey.

### Worked example

A survey is posted to 400 households and only 90 are returned. Give two things the researcher could do.

1. Send a reminder to the households that did not reply, or visit them.
2. Explain that the 90 who replied may not represent all 400 (people who reply may have stronger views), so treat the results with caution, or send out more questionnaires to a fresh random sample.

### Common mistakes

- Ignoring non-response, as if the people who replied are the same as those who did not.

## Check questions

1. Write down the five stages of the statistical enquiry cycle in order. [2 marks]
2. A student asks "Do people like sport?" Explain why this is not a hypothesis, and rewrite it as one that can be tested. [2 marks]
3. A researcher wants to test "children who eat breakfast concentrate better in morning lessons". Give two constraints the researcher might face. [2 marks]
4. A gym sends an online survey to its 2000 members and gets 150 replies. Give one reason the results may be biased and one way to improve the response rate. [2 marks]
5. What is a pilot survey, and give one reason for doing one. [2 marks]
6. Results of a survey show that the median travel time to work is 35 minutes in Town A and 22 minutes in Town B. The hypothesis was "people in Town A travel for longer". Write a conclusion. [1 mark]
7. Suggest one improvement for a survey about screen time that asked 20 students in one Year 10 class. [2 marks]

## Mark scheme

1. Planning, collecting data, processing and presenting, interpreting, evaluating (all five in order, 2; four or five with one swap, 1).
2. It is a question, or "like" is not measurable (1). For example: "People who play sport at least once a week rate their enjoyment of sport higher than those who do not" or "More than half of adults play sport each week" (1).
3. Any two, in context (1 each): time; cost; ethics (consent from parents, cannot stop children eating breakfast); confidentiality of school records; difficulty measuring concentration.
4. Members who replied may be those with strong views, or those who use email often, so they may not represent all members (1). Reminder email, shorter survey, small reward, ask in person at the gym (1).
5. A small trial run of the survey before the main one (1). Checks questions are clear / checks how long it takes / tests the method (1).
6. Town A's median is 13 minutes higher, so the data supports the hypothesis that people in Town A typically travel for longer (1).
7. Use a larger sample (1) from all year groups or chosen at random, so results represent the whole school and are more reliable (1).

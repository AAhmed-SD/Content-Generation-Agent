# Population and sampling

This file covers populations, sampling frames and samples, and the sampling methods: judgement, opportunity (convenience), cluster, quota, simple random, systematic and stratified.

## Overview

All on both tiers. (HT only) On Edexcel, stratifying by more than one category at once is Higher only.

### Board differences

- AQA 8382: 3.2.8 (B3a) to 3.2.13 (B3f). AQA names judgement, opportunity, random, systematic and quota sampling and stratification; it does not name cluster sampling. AQA says you will not have to actually select a sample using dice, cards or random numbers in the exam, only show you understand how.
- Edexcel 1ST0: 1c.01 to 1c.06. Edexcel also names cluster sampling (with judgement, opportunity and quota). Edexcel may ask you to select items using random numbers, including dealing with repeats and numbers out of range, and to calculate strata sizes. (HT only) Stratifying by two categories at once (for example gender and year group) is Higher only on Edexcel.
- Both boards: stratification is something done before sampling, not a sampling method on its own.

## 3.2.8 / 1c.01 Population, sampling frame and sample

*Refs: AQA 3.2.8 (B3a) · Edexcel 1c.01. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- Population: everything or everyone that could be studied for the investigation.
- Sampling frame: a list of the members of the population, from which a sample is chosen.
- Sample: the members actually chosen.
- A census collects data from every member of the population.
- Not every member of the population may be available for sampling: the sampling frame may be incomplete.

### Explained

| | Census | Sample |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Advantages | Unbiased; complete picture; every member included | Cheaper; quicker; less data to process; the only option if testing destroys the item |
| Disadvantages | Expensive; slow; hard to reach everyone; impossible if testing destroys items | May not represent the population; results vary from sample to sample |

Sampling frames are rarely perfect. A school register is a good sampling frame for students at that school. The electoral roll misses people who are not registered to vote, so it is a poor frame for "all adults in a town".

### Worked example

A factory makes 5000 light bulbs a day. It wants to know how long they last. Should it use a census or a sample? Suggest a sampling frame.

1. A sample: testing a bulb's lifetime means using it until it fails, so a census would destroy every bulb.
2. Sampling frame: a numbered list of the bulbs produced that day (for example, by serial number).

### Common mistakes

- Mixing up the sampling frame (the list) and the sample (those chosen).
- Forgetting the "destructive testing" reason for sampling.

## 3.2.9 / 1c.02 Meaning of population in context

*Refs: AQA 3.2.9 (B3b) · Edexcel 1c.02. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- "Population" means whatever group the question is about: it need not be people.

### Explained

The population could be all employees in one office, all women in the UK, all the trees in a park or all the cans made by a machine in a week. Defining it clearly is the first step in planning, because it decides the sampling frame and what conclusions can be drawn. A sample of Year 7 students tells you about Year 7, not the whole school.

### Worked example

A council wants to know what residents of a village think of a new bus route. State the population.

1. All residents of the village (perhaps all adult residents, if only adults are asked). Visitors and people from other villages are not part of it.

### Common mistakes

- Defining the population too broadly ("everyone") or too narrowly (only bus users, when the question asks about all residents).

## 3.2.10 / 1c.03 Judgement, opportunity, cluster and quota sampling

*Refs: AQA 3.2.10 (B3c) · Edexcel 1c.03. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- Judgement sampling: the researcher uses their knowledge to choose members they think are typical.
- Opportunity (convenience) sampling: the first people available who fit, such as people walking past.
- Cluster sampling: the population is split into natural groups (clusters, for example schools or streets); some clusters are chosen at random and everyone (or a sample) within them is surveyed.
- Quota sampling: the researcher must find a set number from each group (for example 20 men and 20 women) but chooses who freely.
- Reasons for using them: cheap, quick, convenient, no sampling frame needed.
- Risks: they are not random, so they can be biased.

### Explained

These methods are popular because random sampling needs a full list and can be slow.

- Judgement depends on the researcher's opinion; a different researcher might pick very different people.
- Opportunity sampling at a station at 8 am finds commuters and misses those who work from home.
- Cluster sampling saves travel: survey 5 schools fully rather than a few students in 200 schools. But the chosen clusters may differ from the rest.
- Quota sampling matches the proportions of the population (like stratified sampling), but within each group the interviewer chooses, so they may pick friendly-looking people.

### Worked example

A market researcher stands outside a supermarket and asks the first 30 men and first 30 women who walk past about their shopping habits. Name the method and give one source of bias.

1. Quota sampling (fixed numbers of men and women, interviewer chooses).
2. Bias: only people who shop at that supermarket, at that time of day, are asked; people who shop online are missed.

### Common mistakes

- Thinking quota sampling is random. It is not.
- Confusing cluster sampling (whole groups chosen) with stratified sampling (some from every group).

## 3.2.11 / 1c.04 Random, systematic and quota sampling to avoid bias

*Refs: AQA 3.2.11 (B3d) · Edexcel 1c.04. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- Choose a sampling method that suits the problem and avoids bias.
- Simple random sampling: every member has the same chance of being chosen.
- Systematic sampling: choose a random starting point, then take every kth member from a list, where k = population size ÷ sample size.
- Systematic and quota sampling are generally not random.
- Systematic sampling can be biased if the list has a repeating pattern that matches the interval.
- Know the advantages and disadvantages of each method.

### Explained

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple random | Unbiased; every member equally likely | Needs a full sampling frame; may by chance under-represent a group; can be slow to reach chosen people |
| Systematic | Simple and quick; spreads the sample through the list | Needs a list; biased if the list has a pattern with the same period |
| Quota | Quick; no sampling frame; matches proportions of groups | Not random; interviewer bias; non-response is replaced, which hides it |
| Stratified (see 3.2.13) | Each group fairly represented | Needs details of the strata and a frame for each |

A pattern problem: a factory machine makes a fault every 10th item. Sampling every 10th item could pick all faulty items or none.

### Worked example

A club has 480 members listed alphabetically. Describe how to take a systematic sample of 30.

1. k = 480 ÷ 30 = 16.
2. Choose a random start from 1 to 16, for example 7.
3. Take members 7, 23, 39, 55, … (adding 16 each time) until 30 members are chosen. The last is 7 + 29 × 16 = 471.

### Common mistakes

- Starting at member 1 every time: the start must be random.
- Saying systematic sampling is random. Only the start is random.

## 3.2.12 / 1c.05 Simple random samples

*Refs: AQA 3.2.12 (B3e) · Edexcel 1c.05. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- In a simple random sample every member, and every possible group of the sample size, is equally likely to be chosen.
- Methods: number every member of the sampling frame, then choose numbers using dice, cards or names drawn from a hat, random number tables or lists, or a calculator or computer random number function.
- Ignore random numbers that are repeated or out of range.

### Explained

Steps for a random sample:

1. Get a sampling frame and number every member (for 300 members, 001 to 300).
2. Generate random numbers of the right length (three digits here).
3. Ignore 000, any number above 300, and repeats.
4. Continue until you have the sample size.

On a calculator, a random integer function (RanInt or similar) gives whole numbers in a range directly.

### Worked example

A class has 60 students numbered 01 to 60. Use these random digits, read in pairs from the start, to choose 4 students:

72 15 60 08 15 93 41 00 27

1. 72: out of range, ignore.
2. 15: choose student 15.
3. 60: choose student 60.
4. 08: choose student 8.
5. 15: repeat, ignore.
6. 93: out of range, ignore.
7. 41: choose student 41.
8. Sample: 15, 60, 8, 41.

### Common mistakes

- Including out-of-range numbers or repeats.
- Numbering with different lengths (1, 2 … 60) and reading digits in pairs, so 6 and 60 get confused. Use 01, 02 … 60.

## 3.2.13 / 1c.06 Stratification

*Refs: AQA 3.2.13 (B3f) · Edexcel 1c.06. Tier: F & H (part Higher only on Edexcel).*

### What you need to know

- Stratification splits the population into groups (strata) such as gender or age group before sampling, so that each group is represented in proportion to its size.
- It is not a sampling method on its own: you still choose within each stratum, usually at random.
- Use it when the strata are likely to give different answers.
- Strata size = (size of stratum ÷ size of population) × sample size.
- (HT only, Edexcel) Stratify by more than one category at once, such as gender and year group.

### Explained

If a school is 60% girls, a stratified sample makes 60% of the sample girls. A simple random sample might by chance be 75% girls.

After rounding, the strata sizes must still add up to the sample size. If rounding makes them total one too many or too few, adjust the stratum whose decimal part was closest to .5.

### Worked example

A sports centre has 240 members aged under 18, 520 aged 18 to 59 and 140 aged 60 or over. A stratified sample of 50 is needed. How many from each age group?

1. Total 240 + 520 + 140 = 900.
2. Under 18: 240/900 × 50 = 13.33 → 13.
3. 18 to 59: 520/900 × 50 = 28.89 → 29.
4. 60 or over: 140/900 × 50 = 7.78 → 8.
5. Check: 13 + 29 + 8 = 50.
6. Then choose 13 under-18s at random from the list of under-18 members, and so on.

(HT only, Edexcel) Two categories: a year group has 70 boys and 50 girls in Year 10, and 60 boys and 60 girls in Year 11. A sample of 40 stratified by year and gender:

1. Total 240.
2. Year 10 boys: 70/240 × 40 = 11.67 → 12. Year 10 girls: 50/240 × 40 = 8.33 → 8.
3. Year 11 boys: 60/240 × 40 = 10. Year 11 girls: 10.
4. Check: 12 + 8 + 10 + 10 = 40.

### Common mistakes

- Dividing the sample equally between strata instead of in proportion.
- Calling stratification a sampling method and stopping there, without saying how members are chosen within each stratum.
- Rounding so the total is not the sample size.

## Check questions

1. Give two reasons why a sample might be used instead of a census. [2 marks]
2. Explain the difference between a sampling frame and a sample. [2 marks]
3. A company has 1200 employees listed by staff number. Describe how to take a systematic sample of 40. [3 marks]
4. Give one reason why systematic sampling might give a biased sample. [1 mark]
5. Students are numbered 001 to 450. Using the random digits 512 087 450 087 239 961 314, starting at the left, write down the first four students chosen. [2 marks]
6. A town has 3600 households in Area P, 2100 in Area Q and 1300 in Area R. A stratified sample of 70 households is taken. How many are taken from each area? [3 marks]
7. Explain one difference between quota sampling and stratified random sampling. [1 mark]
8. A researcher picks 4 of a county's 30 primary schools at random and questions every teacher in those 4 schools. Name this sampling method and give one disadvantage. [2 marks]

## Mark scheme

1. Any two (1 each): cheaper; quicker; less data to process; testing may destroy items; population too large or not all reachable.
2. Sampling frame: the list of members of the population (1). Sample: the members chosen from it to take part (1).
3. 1200 ÷ 40 = 30 (1); choose a random start between 1 and 30 (1); then take every 30th employee after that (1).
4. The list may have a repeating pattern with the same interval, so the chosen items may all share a feature (1).
5. 087 (1 for ignoring 512 as out of range and starting correctly); 087, 450, 239, 314 (1, ignoring the repeat 087 and 961).
6. Total 7000 (1). P: 3600/7000 × 70 = 36; Q: 21; R: 13 (1 for one correct, 2 for all three, total 70).
7. In quota sampling the interviewer chooses who to ask within each group, so it is not random; in stratified random sampling members of each stratum are chosen at random (1).
8. Cluster sampling (1). The 4 schools chosen may not be typical of all 30 / teachers in one school may share views (1).

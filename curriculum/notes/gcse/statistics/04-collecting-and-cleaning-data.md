# Collecting and cleaning data

This file covers where data comes from (experiments, simulation, questionnaires, observation, reference sources, census and sampling), reliability and validity, bias and sensitive questions, designing questions and data collection sheets, interviews, pilots, problems in collected data, cleaning data, and controlling extraneous variables.

## Overview

All on both tiers except: (HT only) level of control as a way to reduce distortion; (HT only) control groups; and, on Edexcel, (HT only) the random response technique and matched pairs.

### Board differences

- AQA 8382: 3.2.5 (B2b), 3.2.6 (B2c), 3.2.7 (B2d), 3.2.14 (B4), 3.2.15 (B5a), 3.2.16 (B5b), 3.2.17 (B5c). AQA's numbering puts sampling (3.2.8 to 3.2.13, file 03) between them.
- Edexcel 1ST0: 1d.01 to 1d.07, in the same order as here. Edexcel's Higher guidance names the random response technique (1d.03) and matched pairs (1d.07), and asks about pilots for questionnaires and pre-tests for experiments (1d.04).

## 3.2.5 / 1d.01 Sources of data

*Refs: AQA 3.2.5 (B2b) · Edexcel 1d.01. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- Data can come from: experiments (laboratory, field, natural), simulation, questionnaires, observation, reference sources, a census of the population, or a sample.
- Sources of secondary data must be acknowledged (say where it came from).
- Know the strengths and weaknesses of each in context, and be able to design a data collection sheet.

### Explained

Experiments:

- Laboratory: carried out in controlled conditions. Easy to control other variables and repeat, but may be artificial.
- Field: in the real world (a test of a new fertiliser on a real farm). More realistic, but harder to control other variables.
- Natural: the researcher does not control the explanatory variable; it changes naturally (comparing health before and after a town's factory closes). Useful when it would be unethical or impossible to change things deliberately, but many other variables change too.

Simulation: a model that imitates a real situation, often using random numbers (for example, a random number from 1 to 10 where 1 to 3 means "the bus is late" to model a 30% chance). Useful when the real thing is too slow, costly or dangerous.

Questionnaires: cheap for large groups, but there is a risk of non-response and misunderstood questions.

Observation: recording what is seen (counting cars that pass). Accurate for behaviour, but slow, and people may act differently if they know they are watched.

Reference sources: books, websites, official statistics. Quick, but secondary.

A data collection sheet is a table with tally and frequency columns, or columns for each variable, designed so that every result fits exactly one place.

### Worked example

Design a data collection sheet to record the number of passengers in cars passing a school between 8 am and 9 am.

| Number of people in car | Tally | Frequency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | | |
| 2 | | |
| 3 | | |
| 4 | | |
| 5 or more | | |

The "5 or more" row means every car fits somewhere.

Simulation: a shop finds 20% of customers pay in cash. Describe how to simulate 10 customers with random digits.

1. Let digits 0 and 1 mean "cash" (2 out of 10 = 20%), and 2 to 9 mean "card".
2. Take 10 random digits; count the 0s and 1s.

### Common mistakes

- Forgetting a final open class ("5 or more"), so some results have nowhere to go.
- Mixing up field and natural experiments: in a field experiment the researcher still controls the explanatory variable.

## 3.2.6 / 1d.02 Reliability and validity

*Refs: AQA 3.2.6 (B2c) · Edexcel 1d.02. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- Reliability: getting similar results when the measurement or survey is repeated.
- Validity: measuring what you intended to measure.

### Explained

A scale that gives 61 kg, 61 kg and 61 kg for the same person is reliable. If the scale is set wrong and the person is really 64 kg, the results are reliable but not valid.

Asking students "How many hours did you revise last week?" may not be valid for "how hard do they work", because people overestimate, and revision time is not the same as effort.

Ways to improve:

- Reliability: larger samples, repeat measurements, standard instructions, consistent equipment.
- Validity: ask about exactly what the hypothesis needs, measure directly rather than by memory, define terms clearly.

### Worked example

A researcher measures fitness by asking people how often they go to the gym. Comment on validity.

1. Gym visits are not the same as fitness: some people exercise elsewhere, and visiting a gym does not show how fit someone is.
2. A more valid measure would be a direct fitness test, such as recovery heart rate after a set exercise.

### Common mistakes

- Using "reliable" and "valid" as if they mean the same.

## 3.2.7 / 1d.03 Bias, sensitivity and distortion

*Refs: AQA 3.2.7 (B2d) · Edexcel 1d.03. Tier: F & H (part Higher only).*

### What you need to know

- Bias is anything that makes results systematically differ from the truth.
- Sources: non-random samples, leading questions, people giving answers they think are acceptable, sensitive topics, interviewer influence, non-response.
- Sensitive questions (income, health, illegal behaviour) may get false answers or no answer. Reduce this with anonymous questionnaires, sealed answers or grouped answer options.
- Know how to minimise distortion of data.
- (HT only) Level of control: in an experiment, keeping other conditions fixed reduces distortion.
- (HT only, Edexcel) The random response technique for sensitive questions.

### Explained

(HT only) Random response technique: each person privately does something random, such as tossing a coin. If heads, they answer the sensitive question truthfully; if tails, they just answer "yes". Nobody knows which, so people are more honest. The researcher knows that about half the people answered "yes" because of the coin, and works back to an estimate.

### Worked example

(HT only) 200 people are asked "Have you ever cheated in a test?" Each tosses a coin: heads, answer truthfully; tails, say "yes". There are 130 "yes" answers. Estimate the proportion who have cheated.

1. Expected tails: 100 people, all saying "yes".
2. The other 30 "yes" answers came from the 100 who got heads.
3. Estimated proportion who have cheated: 30 ÷ 100 = 0.3, or 30%.

### Common mistakes

- In the random response method, dividing by all 200 instead of the 100 who answered honestly.

## 3.2.14 / 1d.04 Planning data collection: questions and interviews

*Refs: AQA 3.2.14 (B4) · Edexcel 1d.04. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- Avoid leading questions (that push towards an answer) and biased sources.
- Think about time factors: when and how long the collection takes, and questions about time periods.
- Open questions let people answer freely; closed questions give fixed options.
- Interview types: face-to-face, telephone, online; structured (fixed questions) or unstructured.
- Pilot survey: a small trial of a questionnaire. Pre-test: a trial run of an experiment.
- Design suitable questions and response boxes.

### Explained

Good closed questions have a time frame, clear wording and response boxes that do not overlap and cover every possible answer.

Poor: "How much TV do you watch? A little / Some / A lot"
Better: "On a typical school day, how many hours of TV do you watch? 0 to less than 1 / 1 to less than 2 / 2 to less than 3 / 3 or more"

| | Open questions | Closed questions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Good | Detailed answers; nothing missed | Quick to answer; easy to analyse |
| Bad | Hard to analyse; slow | Limit answers; options may not fit |

| Interview | Good | Bad |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Face-to-face | High response; can explain questions | Costly; interviewer may influence answers; less honest on sensitive questions |
| Telephone | Cheaper; quick | Many refuse; no visual aids |
| Online / postal | Cheap; anonymous | Low response; questions cannot be explained |

### Worked example

Criticise this question and write a better one: "Don't you agree that the canteen food is too expensive? Yes / No"

1. It is leading ("Don't you agree") and only allows yes or no.
2. Better: "How would you rate the prices in the canteen? Very cheap / Cheap / About right / Expensive / Very expensive"

### Common mistakes

- Response boxes that overlap (0–1, 1–2) or leave gaps.
- No time frame ("How often do you exercise?").

## 3.2.15 / 1d.05 Problems with collected data

*Refs: AQA 3.2.15 (B5a) · Edexcel 1d.05. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- Problems include missing data, incorrect formats, non-responses and incomplete responses.
- Know ways to deal with them.

### Explained

- Missing values: leave the item out of calculations that need that value (and say so), or go back and collect it. Do not just treat it as zero.
- Incorrect formats: "five", "5 hrs" and "5" mean the same; convert them to one format. Dates written in different ways need standardising.
- Non-response: follow up, or take a bigger initial sample, and consider whether non-responders differ.
- Incomplete responses: use the answered questions but report the different totals for each question.

### Worked example

In a survey of heights, some entries are "1.62", "158", "5 ft 4" and blank. What should be done?

1. Convert all to the same unit, such as cm: 162, 158, about 163.
2. Leave the blank out of calculations (so n is one smaller) and record that it was missing.

### Common mistakes

- Replacing a missing value with 0, which drags the mean down.

## 3.2.16 / 1d.06 Cleaning data

*Refs: AQA 3.2.16 (B5b) · Edexcel 1d.06. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- Data may need cleaning before processing: removing or correcting errors, impossible values, extra symbols (£, %, units) that stop software treating values as numbers, and duplicate entries.
- Spot errors using totals (rows and columns that should add up) and by checking values are consistent with each other.
- Outliers may be genuine or errors; check before removing (see file 08).

### Explained

On a spreadsheet, a cell containing "£12" or "12 kg" is often treated as text, so it is left out of a SUM or AVERAGE without any warning. Strip the symbols and keep units in the column heading instead.

### Worked example

A table shows Year 10 students' choices.

| | Art | Music | Drama | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Boys | 18 | 12 | 9 | 39 |
| Girls | 21 | 17 | 14 | 50 |
| Total | 39 | 29 | 23 | 89 |

Find the error.

1. Girls row: 21 + 17 + 14 = 52, not 50.
2. Totals column: 39 + 50 = 89, which matches the grand total. Art column: 18 + 21 = 39 ✓. Music: 12 + 17 = 29 ✓. Drama: 9 + 14 = 23 ✓.
3. Column totals add to 39 + 29 + 23 = 91, not 89. So the girls' total should be 52 and the grand total 91. The error is in the girls' total.

### Common mistakes

- Only checking one direction (rows) and not columns.

## 3.2.17 / 1d.07 Extraneous variables and control groups

*Refs: AQA 3.2.17 (B5c) · Edexcel 1d.07. Tier: F & H (part Higher only).*

### What you need to know

- An extraneous variable is any variable other than the explanatory variable that might affect the response. Identify and control it (keep it the same) so it does not distort results.
- (HT only) A control group gets no treatment (or the usual one) and is compared with the treatment group.
- (HT only, Edexcel) Matched pairs: each person in the treatment group is paired with a similar person (same age, gender, fitness) in the control group.

### Explained

Testing whether a new fertiliser increases yield: extraneous variables include the amount of water, sunlight, soil type and plant variety. Keep these the same for every plant.

(HT only) Without a control group you cannot tell whether a change was due to the treatment or would have happened anyway. Matched pairs make the two groups as alike as possible, so differences are more likely due to the treatment.

### Worked example

(HT only) A school tests whether a revision app improves test scores. Describe how to use a control group and matched pairs.

1. Choose students and split them into two groups. One group uses the app (treatment), the other revises as usual (control).
2. Matched pairs: pair students with similar previous test scores; one of each pair goes into each group (chosen at random).
3. Compare the improvement in scores between the groups; keep the test, time allowed and timing the same for both.

### Common mistakes

- Naming an extraneous variable without saying how to control it.

## Check questions

1. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of a laboratory experiment compared with a field experiment. [2 marks]
2. Explain the difference between reliability and validity. [2 marks]
3. Write down two things wrong with this question: "How much do you spend on lunch? £0–£2 / £2–£4 / £4–£6" [2 marks]
4. Give one advantage of an online questionnaire and one disadvantage. [2 marks]
5. In a data set of ages of club members, one entry is "2O" (with the letter O) and one is 340. Explain how each should be dealt with. [2 marks]
6. (HT only) 400 people take part in a random response survey. Each rolls a fair dice: on a 6 they answer "yes"; otherwise they answer truthfully. There are 140 "yes" answers. Estimate the percentage of people for whom the true answer is "yes". [3 marks]
7. (HT only) Explain why a control group is used in an experiment to test a new medicine. [1 mark]
8. Describe how to use random numbers to simulate whether it rains on each day of a week, where the chance of rain on any day is 0.4. [2 marks]

## Mark scheme

1. Advantage: easier to control other variables / easier to repeat (1). Disadvantage: artificial, may not reflect real behaviour (1).
2. Reliability: repeat measurements give similar results (1). Validity: measures what it is meant to measure (1).
3. Overlapping classes (£2 is in two groups) (1); no option above £6 / no time frame (per day? per week?) (1).
4. Advantage: cheap / quick / anonymous / reaches many people (1). Disadvantage: low response rate / only people online / cannot explain questions (1).
5. "2O" is a typing error: change it to 20 if that is clearly meant (1). 340 is impossible: check the original record, or remove it from calculations (1).
6. Expected "yes" from rolling a 6: 400 × 1/6 ≈ 66.7 (1). Truthful answers: 400 − 66.7 = 333.3 people, of whom 140 − 66.7 = 73.3 said yes (1). 73.3 ÷ 333.3 ≈ 22% (1).
7. To compare with the treatment group, so any difference can be put down to the medicine and not to other factors (1).
8. Let digits 0, 1, 2, 3 mean rain and 4 to 9 mean no rain (1); generate 7 random digits, one for each day (1).

# Topic 13 Exploring starlight (Paper 2)

Almost everything we know about stars comes from their light. This topic covers brightness and the magnitude scale, the distance modulus, spectra and spectral types, the Hertzsprung-Russell (HR) diagram, measuring distance with parallax, HR diagrams and Cepheids, variable and binary stars, star clusters, star trails, digital detectors, and astronomy across the electromagnetic spectrum from the ground and from space.

Equation (given): distance modulus M = m + 5 − 5 log d, with d in parsecs.

## 13.1, 13.2 and 13.3 Magnitudes and the distance modulus

### What you need to know

- The astronomical magnitude scale and how apparent magnitude relates to brightness as seen from Earth.
- The term absolute magnitude.
- Use M = m + 5 − 5 log d to find the absolute (M) or apparent (m) magnitude, given distance d in parsecs.

### Explained

The magnitude scale was started by Hipparchus, who called the brightest stars "first magnitude" and the faintest visible "sixth magnitude". Features of the modern scale:

- It runs backwards: smaller (or more negative) numbers mean brighter. Sirius is −1.5, Vega about 0, Polaris 2.0; the naked-eye limit at a dark site is about +6.
- It is logarithmic: a difference of 5 magnitudes is exactly a factor of 100 in brightness. So 1 magnitude is a factor of about 2.5 (2.512, the fifth root of 100).
- Very bright objects have negative magnitudes: Venus up to about −4.6, the full Moon −12.7, the Sun −26.7.

Apparent magnitude (m) is how bright a star looks from Earth. It depends on both the star's actual luminosity and its distance.

Absolute magnitude (M) is the apparent magnitude the star would have if it were at a standard distance of 10 parsecs. It allows the true brightness (luminosity) of stars to be compared fairly. The Sun's absolute magnitude is about +4.8: from 10 pc away it would be a faint naked-eye star.

The distance modulus relates them: M = m + 5 − 5 log d. If d = 10 pc, log 10 = 1, so M = m. Stars further than 10 pc have m > M (look fainter than they would at 10 pc).

### Worked example

1. A star has m = 6.0 at d = 100 pc. M = 6.0 + 5 − 5 log 100 = 6.0 + 5 − 10 = 1.0.
2. A star with M = −2.0 is at 250 pc. Rearrange: m = M − 5 + 5 log d = −2.0 − 5 + 5 × 2.398 = −2.0 − 5 + 11.99 = 5.0. Just visible to the naked eye.
3. Star A has m = 1; star B has m = 6. B is 5 magnitudes fainter, so A appears 100 times brighter.

### Common mistakes

- Saying a larger magnitude means brighter.
- Using log to base e (ln) instead of log₁₀.
- Saying absolute magnitude is how bright a star looks from Earth.

## 13.4, 13.5, 13.6 and 13.21 Stellar spectra and spectral types

### What you need to know

- The information a stellar spectrum gives: chemical composition, temperature and radial velocity.
- Stars can be classified by spectral type.
- A star's colour and spectral type are related to its surface temperature.
- How astronomers obtain and study the pattern of spectral lines from astronomical objects.

### Explained

Obtaining spectra (13.21): a spectroscope or spectrograph on a telescope spreads the light into its component wavelengths using a prism or, more often, a diffraction grating. The spectrum is recorded on a digital sensor. A star's spectrum is a continuous rainbow crossed by dark absorption lines, where cooler gases in the star's outer layers absorb particular wavelengths. Hot thin gas clouds (nebulae) give emission lines (bright lines on a dark background). The pattern is compared with spectra of elements measured in the laboratory.

Information from a spectrum:

- Chemical composition: each element absorbs a unique set of wavelengths, like a fingerprint. Matching the lines identifies the elements present. Helium was discovered in the Sun's spectrum before it was found on Earth. Most stars are about 73% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass.
- Temperature: hotter objects emit more strongly at shorter wavelengths, so the peak of the continuous spectrum (and the colour) shows the surface temperature. The strengths of certain lines also depend on temperature.
- Radial velocity: if the star moves towards or away from us, all its lines are shifted to shorter (blueshift) or longer (redshift) wavelengths by the Doppler effect. The size of the shift gives the speed along the line of sight. Used in binary stars and exoplanet detection (12.4) and cosmology (16.3).

Spectral types: stars are classified by the lines in their spectra into types O, B, A, F, G, K, M, in order of decreasing temperature (each type subdivided 0 to 9, so the Sun is G2).

| Type | Colour | Surface temperature (approx.) | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| O | Blue | Above 30 000 K | Stars in Orion's Belt (Alnitak) |
| B | Blue-white | 10 000 to 30 000 K | Rigel |
| A | White | 7500 to 10 000 K | Sirius, Vega |
| F | Yellow-white | 6000 to 7500 K | Procyon |
| G | Yellow | 5200 to 6000 K | The Sun (5800 K) |
| K | Orange | 3700 to 5200 K | Arcturus, Aldebaran |
| M | Red | Below 3700 K | Betelgeuse, Proxima Centauri |

### Common mistakes

- Saying red stars are hottest. Red stars are the coolest; blue the hottest.
- Saying dark lines are caused by gaps in the star. They are absorption by cooler gas.

## 13.7, 13.8 and 13.13 The Hertzsprung-Russell diagram

### What you need to know

- Sketch a simple HR diagram with labelled axes, showing main sequence stars, the Sun, red and blue giants, white dwarfs and supergiants.
- How a star's life cycle relates to its position on the HR diagram, for stars of about the Sun's mass and much more massive stars.
- Use an HR diagram to determine distances to stars.

### Explained

The HR diagram plots stars' luminosity (or absolute magnitude) on the vertical axis against surface temperature (or spectral type, or colour) on the horizontal axis. Conventions:

- Vertical: luminosity increases upwards (absolute magnitude becomes more negative upwards), often in units of the Sun's luminosity on a logarithmic scale (10⁻⁴ to 10⁶).
- Horizontal: temperature decreases from left to right (hot O and B stars on the left, about 30 000 K or more; cool M stars on the right, about 3000 K).

Regions:

- Main sequence: a diagonal band from top left (hot, bright, massive blue stars) to bottom right (cool, dim, low-mass red dwarfs). About 90% of stars are here, fusing hydrogen in their cores.
- The Sun: on the main sequence, in the middle (G2, 5800 K, luminosity 1, M = +4.8).
- Red giants: upper right of the main sequence: cool but very luminous, because they are huge.
- Blue giants: upper left, hot and very luminous.
- Supergiants: across the very top (red supergiants such as Betelgeuse at top right; blue supergiants such as Rigel at top left).
- White dwarfs: lower left: hot but very dim, because they are tiny.

Life cycles on the diagram (14.9 and 14.10):

- Sun-like star: a protostar moves onto the main sequence, where it stays about 10 billion years; it then moves up and to the right to become a red giant; after losing its outer layers (planetary nebula) its core moves to the lower left as a white dwarf, then slowly cools and fades towards the bottom right (black dwarf).
- Much more massive star: joins the main sequence near the top left, stays only millions of years, then moves to the right across the top as a red supergiant, before exploding as a supernova; the remnant (neutron star or black hole) is not on the normal diagram.

Distances from the HR diagram (spectroscopic parallax): take a main sequence star's spectrum to find its spectral type; read its absolute magnitude M from the main sequence on the HR diagram; measure its apparent magnitude m; then use the distance modulus to find d. This works for stars too far for parallax. Similarly, for a whole cluster, fitting its main sequence to the standard one gives the cluster's distance.

### Worked example

A main sequence star has spectral type A0, which on the HR diagram corresponds to M = +0.6. Its apparent magnitude is 10.6. m − M = 10.0 = 5 log d − 5, so log d = 3, d = 1000 pc.

### Common mistakes

- Drawing temperature increasing to the right.
- Putting white dwarfs at the top. They are hot but faint, so bottom left.
- Saying stars move along the main sequence as they age. They stay in roughly one place on it during hydrogen burning.

## 13.9 The inverse square law

### What you need to know

- The inverse square relationship between distance and brightness (intensity).

### Explained

Light from a star spreads out in all directions over the surface of an ever-larger sphere. The area of a sphere is proportional to r². So the brightness (intensity, power received per square metre) falls as 1 ÷ distance²: twice as far means 1/4 as bright; three times as far, 1/9; ten times as far, 1/100.

This is why apparent magnitude depends on distance, and why a factor of 10 in distance changes the magnitude by 5 (a factor of 100 in brightness).

### Worked example

A star is moved (in imagination) from 10 pc to 40 pc. Distance × 4, so brightness × 1/16. 16 is about 2.5³, so it would be about 3 magnitudes fainter.

### Common mistakes

- Halving the brightness when the distance doubles.

## 13.10, 13.11 and 13.12 Angles, parsecs and parallax

### What you need to know

- One degree is 60 arcminutes (60′) and one arcminute is 60 arcseconds (60″).
- The term parsec.
- Determine astronomical distances using heliocentric parallax.

### Explained

Small angles: 1° = 60′, 1′ = 60″, so 1° = 3600″. The full Moon is about 30′ (half a degree) across; the eye's resolution is about 1′; good ground-based telescopes resolve about 1″.

Heliocentric parallax: as the Earth orbits the Sun, a nearby star appears to shift its position against much more distant background stars. Photograph the star six months apart, when the Earth is on opposite sides of its orbit (a baseline of 2 AU). The parallax angle p is defined as half the total apparent shift (the angle corresponding to a 1 AU baseline).

The parsec ("parallax second") is the distance at which a star has a parallax of exactly 1 arcsecond. 1 pc = 3.26 light years = 3.1 × 10¹³ km.

Distance in parsecs = 1 ÷ parallax in arcseconds: d = 1 ÷ p.

Even the nearest star, Proxima Centauri, has p = 0.77″ (d = 1.3 pc). Parallaxes are so small that they were not measured until 1838 (Bessel, 61 Cygni). Ground-based parallax works to about 100 pc; the Hipparcos and Gaia satellites, above the blurring atmosphere, measure parallaxes far more accurately, out to thousands of parsecs.

### Worked example

1. A star shifts by a total of 0.40″ between January and July. p = 0.20″. d = 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5.0 pc = 16.3 light years.
2. Convert 0.5° to arcseconds: 0.5 × 3600 = 1800″.

### Common mistakes

- Using the full shift instead of half of it.
- Saying a larger parallax means a further star. Larger parallax means closer.

## 13.14 to 13.18 Variable stars, binaries and clusters

### What you need to know

- Light curves of short- and long-period variables, eclipsing binaries, Cepheids, novae and supernovae.
- Why the light curve of an eclipsing binary varies.
- How Cepheid variables are used to determine distances.
- The structure of gravitationally bound groups such as binary stars and clusters.
- How the period of an eclipsing binary is found from its light curve.

### Explained

A light curve is a graph of a star's brightness (magnitude, with brighter upwards) against time.

| Type | Light curve | Cause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short-period variable | Regular repeating changes over hours to days | Often pulsating stars or close binaries |
| Long-period variable (such as Mira) | Large, fairly regular changes over months to a year or more | Pulsating red giants that swell and shrink |
| Eclipsing binary | Mostly steady, with sharp regular dips | Two stars orbiting each other edge-on to us, one passing in front of the other |
| Cepheid variable | Regular, with a steep rise and slower fall; periods of about 1 to 100 days | Giant star pulsating in size and temperature |
| Nova | Sudden rise of many magnitudes in days, then slow fading over months | A white dwarf in a close binary pulls hydrogen from its companion; the hydrogen builds up and explodes in a runaway fusion reaction on its surface |
| Supernova | Huge sudden rise (can outshine a galaxy), then fading over months | Explosion of a massive star or the total destruction of a white dwarf |

Eclipsing binaries (13.15 and 13.18): the two stars orbit their common centre of mass. When the dimmer star passes in front of the brighter one, a deep primary minimum occurs; when the brighter star passes in front of the dimmer one, a shallower secondary minimum. Between eclipses the combined light is steady. The period of the system is the time between one primary minimum and the next (not between a primary and a secondary). The depth and shape of the dips give information on the stars' relative sizes and brightnesses. Algol in Perseus dims every 2.87 days.

Cepheids (13.16): Henrietta Leavitt found (1912) that Cepheids with longer periods are more luminous: the period-luminosity relationship. So: measure a Cepheid's period from its light curve; read its absolute magnitude from the period-luminosity graph; measure its apparent magnitude; use the distance modulus to find its distance. Cepheids are very luminous and can be seen in other galaxies, so they are "standard candles" for measuring distances far beyond parallax range. Edwin Hubble used them to show that the Andromeda Galaxy is far outside the Milky Way.

Binary stars and clusters (13.17):

- Binary stars: two stars bound by gravity, orbiting a common centre of mass. Visual binaries can be seen as two stars in a telescope. (An optical double is two stars that only appear close because they lie in the same direction, at different distances.) Most stars are in binary or multiple systems.
- Open clusters: loose groups of tens to thousands of young stars that formed together from the same cloud, found in the disc of the Galaxy, such as the Pleiades and the Hyades. They gradually drift apart.
- Globular clusters: dense, spherical groups of hundreds of thousands of very old stars, found in the halo around the Galaxy, such as M13 in Hercules. Tightly bound by gravity.

### Worked example

A Cepheid has a period of 10 days, which from the period-luminosity graph gives M = −4.0. Its apparent magnitude is 11.0. m − M = 15 = 5 log d − 5, so log d = 4 and d = 10 000 pc (10 kpc).

Eclipsing binary: primary minima are observed at day 0.0, day 3.2 and day 6.4; a shallower minimum at day 1.6. The period is 3.2 days.

### Common mistakes

- Taking the time between a primary and secondary minimum as the period.
- Saying open clusters are older than globular clusters. Globular clusters are much older.
- Mixing up novae (surface explosion on a white dwarf, star survives) and supernovae.

## 13.19 Star trails and the sidereal day

### What you need to know

- Use star-trail photographs to determine the length of the sidereal day.

### Explained

A camera on a fixed tripod, pointed at the celestial pole with its shutter open, records stars as arcs (star trails) around the pole as the Earth rotates. In a full sidereal day the stars would trace complete circles (360°). Method (task B12): take a long exposure of known duration t. Measure the angle θ of the arcs at the pole with a protractor. Then sidereal day = 360° ÷ θ × t. For accuracy, use long exposures and measure several trails (or take two short exposures hours apart and measure the rotation angle of the star pattern between them).

### Worked example

A 2.0-hour exposure produces trails of 30.1°. Sidereal day = (360 ÷ 30.1) × 2.0 h = 23.9 h = 23 h 55 min. (Accepted value 23 h 56 min.)

### Common mistakes

- Measuring the length of the trail in mm instead of the angle at the pole.

## 13.20 Digital sensors

### What you need to know

- Most modern observations are recorded with digital sensors that convert light into electrical signals, which can be processed and stored as data files.

### Explained

CCD (charge-coupled device) and CMOS sensors contain millions of tiny light-sensitive pixels. Light hitting each pixel releases electrons; the charge collected is proportional to the amount of light. The charges are read out, converted to numbers and stored as an image file.

Advantages over the eye and photographic film: very sensitive (they record most of the photons that arrive, compared with a few per cent for film); can make long exposures to build up faint images; the response is linear, so brightness can be measured accurately (photometry); images can be processed digitally (stacking, adjusting brightness and contrast, false colour) and shared instantly; they can record infrared and ultraviolet as well as visible light. Cooling the sensor reduces electronic noise.

### Common mistakes

- Saying digital sensors make objects brighter. They collect and record light more efficiently.

## 13.22 to 13.33 Observing across the electromagnetic spectrum

### What you need to know

- The Earth's atmosphere blocks almost all wavelengths except visible light and radio waves.
- Only optical and radio telescopes should be located at sea level.
- How a simple radio telescope works; why radio telescopes need very large apertures; how several radio telescopes work together as an aperture synthesis array.
- Radio astronomy's importance in discovering quasars, jets from black holes, the structure of the Milky Way and protoplanetary discs.
- Why some infrared telescopes can operate at high-altitude sites; infrared astronomy's importance in discovering protostars, dust and molecular clouds, and hotspots on moons.
- The damaging effect of the atmosphere on images from ground-based telescopes.
- Why telescopes working outside the optical and radio windows must be sited above the atmosphere.
- Advantages and disadvantages of space telescopes and orbiting platforms.
- How gamma-ray, X-ray and ultraviolet astronomy have been important in discovering gamma-ray bursts, black hole accretion discs and the structure of the corona and chromosphere of young stars.

### Explained

Atmospheric windows (13.22, 13.23): the atmosphere absorbs gamma rays, X-rays and most ultraviolet (absorbed high up by oxygen and ozone), and most infrared (absorbed by water vapour and carbon dioxide), and microwaves at some wavelengths. Only visible light (the optical window) and a wide range of radio waves (the radio window) reach sea level. So only optical and radio telescopes can work at sea level.

Radio telescopes (13.24 to 13.26): a simple radio telescope is a large curved (parabolic) metal dish that reflects radio waves to a focus, where an antenna (receiver) converts them into an electrical signal; the signal is amplified, recorded and processed by computers into a map of the sky. The dish can be a mesh as long as its holes are much smaller than the wavelength. Radio telescopes can be used day and night and through cloud.

Resolution is proportional to wavelength ÷ diameter (11.22). Radio wavelengths (cm to m) are about a million times longer than visible light, so to resolve as finely as an optical telescope, a radio dish would need to be a million times wider. So radio telescopes are made as large as possible (Lovell 76 m; FAST in China 500 m), and still have poor resolution on their own.

Aperture synthesis: several radio dishes spread over a large area (kilometres, or even across continents) observe the same object at the same time; their signals are combined by computer. The array has the resolution of a single dish as wide as the largest separation between dishes. As the Earth rotates, the dishes' positions relative to the source change, filling in the picture. Examples: the Very Large Array (USA), e-MERLIN (UK), ALMA (Chile), and the Event Horizon Telescope, an Earth-sized array that imaged the shadow of the black hole in M87 (2019).

Radio discoveries (13.27): quasars (distant, extremely luminous galactic nuclei, first found as radio sources); jets of material shot out from near black holes in active galaxies; the spiral structure and rotation of the Milky Way, mapped using 21 cm hydrogen emission which passes through dust (15.3); protoplanetary discs around young stars (imaged in detail by ALMA at millimetre wavelengths); also pulsars and the cosmic microwave background.

Infrared (13.28, 13.29): infrared is absorbed mainly by water vapour, which is concentrated low in the atmosphere. At high, dry mountain sites (such as Mauna Kea in Hawaii, about 4200 m, or the Atacama Desert in Chile) much of the water vapour is below the telescope, so some infrared wavelengths get through. Infrared also passes through dust that blocks visible light. Infrared astronomy discovered: protostars hidden inside dusty clouds; the dust and cold molecular clouds where stars form; volcanic hotspots on Io and other moons; and it reveals the Galactic centre. Infrared telescopes are cooled to stop their own heat swamping the signal; space telescopes such as Spitzer and JWST avoid the atmosphere entirely.

Atmospheric effects on images (13.30): turbulence makes images twinkle and blur (seeing), limiting resolution to about 1″ even for huge telescopes; light pollution and skyglow reduce contrast; absorption dims light, especially near the horizon; clouds and weather stop observations. Remedies: high mountain sites; adaptive optics (a deformable mirror that changes shape hundreds of times a second to cancel turbulence); going into space.

Above the atmosphere (13.31): gamma-ray, X-ray, most ultraviolet and most infrared telescopes must be in space (or on high balloons and rockets), because these wavelengths never reach the ground.

Space telescopes (13.32): advantages: access to all wavelengths; no atmospheric blurring, so sharper images; no weather, no light pollution, no day-night cycle (can observe continuously); very stable conditions. Disadvantages: very expensive to build and launch; size limited by the rocket; difficult or impossible to repair or upgrade (the Hubble Space Telescope was serviced by astronauts; JWST at L2 cannot be); limited lifetime (fuel, coolant); risk of launch failure; damage from radiation and space debris. Orbiting platforms include Hubble (visible, UV), JWST (infrared), Chandra (X-ray), Fermi (gamma-ray), SOHO and SDO (Sun), Gaia (star positions).

High-energy astronomy (13.33):

- Gamma-ray astronomy discovered gamma-ray bursts: extremely powerful, brief flashes from distant galaxies, from the collapse of very massive stars or the merging of neutron stars.
- X-ray astronomy revealed the accretion discs of black holes: gas spiralling into a black hole (for example in the binary Cygnus X-1, or in galaxy centres) is heated to millions of degrees and emits X-rays. Also hot gas in galaxy clusters and supernova remnants.
- Ultraviolet (and X-ray) astronomy revealed the structure of the hot corona and chromosphere of the Sun and of young, active stars, where strong magnetic activity heats gas to very high temperatures.

### Common mistakes

- Saying radio telescopes are big because radio waves are weak. The main reason is resolution at long wavelengths (though collecting area also helps).
- Saying X-ray telescopes can work on mountains. X-rays do not reach any ground level.
- Saying infrared is blocked by "the ozone layer". It is mainly absorbed by water vapour.

## 13.34 How a telescope changes the appearance of objects

### What you need to know

- How a telescope alters the appearance of stars, double stars, binary stars, open clusters, globular clusters, nebulae and galaxies.

### Explained

| Object | Naked eye | Through a telescope |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stars | Points of light | Still points (they are too far to show a disc), but brighter, colours clearer, and many more faint stars visible |
| Double stars | One star | Split into two separate stars (if the telescope's resolution is good enough), often with contrasting colours (Albireo: gold and blue) |
| Binary stars | One star | May be split into two; over years, the pair can be seen to orbit each other |
| Open clusters | A fuzzy patch or a few stars | Resolved into dozens or hundreds of individual stars; best at low magnification with a wide field |
| Globular clusters | A faint fuzzy spot (if visible) | A ball of light whose edges break up into many individual stars; the dense core may remain unresolved |
| Nebulae | Faint misty patches | Brighter, with visible structure such as dark lanes and glowing gas; colours appear in photographs (red hydrogen) but mostly grey to the eye |
| Galaxies | Faint fuzzy patch (Andromeda) | Brighter core and oval shape; larger telescopes show spiral arms or dust lanes in some; individual stars are not resolved except in the nearest galaxies |

### Common mistakes

- Saying a telescope shows stars as discs. Stars remain points.

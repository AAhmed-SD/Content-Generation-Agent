# Topic 16 Cosmology (Paper 2)

Cosmology is the study of the Universe as a whole: its origin, structure and future. This topic covers redshift and the redshift formula, Hubble's law and the age of the Universe, the Big Bang and Steady State theories and the evidence for the Big Bang, the cosmic microwave background, dark matter and dark energy, and the possible futures of the Universe. Simpler treatments are in [AQA GCSE Physics 4.8](../../../aqa/gcse/physics-8463/4.8-space-physics.md) and the [Edexcel GCSE Physics astronomy file](../../edexcel-sciences/physics-04-astronomy.md).

Equations (given): (λ − λ₀) ÷ λ₀ = v ÷ c; v = H₀d.
Data: H₀ = 68 km/s/Mpc; c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s; 1 pc = 3.1 × 10¹³ km.

## 16.1, 16.2 and 16.3 Redshift

### What you need to know

- Light from galaxies outside the Local Group is shifted to longer wavelengths (redshift).
- Redshift is caused by galaxies moving away (receding) from us.
- Use (λ − λ₀) ÷ λ₀ = v ÷ c, where λ is the observed wavelength, λ₀ the emitted wavelength, v the radial velocity of the source and c the speed of light.

### Explained

The absorption lines in a galaxy's spectrum have the same pattern as in the laboratory, but every line is moved to a longer wavelength, towards the red end. Vesto Slipher found in the 1910s that most galaxies are redshifted. Apart from a few in the Local Group (such as Andromeda, which is approaching and is blueshifted), every galaxy is redshifted.

This is like the Doppler effect: waves from a source moving away are stretched to longer wavelengths. (Strictly, cosmological redshift is caused by the expansion of space stretching the light as it travels, but at GCSE the redshift formula treats it as a recession velocity.)

The fractional change in wavelength, (λ − λ₀) ÷ λ₀, is called the redshift z, and equals v ÷ c for speeds much less than the speed of light.

### Worked example

A hydrogen line emitted at λ₀ = 656.3 nm is observed in a galaxy at λ = 669.4 nm.

- Δλ = 669.4 − 656.3 = 13.1 nm.
- v ÷ c = 13.1 ÷ 656.3 = 0.0200.
- v = 0.0200 × 3.0 × 10⁸ = 6.0 × 10⁶ m/s = 6000 km/s (moving away).

### Common mistakes

- Dividing by the observed wavelength instead of the emitted wavelength.
- Mixing m/s and km/s.
- Saying redshift means the galaxy looks red.

## 16.4, 16.5 and 16.6 Expansion, Hubble's law and the age of the Universe

### What you need to know

- The evidence that confirmed the discovery of the expanding Universe.
- Use Hubble's law v = H₀d, where v is the recession velocity, H₀ the Hubble constant and d the distance.
- How the Hubble constant is used to estimate the age and size of the Universe.

### Explained

In 1929 Edwin Hubble combined Slipher's redshift velocities with his own distances to galaxies (found using Cepheid variables, 13.16). He found that the further away a galaxy is, the faster it is receding: speed is directly proportional to distance. A graph of recession velocity against distance is a straight line through the origin; its gradient is the Hubble constant, H₀. The modern value is about 68 km/s per megaparsec: for every megaparsec of distance, galaxies recede about 68 km/s faster.

Evidence for expansion: almost all galaxies are redshifted, and redshift increases in proportion to distance. This is exactly what happens if the whole Universe is expanding uniformly: every galaxy sees every other galaxy moving away, with more distant ones moving faster, like dots on an inflating balloon or raisins in a rising loaf. There is no centre.

Age: if the galaxies have been moving apart at constant speed, then running time backwards they were all together at time t = d ÷ v = 1 ÷ H₀. Converting units: 1 ÷ (68 km/s/Mpc). 1 Mpc = 3.1 × 10¹⁹ km. So t = 3.1 × 10¹⁹ ÷ 68 = 4.6 × 10¹⁷ s ≈ 14 billion years. (The accepted age is 13.8 billion years.) A larger H₀ means a faster expansion and so a younger Universe.

Size: the observable Universe's radius is roughly the distance light could travel in the age of the Universe, about 14 billion light years (the actual distance to the edge of the observable Universe is larger, about 46 billion light years, because space has expanded while the light travelled).

### Worked example

1. A galaxy is 150 Mpc away. v = 68 × 150 = 10 200 km/s.
2. A galaxy recedes at 3400 km/s. d = v ÷ H₀ = 3400 ÷ 68 = 50 Mpc.
3. Combined: a galaxy's redshift is 0.010. v = 0.010 × 300 000 km/s = 3000 km/s; d = 3000 ÷ 68 ≈ 44 Mpc.

### Common mistakes

- Using c in m/s with H₀ in km/s.
- Forgetting the unit conversion when finding 1 ÷ H₀.
- Saying we are at the centre of the expansion.

## 16.7 Big Bang and Steady State

### What you need to know

- How the expansion of the Universe supports both the Big Bang theory and the Steady State theory.

### Explained

- Big Bang theory: the Universe began about 13.8 billion years ago from an extremely hot, dense state and has been expanding and cooling ever since. As it expands, galaxies get further apart and the average density falls. It has a beginning and changes with time.
- Steady State theory (Hoyle, Bondi and Gold, 1948): the Universe has no beginning or end and looks the same at all times and places. It is expanding, but new matter is continuously created in the gaps between galaxies (a few hydrogen atoms per cubic metre per billion years), forming new galaxies, so the average density stays constant.

Both theories explain the expansion and the redshift–distance relationship, so redshift alone cannot decide between them. Other evidence is needed (16.8): it showed that the Universe has changed over time, which the Steady State theory cannot explain.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the Steady State theory denies expansion. It accepts expansion but adds continuous creation of matter.
- Saying the Big Bang was an explosion at a point in space. Space itself has been expanding everywhere.

## 16.8 Evidence for the Big Bang

### What you need to know

- The major observational evidence for the Big Bang: quasars (QSOs), cosmic microwave background (CMB) radiation, and the Hubble Deep Field image.

### Explained

- Quasars: almost all quasars have large redshifts, so they are very far away; we see them as they were billions of years ago, because light takes billions of years to reach us. There are no nearby quasars. So the Universe was different in the past (more active galaxies then). The Steady State theory says the Universe should look the same at all times, so quasars should be spread evenly at all distances. Quasars support an evolving Universe, as in the Big Bang theory.
- Cosmic microwave background (CMB): discovered by accident in 1965 by Penzias and Wilson as a faint microwave signal coming equally from every direction. It is the afterglow of the hot early Universe. About 380 000 years after the Big Bang, the Universe cooled enough (to about 3000 K) for atoms to form and become transparent, releasing radiation. Expansion since then has stretched its wavelength by about 1100 times, into microwaves, matching a temperature of about 2.7 K. The Big Bang theory predicted this radiation; the Steady State theory has no natural explanation for it. This is the strongest evidence.
- Hubble Deep Field (1995): the Hubble Space Telescope stared at a tiny, apparently empty patch of sky for about 10 days. The image showed around 3000 galaxies, many extremely distant and seen as they were billions of years ago. These young galaxies look smaller, more irregular and different from nearby galaxies, showing galaxies have evolved over time, which supports the Big Bang. (Later images, such as the Hubble Ultra Deep Field and JWST deep fields, go further back.)

Also: the observed amounts of hydrogen and helium (about 3 : 1 by mass) match predictions of fusion in the first few minutes after the Big Bang.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the CMB is light from the first stars.
- Saying the Hubble Deep Field shows the edge of the Universe.

## 16.9 Fluctuations in the CMB

### What you need to know

- The significance of fluctuations in the CMB for theories of the evolution of the Universe, including the discoveries of the WMAP and Planck missions.

### Explained

The CMB is extremely smooth, the same temperature in every direction to about 1 part in 100 000. But space missions found tiny variations (anisotropies): slightly warmer and cooler patches.

- COBE (1992) first detected these fluctuations.
- WMAP (Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe, 2001 to 2010) mapped them across the whole sky in much more detail.
- Planck (ESA, 2009 to 2013) produced the most detailed map.

Significance: the patches show slightly denser and less dense regions in the early Universe. Gravity made the denser regions grow over billions of years into the galaxies, clusters and superclusters we see today (15.13). Without these seeds, structure could not have formed. The pattern and sizes of the patches let astronomers measure key properties of the Universe very precisely: its age (13.8 billion years), that its geometry is flat, and its make-up: about 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter and 68% dark energy. These measurements strongly support the Big Bang model with dark matter and dark energy.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the fluctuations disprove the Big Bang. They are exactly what is needed for structure to form, and they confirm the model.

## 16.10 and 16.11 Dark matter and dark energy

### What you need to know

- The significance and possible nature of dark matter and dark energy.
- The difficulties in detecting them.

### Explained

Dark matter:

- Evidence: the outer parts of spiral galaxies rotate much faster than expected from the visible stars and gas (flat rotation curves, 15.3), so there must be extra unseen mass holding them together; galaxies in clusters move too fast for the cluster to stay bound by visible mass alone; gravitational lensing shows more mass than is visible; it is needed for structure to grow from the CMB fluctuations.
- Possible nature: matter that does not emit, absorb or reflect light and interacts mainly through gravity. Ideas include WIMPs (weakly interacting massive particles) and axions; some suggested ordinary dark objects (MACHOs, such as faint stars and black holes) but these cannot account for most of it. Some scientists propose changing the law of gravity instead.
- Significance: about 85% of all matter in the Universe is dark matter.

Dark energy:

- Evidence: in 1998, observations of type Ia supernovae in distant galaxies (used as standard candles) showed they were fainter, so further away, than expected: the expansion of the Universe is accelerating, not slowing down.
- Possible nature: an unknown form of energy that fills space and causes repulsion, perhaps a property of empty space itself (the "cosmological constant").
- Significance: it makes up about 68% of the total energy content of the Universe and controls its future.

Difficulties in detection: neither interacts with light, so neither can be seen with any telescope; dark matter particles interact so weakly with ordinary matter that very sensitive underground detectors have not yet found them; particle accelerators have not produced them; dark energy is spread extremely thinly and is only noticed on the scale of the whole Universe. We only know about them from their gravitational effects.

### Common mistakes

- Confusing dark matter (extra gravity, holds galaxies together) with dark energy (accelerates expansion).
- Saying dark matter is black holes or dust clouds. These are ordinary matter and cannot account for most of it.

## 16.12 The future of the Universe

### What you need to know

- Current models predict different possible futures for the Universe.

### Explained

The fate depends on the balance between gravity (from matter and dark matter) slowing expansion and dark energy speeding it up:

- Big Freeze (Heat Death): the Universe expands for ever. Galaxies move further apart, stars use up their fuel and die, and everything becomes cold and dark. This is the most likely future given the measured accelerating expansion.
- Big Rip: if dark energy grows stronger with time, the acceleration becomes so great that galaxies, stars, planets and eventually atoms are torn apart.
- Big Crunch: if gravity were strong enough (density above a critical value) or dark energy reversed, expansion would stop and reverse, and the Universe would collapse back into a hot, dense state, perhaps followed by another Big Bang (a "Big Bounce").

Which future happens depends on the true nature of dark energy, which is not yet known. Current observations favour continued, accelerating expansion.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the Universe will definitely end in a Big Crunch. Present evidence favours expansion for ever.

# Observational skills and observation tasks

Every 1AS0 student must carry out at least one unaided (naked-eye) and one aided (binoculars, telescope or camera) observation task, chosen from different rows of the board's list, and keep a record. The tasks are not marked, but both papers ask about planning, making, analysing and evaluating observations. This file covers the four skill areas and the method behind each task.

## Obs 1 Designing observations

### What you need to know

- Produce a list of feasible targets, locations, dates, times and instruments for the task.
- Use astronomical data to select the most appropriate target, location, date, time or instrument.
- Write an initial observing programme, including health and safety.
- Refine the programme after initial trials.

### Explained

Astronomers cannot control their "experiment": the weather, the Moon's phase, the time of year and the object's position are fixed. So planning is essential.

- Target: is it above the horizon in darkness at a good altitude (6.12)? Is it bright enough for the instrument? Is the Moon nearby or bright?
- Date and time: use a planisphere, app or almanac to find when the object culminates, the Moon's phase, and dates of events (meteor shower peaks, eclipses, transits, maxima of variable stars).
- Location: dark sky (low light pollution), clear horizon in the right direction, safe access, stable ground.
- Instrument: naked eye, binoculars (wide field, easy), telescope (magnification, resolution, light grasp), camera (long exposures, measurements).
- Health and safety: never look at the Sun without proper protection; warm clothing; red torch; tell someone where you are; trip hazards in the dark; electrical safety outdoors.
- Refinement: after a trial night, change the timing, location, exposure or method to improve the data.

### Common mistakes

- Planning to observe an object that is below the horizon or in daylight at the chosen time.

## Obs 2 Making observations

### What you need to know

- Carry out unaided observations using techniques such as dark adaptation and averted vision.
- Carry out aided observations with binoculars, telescopes and cameras.
- Record full details with every observation: date, time, location, seeing conditions, instruments used.
- Take enough observations to give accurate data.

### Explained

Good practice: allow 20 to 30 minutes for dark adaptation; use averted vision for faint objects (6.19); record times in UT; describe seeing (for example on a 1 to 5 scale) and transparency; note the Moon's phase and any cloud; for drawings, note orientation (north and east) and the field of view; for photographs, record exposure time, ISO or gain, focal length and any filter. Repeat observations on several nights to reduce the effect of random errors.

### Common mistakes

- Recording a drawing without date, time or orientation, which makes it useless for comparison.

## Obs 3 Analysing observations

### What you need to know

- Identify patterns or trends in a series of drawings or photographs.
- Present numerical data as graphs or charts.
- Perform calculations.
- Perform basic digital image processing: adjusting brightness and dynamic range, and using false colour.

### Explained

- Patterns: for example, the movement of a sunspot from day to day, or changes in the shading of a lunar crater as the terminator passes.
- Graphs: light curves (magnitude against time, magnitudes increasing downwards so brighter is up), shadow length against time, sunspot position against date.
- Calculations: rotation period from angle per day; sidereal day from star trails; longitude from time difference; mean values and ranges.
- Image processing: stretching the brightness levels to reveal faint detail; adjusting contrast; stacking several images to reduce noise; false colour, where different brightness levels or wavelengths are given colours to show structure that the eye cannot easily see.

## Obs 4 Evaluating observations

### What you need to know

- Compare results with professional images or accepted values to assess accuracy; calculate a quantitative measure of accuracy where possible.
- Identify the effects of light pollution on naked-eye observations, and of light pollution, exposure time and filtering on photographs.
- Recognise major image artefacts: scattered light, diffraction spikes, cosmic rays, trails from satellites, aircraft or meteors.
- Identify the major causes of error and suggest and implement improvements.

### Explained

Percentage error = (your value − accepted value) ÷ accepted value × 100%.

Artefacts:

- Scattered light: glare or gradients from nearby lights or the Moon.
- Diffraction spikes: crossed lines from bright stars, caused by the supports of a reflector's secondary mirror.
- Cosmic rays: single bright pixels or short streaks, appearing randomly on one frame only.
- Trails: straight lines from satellites or aircraft (aircraft trails may be dotted by flashing lights); a meteor trail may be brighter in the middle and fade at the ends.

Exposure time: too short and faint objects are missed; too long and bright stars saturate, light pollution fogs the image, and stars trail if the camera is not tracking. Filters can cut light pollution or isolate one wavelength (such as H-alpha).

Typical sources of error: timing errors (reaction time); estimating rather than measuring positions; poor seeing; light pollution; small number of observations; the observer's eye judging brightness inconsistently.

### Worked example

A7: a student estimates the magnitude of a variable star on 30 nights and plots a light curve. Maxima occur on nights 3, 13.5 and 24. Mean interval = (24 − 3) ÷ 2 = 10.5 days, so the period is about 10.5 days. If the accepted period is 10.2 days, percentage error = (10.5 − 10.2) ÷ 10.2 × 100 ≈ 3%.

A3: a target star looks halfway in brightness between reference stars of magnitude 3.4 and 4.0, so its estimated magnitude is 3.7. The catalogue gives 3.85, so the estimate is 0.15 mag too bright.

## Check questions

1. List four details that must be recorded with every astronomical observation. [4 marks]
2. A student plans to observe Orion at 21:00 in July from the UK. Explain why this plan is not feasible and suggest a better month. [2 marks]
3. Describe how a student could estimate the magnitude of a star using reference stars. [3 marks]
4. A photograph shows a bright star with four thin lines extending from it. Name this artefact and its cause. [2 marks]
5. A single bright pixel appears on one image but not on the next image of the same field. Suggest its cause. [1 mark]
6. A student's value for the solar rotation period is 29.0 days. The accepted value (as seen from Earth) is 27.3 days. Calculate the percentage error and suggest one cause of the error. [3 marks]
7. Explain why repeating observations over several nights improves the reliability of a light curve. [2 marks]
8. Describe a method for comparing the density of stars in the plane of the Milky Way with that away from it. [3 marks]
9. Explain why false colour is used in processing some astronomical images. [1 mark]
10. Give two health and safety precautions for an observing session in a dark field at night. [2 marks]

## Mark scheme

1. Any four: date (1); time (1); location (1); seeing / weather conditions (1); instrument used (1).
2. In July Orion is near the Sun / below the horizon or only visible in daylight at 21:00 (1); choose a winter month such as December to February (1).
3. Choose two reference stars of known magnitude, one brighter and one fainter than the target (1); judge where the target lies in brightness between them (1); interpolate to estimate its magnitude (1).
4. Diffraction spikes (1); caused by the supports (spider vanes) of the secondary mirror in a reflector (1).
5. Cosmic ray (1).
6. (29.0 − 27.3) ÷ 27.3 × 100 (1) = 6.2% (1). Cause: e.g. inaccurate drawing positions / spot near the limb / few days of data / evolving spot shape (1).
7. Random errors / poor nights average out (1); more data points to define the shape and period (1).
8. Use a tube or fixed field of view to count stars (1) in several directions in the Milky Way band and away from it (1); compare the mean counts (1).
9. To show differences in brightness or wavelength / structure that the eye cannot easily distinguish (1).
10. Any two: red torch / tell someone where you are / warm clothing / check for trip hazards / never look at the Sun / go in a group (1)(1).

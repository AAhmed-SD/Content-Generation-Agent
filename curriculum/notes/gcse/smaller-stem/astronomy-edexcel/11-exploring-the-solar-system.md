# Topic 11 Exploring the Solar System (Paper 2)

This topic has three parts. First, the bodies of the Solar System: planets, dwarf planets, comets, asteroids and meteoroids, the regions beyond Neptune, how the AU was measured and where Earth's water came from. Second, telescopes: why we need them, how they collect and focus light, the main designs, and the quantities that describe them (light grasp, aperture, field of view, resolution, magnification). Third, space exploration: Galileo's discoveries, types of space probe with named examples, rockets, crewed missions and Apollo.

Equation (given): magnification = fₒ ÷ fₑ.

## 11.1, 11.6, 11.8, 11.9 and 11.11 Bodies of the Solar System

### What you need to know

- Use data about the names and relative locations of planets, dwarf planets and Small Solar System Objects (asteroids, meteoroids and comets).
- The principal characteristics of the planets: relative size, relative mass, surface temperature, atmospheric composition, presence of moons and ring systems.
- Use information about the size of the Solar System; use the AU, light year and parsec (see also Topic 7).
- Most bodies orbit the Sun in or close to the plane of the ecliptic.

### Explained

Definitions (International Astronomical Union):

- Planet: orbits the Sun, has enough mass for its gravity to pull it into a nearly round shape, and has cleared the neighbourhood of its orbit of other material.
- Dwarf planet: orbits the Sun and is nearly round, but has not cleared its orbit, and is not a moon. Ceres (in the asteroid belt), Pluto, Haumea, Makemake, Eris.
- Small Solar System Objects (SSSOs): everything else orbiting the Sun: asteroids (rocky or metallic bodies, mostly in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter), comets (icy bodies) and meteoroids (small fragments).

Planet characteristics (data sheet values; all given in the exam):

| Planet | Distance (AU) | Diameter (1000 km) | Mass (Earth = 1) | Mean temperature (°C) | Atmosphere | Moons | Rings |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mercury | 0.38 | 4.9 | 0.055 | 170 (huge day-night range) | Almost none | 0 | No |
| Venus | 0.72 | 12.1 | 0.82 | 470 (hottest; runaway greenhouse effect) | Very thick carbon dioxide; sulfuric acid clouds | 0 | No |
| Earth | 1.0 | 12.8 | 1.00 | 15 | Nitrogen, oxygen | 1 | No |
| Mars | 1.5 | 6.9 | 0.11 | −50 | Thin carbon dioxide | 2 small (Phobos, Deimos) | No |
| Jupiter | 5.2 | 143 | 318 | −150 | Hydrogen, helium; bands, Great Red Spot | 4 large Galilean (Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto) plus many | Yes (faint) |
| Saturn | 9.5 | 121 | 95 | −180 | Hydrogen, helium | Titan and many others | Yes (bright, icy) |
| Uranus | 19.1 | 51 | 15 | −210 | Hydrogen, helium, methane (blue-green) | Many | Yes (thin) |
| Neptune | 30.0 | 50 | 17 | −220 | Hydrogen, helium, methane (blue) | Triton and others | Yes (thin) |

Patterns: the four inner terrestrial planets are small, dense and rocky with few or no moons and no rings; the four outer giant planets are large, low-density, mostly gas and ice, with many moons and ring systems. Temperature generally falls with distance, except Venus, which is hotter than Mercury because of its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere.

Size: Neptune is 30 AU from the Sun; the Kuiper Belt extends to about 50 AU; the heliosphere's edge is over 100 AU; the Oort Cloud perhaps 2000 to 100 000 AU. Light takes about 4 hours to reach Neptune and over a year to reach the outer Oort Cloud.

Most planets, the asteroid belt and the Kuiper Belt objects orbit close to the plane of the ecliptic (within a few degrees; Mercury about 7°, Pluto about 17°), and all planets orbit in the same direction. This flatness is evidence that they formed from a flat, spinning disc (12.3). The Oort Cloud is an exception: it is a spherical shell, so long-period comets arrive from all directions.

### Worked example

How many times further from the Sun is Saturn than Mars? 9.5 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 6.3.

Jupiter's diameter is 143 ÷ 12.8 ≈ 11 times the Earth's, but its mass is only 318 times, not 11³ ≈ 1400 times, so its density is much lower.

### Common mistakes

- Calling Pluto a planet. It is a dwarf planet (since 2006).
- Saying Mercury is the hottest planet. Venus is, because of its greenhouse atmosphere.
- Saying only Saturn has rings. All four giant planets do.

## 11.2, 11.3, 11.4 and 11.5 Comets, the Kuiper Belt, the Oort Cloud and the heliosphere

### What you need to know

- The structure of comets: nucleus, coma and tails.
- Orbits of short-period comets and their origin in the Kuiper Belt.
- Orbits of long-period comets and their origin in the Oort Cloud.
- The location and nature of the Kuiper Belt, Oort Cloud and heliosphere.

### Explained

Structure of a comet:

- Nucleus: a solid body a few km across of ice (water, carbon dioxide, ammonia, methane) mixed with dust and rock: a "dirty snowball". Very dark surface.
- Coma: as the comet approaches the Sun (within a few AU), heat makes the ices sublime (turn straight to gas), releasing gas and dust into a huge glowing cloud around the nucleus, which can be larger than the Sun.
- Tails: two kinds. The dust tail is curved, yellowish-white, made of dust pushed away gently by radiation pressure of sunlight, and lags behind along the orbit. The ion (gas or plasma) tail is straight and bluish, made of ionised gas swept out by the solar wind; it points directly away from the Sun. Tails can be millions of km long. Because both are pushed by the Sun, a comet moving away from the Sun travels tail first.

Orbits:

- Short-period comets have periods of less than 200 years, orbit fairly close to the ecliptic, mostly in the same direction as the planets, and have elliptical orbits reaching out to around the outer planets or the Kuiper Belt. Most come from the Kuiper Belt (and the scattered disc beyond it), deflected inwards by Neptune's gravity. Example: Halley's comet (76 years), 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko (6.4 years).
- Long-period comets have periods of thousands to millions of years, very elongated orbits, and come from all directions and at any angle to the ecliptic. They come from the Oort Cloud, nudged inwards by passing stars or by the Galaxy's tidal pull.

Regions:

- Kuiper Belt: a flat, doughnut-shaped belt of icy bodies beyond Neptune, from about 30 to 50 AU, close to the plane of the ecliptic. Contains dwarf planets such as Pluto and Makemake and many smaller objects, leftovers from the formation of the Solar System.
- Oort Cloud: a hypothetical, roughly spherical cloud of trillions of icy bodies surrounding the Solar System at roughly 2000 to 100 000 AU. Never directly observed: its existence is inferred from the orbits of long-period comets.
- Heliosphere: the huge bubble in interstellar space blown by the solar wind. The termination shock is where the solar wind slows sharply (about 90 AU); the heliopause, the boundary where the solar wind meets the interstellar medium, is about 120 AU from the Sun. Voyager 1 crossed it in 2012 and Voyager 2 in 2018.

### Common mistakes

- Saying a comet's tail points behind it. It points away from the Sun.
- Saying the Oort Cloud has been photographed.

## 11.7 Formation and current position of the gas giants

### What you need to know

- The main theories for how the gas giant planets in our Solar System formed and how they reached their current positions.

### Explained

Formation: beyond the "snow line" (about 3 to 5 AU from the young Sun), it was cold enough for water and other compounds to freeze into ice, so there was much more solid material. Rocky and icy planetesimals collected quickly into large cores of about 10 Earth masses. Their gravity was then strong enough to pull in huge envelopes of hydrogen and helium gas from the surrounding disc before the young Sun blew the gas away. This is the core accretion model (12.3 describes it and the alternative, disc instability).

Current positions: the giant planets probably did not form exactly where they are now. Models (such as the "Nice model") suggest that gravitational interactions with the remaining planetesimals, and with each other, made the giants migrate: Jupiter moved slightly inward, while Saturn, Uranus and Neptune moved outwards. Uranus and Neptune may have swapped places. This migration scattered many small bodies, perhaps causing the Late Heavy Bombardment of the inner planets and shaping the Kuiper Belt. The "Grand Tack" idea suggests Jupiter moved inwards towards Mars's orbit and then back out again, which would explain why Mars is small and the asteroid belt has little mass.

### Common mistakes

- Saying giant planets formed close to the Sun and then moved out. Their cores formed beyond the snow line where ices were available.

## 11.10 Meteoroids and meteorites

### What you need to know

- The origin and structure of meteoroids and meteorites.

### Explained

Meteoroids are small rocky or metallic particles in space, from dust grains up to about a metre across (larger bodies are asteroids). Most come from comets (dust released as the ice evaporates, forming meteor streams, 5.7) or from collisions between asteroids, and a few are chipped off the Moon or Mars by impacts.

A meteorite is a meteoroid that survives its passage through the atmosphere and reaches the ground. Main types:

- Stony meteorites (most common, over 90% of falls): mainly silicate rock. Chondrites contain small round grains called chondrules and are some of the oldest, least changed material in the Solar System (about 4.6 billion years old); some carbonaceous chondrites contain water and organic compounds.
- Iron meteorites: mostly iron-nickel metal, from the cores of asteroids that were broken apart. Show crystal patterns when cut and etched.
- Stony-iron meteorites: a mixture of metal and rock, from the boundary between core and mantle of a broken asteroid.

They are studied because they show what the early Solar System was made of and how old it is.

### Common mistakes

- Confusing the three terms: meteoroid (in space), meteor (light in the sky), meteorite (on the ground).

## 11.12 Transits of Venus and the size of the AU

### What you need to know

- How transits of Venus, as proposed by Halley, were used to find the size of the astronomical unit and so the absolute size of the Solar System.

### Explained

Kepler's third law gave the relative distances of all the planets in AU, but nobody knew how big an AU was in km. Edmond Halley proposed in 1716 that timing a transit of Venus across the Sun from widely separated places on Earth would give the answer.

The principle is parallax: observers at different latitudes see Venus cross the Sun along slightly different paths (chords), because they view it from slightly different positions. The separation between the paths depends on the distance to Venus and the Sun. Each observer times the transit from start to end; the duration tells them the length of their chord, and hence where it lies on the Sun's disc. Knowing the distance between the observers on Earth (in km), the angular difference between the paths, and Venus's distance relative to the Earth's from Kepler's third law (0.72 AU), the Earth-Sun distance in km can be calculated.

Transits of Venus are rare: they come in pairs 8 years apart, more than a century apart (1761 and 1769; 1874 and 1882; 2004 and 2012; next 2117). Expeditions were sent all over the world in 1761 and 1769 (including Captain Cook's voyage to Tahiti). Results were affected by the "black drop effect", which made exact contact times hard to judge, but gave an AU of about 150 million km, within a few per cent of today's value (now measured accurately by radar).

### Common mistakes

- Saying a transit of Venus can happen at every inferior conjunction. Venus's orbit is tilted, so it usually passes above or below the Sun.

## 11.13 The origin of water on Earth

### What you need to know

- The main theories for the origin of Earth's water.

### Explained

The early Earth formed inside the snow line, where it was too hot for ice to be included in large amounts, and it was very hot after formation and the Giant Impact. So where did its oceans come from? Main theories:

- Delivered by comets: icy comets striking the young Earth. But measurements of the ratio of deuterium (heavy hydrogen) to normal hydrogen in most comets (for example comet 67P measured by Rosetta) show it is higher than in Earth's oceans, so comets cannot have supplied most of the water.
- Delivered by asteroids: carbonaceous chondrite asteroids from the outer asteroid belt contain water in their minerals with a deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio close to that of Earth's oceans. This is the favoured source of much of Earth's water.
- From within the Earth (outgassing): water was locked in minerals in the rocks that formed the Earth and was released by volcanoes as the planet cooled, condensing as rain to fill the oceans.

Most scientists think the water came from a mixture of these sources.

### Common mistakes

- Stating that comets definitely brought Earth's water. Isotope evidence suggests they were not the main source.

## 11.14 to 11.17 The eye, objectives and simple telescopes

### What you need to know

- The human eye is limited in astronomy by its small aperture and limited sensitivity in low light.
- The objective of a telescope captures and focuses light so the image can be magnified by an eyepiece.
- Convex (converging) lenses and concave (converging) mirrors can collect and focus light.
- Simple telescopes combine an objective (lens or mirror) with an eyepiece.

### Explained

The eye's pupil is at most about 7 mm across in the dark, so it collects very little light; and the eye cannot store light over a long exposure, as a camera can. So many faint objects are invisible to the eye, and fine detail cannot be resolved.

A telescope has two optical parts:

- The objective (or primary): a large converging lens or concave mirror that collects light from a distant object and brings it to a focus, forming a real image at its focal point. The bigger it is, the more light it collects.
- The eyepiece: a small, short-focal-length converging lens (or set of lenses) that magnifies this image for the eye, like a magnifying glass.

A convex (converging) lens refracts parallel light rays from a distant object so they meet at the focal point. A concave mirror reflects parallel rays to a focus. The distance from the lens or mirror to the focus is its focal length.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the main purpose of a telescope is magnification. Collecting more light (and resolving detail) is more important.
- Calling a concave mirror diverging. A concave mirror converges light.

## 11.18 and 11.25 Telescope designs; reflectors versus refractors

### What you need to know

- The basic designs of the Galilean refractor, Keplerian refractor, Newtonian reflector and Cassegrain reflector. Detailed ray diagrams are not needed.
- The advantages of reflecting telescopes compared with refractors, in terms of chromatic aberration, very long focal lengths, large apertures and multiple mirrors.

### Explained

| Design | Objective | Eyepiece and layout | Image |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Galilean refractor | Convex lens at the front of a tube | Concave (diverging) lens, placed before the focus | Upright; narrow field of view; used by Galileo |
| Keplerian refractor | Convex lens | Convex (converging) lens, placed after the focus | Inverted; wider field; the basis of modern refractors |
| Newtonian reflector | Concave (parabolic) primary mirror at the bottom of an open tube | A small flat secondary mirror at 45° near the top reflects light out to an eyepiece in the side of the tube | Inverted; simple and cheap; popular with amateurs |
| Cassegrain reflector | Concave primary mirror with a central hole | A small convex secondary mirror reflects light back down through the hole to an eyepiece or camera behind the primary | Compact tube with long effective focal length; used in most large professional telescopes |

Advantages of reflectors over refractors:

- Chromatic aberration: a lens refracts different colours by different amounts, so they focus at slightly different points, giving coloured fringes round bright objects. Mirrors reflect all colours the same way, so reflectors have no chromatic aberration.
- Very long focal lengths: high magnification needs a long focal length; a refractor with a long focal length needs a very long, awkward tube. Reflector designs such as the Cassegrain fold the light path, so a long focal length fits in a short tube.
- Large apertures: a large lens is very heavy, can only be supported at its edge, and sags under its own weight, distorting the image; the glass must be flawless all the way through. A mirror can be supported across its whole back, only its front surface must be accurate, and it can be made thin and light. The largest refractor is about 1 m; reflectors are over 10 m.
- Multiple mirrors: very large primaries can be built from many smaller hexagonal segments (for example the Keck telescopes, JWST), which is impossible with lenses.

Disadvantages of reflectors: the secondary mirror blocks some light and causes diffraction spikes; open tubes let in dust and air currents; mirrors need recoating and aligning (collimation).

### Common mistakes

- Saying reflectors are better because they magnify more. Magnification depends on focal lengths.
- Saying the Galilean telescope uses two convex lenses. Its eyepiece is concave.

## 11.19 to 11.23 Telescope properties

### What you need to know

- Light grasp is directly proportional to the area of the objective, so to the square of its diameter.
- Aperture is related to the diameter of the objective.
- Field of view is the circle of sky visible through the eyepiece, in degrees or arcminutes.
- Resolution is better (finer) for a larger objective, and worse at longer wavelengths.
- Use magnification = fₒ ÷ fₑ.

### Explained

- Aperture: the diameter of the objective lens or mirror (the "size" of a telescope).
- Light grasp (light-gathering power): how much light the telescope collects, proportional to the area of the objective, so to the diameter squared. Doubling the diameter collects 4 times as much light, showing fainter objects.
- Resolution (resolving power): the ability to show fine detail and separate close objects (such as a close double star). The smallest angle that can be resolved is proportional to wavelength ÷ diameter, so a larger aperture gives better resolution, and observing at longer wavelengths (such as radio) gives worse resolution for the same size of dish (13.25). Atmospheric seeing often limits ground-based resolution.
- Field of view: the circle of sky seen through the eyepiece. Higher magnification gives a smaller field of view. Wide fields suit large objects (the Pleiades, the Andromeda Galaxy); narrow fields suit planets and double stars.
- Magnification = focal length of objective ÷ focal length of eyepiece. A long-focal-length objective and short eyepiece give high magnification. Changing the eyepiece changes the magnification. Too much magnification just gives a larger, dimmer, blurrier image ("empty magnification").

### Worked example

1. A telescope has fₒ = 1200 mm. With a 25 mm eyepiece, magnification = 1200 ÷ 25 = 48×. With a 6 mm eyepiece, 200×.
2. Light grasp: a 200 mm telescope compared with the eye's 7 mm pupil: (200 ÷ 7)² ≈ 816 times as much light.
3. A 300 mm telescope compared with a 100 mm telescope: (300 ÷ 100)² = 9 times the light grasp, and 3 times better resolution.
4. A student wants 150× with an eyepiece of 8 mm. fₒ = 150 × 8 = 1200 mm.

### Common mistakes

- Saying light grasp is proportional to diameter. It is proportional to diameter squared.
- Dividing fₑ by fₒ.
- Saying a larger aperture gives a larger field of view.

## 11.24 Galileo's early observations

### What you need to know

- The importance of Galileo's early telescope observations in establishing the heliocentric model.

### Explained

From 1609 Galileo Galilei used his own small refracting telescopes (about 20× to 30× magnification) and saw:

- Four moons orbiting Jupiter (now called the Galilean moons). This showed that not everything orbits the Earth, and that a moving planet can keep its moons (an objection to a moving Earth had been that it would leave the Moon behind).
- The phases of Venus: Venus shows a full set of phases, from crescent to nearly full, and appears much larger as a crescent than when gibbous. This is only possible if Venus orbits the Sun. In the Ptolemaic model, Venus always lies between the Earth and the Sun and could only ever show crescent phases. This was strong evidence against the Ptolemaic model.
- Mountains and craters on the Moon, and spots on the Sun that moved (showing the Sun rotates): heavenly bodies were not perfect, unchanging spheres.
- Many more stars than can be seen with the naked eye, and that the Milky Way is made of countless stars.

These observations supported the heliocentric model of Copernicus and Kepler, though they did not by themselves rule out Brahe's hybrid model. Galileo's support for heliocentrism brought him into conflict with the Church authorities of the time.

### Common mistakes

- Saying Galileo invented the telescope. He improved it and was among the first to use it for astronomy.
- Saying the moons of Jupiter proved the Earth goes round the Sun. They showed not everything orbits the Earth; the phases of Venus were the stronger evidence.

## 11.26, 11.27 and 11.28 Space probes

### What you need to know

- Advantages and disadvantages of fly-by, orbiter, impactor and lander probes.
- An example of each with its target and major discoveries: fly-by New Horizons (outer Solar System); orbiter Juno (Jupiter) or Dawn (Vesta and Ceres); impactor Deep Impact (comet Tempel 1); lander Philae (comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko).
- A space probe must reach (close to) the Earth's escape velocity, which needs rockets (see 9.4).

### Explained

| Type | What it does | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fly-by | Passes close to a body once and continues on its way; may visit several bodies using gravity assists | Cheaper; can visit several targets; needs no fuel to slow down | Only a brief look; only one side may be seen; no long-term monitoring |
| Orbiter | Fires rockets to slow down and go into orbit | Studies the whole body over months or years; maps the surface; observes changes and seasons | Needs extra fuel to brake into orbit, so heavier and costlier; cannot sample the surface directly |
| Impactor | Deliberately crashes into a body | Reveals material beneath the surface; can test methods of deflecting asteroids | One-off; the impactor is destroyed; needs another craft to observe |
| Lander | Lands on the surface (some carry rovers) | Direct measurements, chemical analysis, close-up images, can drill or sample | Very complex and risky landing; studies only one small area (unless a rover); most expensive |

Examples:

- New Horizons (fly-by): launched 2006, flew past Pluto in 2015, giving the first detailed images: a young, smooth plain of nitrogen ice (Sputnik Planitia), mountains of water ice, and a thin hazy atmosphere. In 2019 it flew past the Kuiper Belt object Arrokoth, a "contact binary" of two lobes stuck together.
- Juno (orbiter): in orbit around Jupiter since 2016 in a polar orbit. Discovered huge cyclone clusters at the poles, measured Jupiter's strong and irregular magnetic field, and found that its core is "fuzzy", spread out rather than compact.
- Dawn (orbiter): orbited the asteroid Vesta (2011 to 2012) and the dwarf planet Ceres (2015 to 2018), the first craft to orbit two bodies, using ion engines. Found that Vesta is a layered protoplanet with an iron core, and found bright salt deposits (sodium carbonate) in Occator crater on Ceres, evidence of briny water below the surface.
- Deep Impact (impactor): in 2005 it released a 370 kg copper impactor into comet Tempel 1 while the main craft watched. The cloud of ejected material showed the nucleus is fluffy, porous and covered in fine dust, with water ice and organic material underneath.
- Philae (lander): carried by the Rosetta orbiter, Philae landed on comet 67P in 2014, the first soft landing on a comet. Its harpoons failed and it bounced into a shadowed crack, so its batteries ran out after about 60 hours, but it detected organic molecules on the surface and found it was harder than expected. Rosetta showed the comet's water has a different deuterium ratio from Earth's oceans (11.13).

### Common mistakes

- Saying all probes land. Most are fly-bys or orbiters.
- Mixing up Rosetta (orbiter) and Philae (lander).

## 11.29 and 11.30 Crewed exploration and Apollo

### What you need to know

- Advantages and disadvantages of direct observation by crewed missions.
- The main features of the Apollo programme to land astronauts on the Moon.

### Explained

Crewed missions: advantages: humans can make decisions on the spot, adapt to the unexpected, choose the most interesting samples, repair equipment, and do complex experiments; they inspire the public. Disadvantages: very much more expensive (life support, food, water, air, shielding, safe return); risk to life (radiation, accidents); limited by human endurance; the spacecraft must return to Earth, so it carries much more mass. Robotic probes can go further, for longer, and to places too hostile for people.

The Apollo programme (USA, NASA):

- Aim: to land astronauts on the Moon and return them safely, set by President Kennedy in 1961, in a "space race" with the Soviet Union.
- Hardware: the Saturn V three-stage rocket (the most powerful ever flown at the time) launched the Command and Service Module (CSM, which stayed in lunar orbit with one astronaut) and the Lunar Module (LM, which took two astronauts to the surface and back up to the CSM). Only the small Command Module returned to Earth, splashing down in the ocean.
- Key missions: Apollo 1 (1967, a fire killed three astronauts in a ground test); Apollo 8 (1968, first humans to orbit the Moon and see the far side); Apollo 11 (July 1969, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin made the first landing, in the Sea of Tranquility); Apollo 13 (1970, an oxygen tank exploded, landing cancelled, crew returned safely); Apollo 15 to 17 used the Lunar Roving Vehicle; Apollo 17 (1972) was the last.
- Results: six successful landings, twelve astronauts walked on the Moon; about 382 kg of rock and soil returned, used to date the lunar surface and to support the Giant Impact Hypothesis; seismometers and laser reflectors left on the surface (the reflectors are still used to measure the Earth-Moon distance precisely).

### Common mistakes

- Saying all three astronauts landed. One stayed in orbit in the Command Module.
- Saying Apollo 13 landed on the Moon.

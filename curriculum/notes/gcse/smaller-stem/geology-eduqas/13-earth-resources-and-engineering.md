# 4.2 Earth resources and engineering

Geology underpins the materials, energy and water we use and the safety of what we build. This topic covers resources and reserves, three named mineral resources, prospecting methods, oil and gas traps and extraction, groundwater and aquifers, waste disposal, and geological factors in siting engineering projects.

## 4.2a Resources and reserves

### What you need to know

- Resources are naturally occurring useful substances; reserves are the calculated amount of a resource that is economic to extract.

### Explained

A resource includes all of a substance that exists, known or estimated, whether or not it can be extracted profitably now. A reserve is the part of a resource that has been located and measured and can be extracted at a profit with current technology and prices. Reserves change: if the price rises or new technology lowers costs, more of the resource becomes reserves; if the price falls, some reserves become uneconomic. Economic factors include the grade (concentration) of the ore, depth, size of deposit, accessibility and transport, extraction cost, market price and environmental restrictions.

### Common mistakes

- Using "resource" and "reserve" as the same thing.

## 4.2b Mineral resources: limestone, haematite, uranium

### What you need to know

- Mineral resources are important in construction, manufacturing and energy generation; investigate uses of limestone (aggregate in construction), haematite (steel industry) and uranium (energy generation).

### Explained

- Limestone: quarried in large amounts and crushed for aggregate (crushed rock used in concrete, road foundations and railway ballast); also used to make cement (heated with clay), lime for agriculture and steel-making flux, and as building stone. Quarries are large and close to markets because aggregate is heavy and cheap, so transport costs matter.
- Haematite (iron oxide, Fe₂O₃): the main ore of iron. In a blast furnace, it is reduced by carbon to iron, which is made into steel for construction, vehicles and machinery. Forms in banded iron formations, hydrothermal veins and sedimentary deposits.
- Uranium: found as minerals such as uraninite (pitchblende), in granites, hydrothermal veins and some sandstones. Mined, concentrated and enriched to fuel nuclear power stations, where fission releases large amounts of energy with very low carbon dioxide emissions, but produces long-lived radioactive waste (4.2g).

### Common mistakes

- Saying aggregate is a mineral. It is crushed rock used in construction.

## 4.2c Prospecting

### What you need to know

- Prospecting techniques: geological mapping; borehole correlation (using microfossils); geophysical methods (seismic, magnetic, ground-penetrating radar); geochemical methods (soil and river sediment analysis).
- Interpret prospecting data to identify possible valuable mineral resources.

### Explained

- Geological mapping: recording rock types, structures and mineralised veins at the surface; maps show where favourable rocks and structures (such as faults that may carry hydrothermal veins, or granite contacts) occur.
- Borehole correlation: drilling boreholes and matching layers between them by rock type and microfossils (tiny fossils that survive in drill cuttings, used as zone fossils, 2.3) to map the depth and thickness of layers underground, such as a coal seam or reservoir rock.
- Geophysical methods (measuring physical properties from the surface, aircraft or ships):
  - Seismic surveys: sound waves from small explosions or vibrating trucks reflect off rock boundaries; the echoes reveal underground structures such as anticlines and faults (vital for oil and gas).
  - Magnetic surveys: magnetometers detect anomalies (unusually strong readings) from magnetic minerals such as magnetite, or iron-rich igneous rocks.
  - Ground-penetrating radar: radar pulses reflect from shallow features (buried cavities, water table, sediment layers), useful for shallow engineering and archaeology.
  - (Gravity surveys detect dense ore bodies.)
- Geochemical methods: samples of soil or river (stream) sediment are analysed for metal concentrations. Unusually high values (an anomaly) suggest a deposit nearby. In streams, follow the anomaly upstream until values drop; the source lies between the last high and first low sample.

### Worked example

Stream sediment copper values (ppm) going upstream: 40, 45, 300, 320, 310, 50, 40. Background is about 40 to 50 ppm. High values start at the third sample and stop after the fifth, so the source of the copper is probably between the fifth and sixth sample sites (or in a tributary entering there).

### Common mistakes

- Looking for the source downstream of the anomaly. Sediment travels downstream, so the source is upstream.

## 4.2d and 4.2e Oil and gas

### What you need to know

- Structures and rock properties linked to the migration and accumulation of oil and gas onshore and offshore: source rock; contrasting porosity and permeability of reservoir and cap rocks; main traps (anticline, fault, unconformity, salt dome).
- Interpret maps, cross-sections and seismic surveys to identify possible oil and gas fields.
- Technological difficulties and environmental issues in exploring for and extracting oil and gas, including fracking.

### Explained

Formation: organic-rich mud (from plankton) accumulates in oxygen-poor water and becomes a source rock (black shale). Buried to depths with suitable temperatures (about 60 to 150 °C, the "oil window"), the organic matter is slowly converted to oil and then gas. Being less dense than water, oil and gas migrate upwards through permeable rocks.

Requirements for an oil field:

1. Source rock: organic-rich shale.
2. Reservoir rock: porous (to store oil and gas) and permeable (so they can flow to a well), e.g. sandstone or fractured limestone.
3. Cap (seal) rock: impermeable rock above the reservoir that stops further upward movement, e.g. shale, clay or salt.
4. Trap: a structure that collects oil and gas in one place.

Traps:

- Anticline trap: reservoir and cap rock folded into an arch; oil and gas collect at the crest. Gas on top, then oil, then water (by density).
- Fault trap: a fault moves the reservoir rock against impermeable rock, sealing it.
- Unconformity trap: tilted reservoir beds are cut off by an unconformity, and impermeable beds above seal them.
- Salt dome trap: a column of salt rises through the overlying rocks (salt is low density and flows), bending them upward; reservoir beds are sealed against the impermeable salt.

On seismic sections and cross-sections, look for a porous layer beneath an impermeable layer, arched or sealed by a fault, salt or unconformity, above a source rock.

Difficulties and issues: offshore drilling in deep water and stormy seas (North Sea) needs expensive platforms; drilling to great depths at high temperature and pressure; risk of blowouts, spills (damaging marine life and coasts) and gas flaring; pipelines and tankers can leak; burning oil and gas releases carbon dioxide (2.4). Fracking (hydraulic fracturing): water, sand and chemicals are pumped at high pressure into low-permeability shale to crack it and release gas. Concerns include triggering small earthquakes (as near Blackpool, UK, in 2011 and 2019), possible contamination of groundwater by fluids or methane, large water use, disposal of waste water, traffic and noise; supporters cite energy security.

### Common mistakes

- Saying oil forms in the reservoir rock. It forms in the source rock and migrates.
- Saying the cap rock must be porous. It must be impermeable.

## 4.2f Groundwater and aquifers

### What you need to know

- Factors affecting extraction of groundwater from aquifers: height of the water table, porosity and permeability of the aquifer, presence of natural springs, distribution of wells.
- Analyse rock types for their suitability as an aquifer.

### Explained

An aquifer is a body of porous and permeable rock that stores and transmits water (for example chalk, sandstone and some limestones in Britain). Rocks such as clay, shale and unfractured granite are aquicludes (impermeable). Groundwater fills the pores below the water table; above it, pores contain air and water (unsaturated zone).

- Water table height: wells must reach below the water table, preferably well below the lowest level it falls to in dry seasons. The water table rises in wet periods (recharge) and falls with drought and pumping.
- Porosity and permeability: high porosity stores lots of water; high permeability lets it flow quickly to wells.
- Springs: where the water table meets the surface, or where water flowing through an aquifer meets an impermeable layer below, water flows out naturally, a useful supply.
- Distribution of wells: pumping lowers the water table around a well in a cone of depression. Wells too close together interfere; over-pumping lowers the water table, dries up springs and wells, and in coastal areas can draw salt water into the aquifer.
- Confined aquifers sandwiched between impermeable layers may be under pressure (artesian), so water rises up the well on its own.

### Common mistakes

- Saying an aquifer is an underground lake. Water fills pores and cracks in rock.

## 4.2g Waste disposal and aquifers

### What you need to know

- The impact of domestic and hazardous waste disposal on vulnerable aquifers depends on geological factors (permeability), engineering factors (geomembranes), monitoring of potentially polluted water, and restoration of contaminated ground.
- Investigate the suitability of sites for landfill of domestic waste and for long-term storage of hazardous waste.

### Explained

Landfill waste produces leachate (polluted liquid from rainwater percolating through waste) and landfill gas (methane). If leachate reaches an aquifer, drinking water supplies can be contaminated.

- Geological factors: an ideal landfill site is on impermeable rock or clay (to contain leachate), away from aquifers, faults and joints (pathways for pollutants), with a deep water table, stable ground and not on a floodplain.
- Engineering factors: sites are lined with compacted clay and synthetic geomembranes (plastic liners); leachate is collected by drains and treated; gas is collected and burned or used for energy; the site is capped when full.
- Monitoring: boreholes around the site are sampled regularly to check groundwater quality and detect leaks early.
- Restoration: contaminated land and groundwater can be cleaned by removing polluted soil, pumping and treating water, or using natural processes; closed landfills are capped and landscaped.

Hazardous waste, including high-level radioactive waste, needs storage for many thousands of years. A geological disposal facility should be deep underground (hundreds of metres) in stable, low-permeability rock (clay, salt, or unfractured granite), in a tectonically stable area away from faults, earthquakes and volcanoes, with slow groundwater flow and no valuable resources that people might drill for in future, plus engineered barriers (canisters, clay buffer).

### Common mistakes

- Choosing permeable sandstone or chalk as a landfill site.

## 4.2h Siting engineering projects

### What you need to know

- Geological factors affect the siting of reservoirs, dams, tunnels and cuttings: permeability, stability of bedrock, dip of strata, presence of faults and joints.
- Use descriptions, diagrams, photographs, maps and cross-sections to investigate geological factors.

### Explained

| Project | Good geology | Problems to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reservoir | Impermeable rocks under the reservoir floor and sides (so water does not leak away); stable valley sides | Permeable or soluble rock (limestone with caves, sandstone); faults that could leak; landslide-prone slopes that could slide into the water and cause a wave |
| Dam | Strong, impermeable, unweathered bedrock for foundations; narrow valley | Faults (could move and leak); weak or weathered rock; beds dipping downstream (could slide); joints that let water pass under the dam |
| Tunnel | Strong, uniform, impermeable rock that needs little support; few faults | Faults and shattered rock (collapse); water-bearing permeable layers (flooding); changes in rock type (cutting difficulties); swelling clays |
| Cutting (road or rail) | Beds dipping into the slope or horizontal; strong rock | Beds or joints dipping towards the road (rock slides); clay that slumps when wet; faults |

Site investigation uses geological mapping, boreholes, geophysical surveys, and data from the British Geological Survey and Environment Agency (often in a GIS, 14).

### Common mistakes

- Siting a reservoir on cavernous limestone.
- Ignoring the dip direction relative to a cutting face.

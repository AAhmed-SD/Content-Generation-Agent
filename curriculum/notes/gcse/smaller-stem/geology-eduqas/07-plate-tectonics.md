# 2.2 Plate tectonics

Plate tectonics is the unifying theory of geology: it explains earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain belts and ocean basins. This topic covers the Earth's layers, the lithosphere and asthenosphere, how the theory grew from continental drift, the evidence, what happens at each type of plate boundary, and how the theory is still being tested. Background on the Earth's layers is also in [Astronomy 01 Planet Earth](../astronomy-edexcel/01-planet-earth.md).

## 2.2a, 2.2b and 2.2c The Earth's structure, lithosphere and plates

### What you need to know

- The Earth has a concentric structure based on chemical properties (crust, mantle, core) and on mechanical behaviour (lithosphere, asthenosphere).
- The lithosphere is a cold, rigid outer shell of crust and uppermost mantle, underlain by the weaker asthenosphere in the upper mantle.
- The lithosphere is divided into rigid tectonic plates that move relative to each other, by mechanisms not yet completely understood.

### Explained

Chemical layers:

- Crust: oceanic crust (basalt, thin: about 7 km, denser, young) and continental crust (granitic on average, thick: 30 to 70 km, less dense, can be very old).
- Mantle: silicate rock rich in iron and magnesium (peridotite), to about 2900 km.
- Core: iron and nickel; liquid outer core, solid inner core.

Mechanical layers (by how the rock behaves):

- Lithosphere: the crust plus the uppermost mantle, about 100 km thick (thinner under oceans, thicker under continents). Cold and rigid; it breaks into plates.
- Asthenosphere: below the lithosphere, in the upper mantle, to about 350 km. Hot enough to be weak and to flow very slowly (a tiny amount of it may be molten), so the lithosphere plates can move over it.

There are about seven major plates and many smaller ones, moving a few centimetres per year. Proposed driving mechanisms include convection currents in the mantle, ridge push (elevated, hot ridges sliding down and away) and slab pull (cold, dense subducting plates pulling the rest of the plate behind them, thought to be the largest force). The exact balance is still not fully understood.

### Common mistakes

- Equating lithosphere with crust. The lithosphere includes the uppermost mantle.
- Saying the asthenosphere is liquid magma.

## 2.2d and 2.2e From continental drift to plate tectonics; the evidence

### What you need to know

- Plate tectonic theory developed from continental drift as new evidence appeared: Wegener (1915) proposed continental drift; evidence for sea-floor spreading came from Hess (1960), Vine and Matthews (1963) and J. Tuzo Wilson (1965).
- Evidence for plate tectonics and for the direction and rate of movement: jigsaw fit, fossil distributions, heat flow, magnetic stripes, age of the ocean floor, GPS data.
- Use maps of earthquakes, volcanoes and mountain belts to interpret processes at plate boundaries.

### Explained

Alfred Wegener (1915) proposed that the continents were once joined in a supercontinent (Pangaea) and had drifted apart. His evidence: the jigsaw fit of coastlines (especially South America and Africa, even better at the edge of the continental shelf); matching fossils (such as the land reptile Mesosaurus and the plant Glossopteris) on continents now separated by oceans; matching rock types and mountain belts across oceans; evidence of past climates in the wrong places (glacial deposits in tropical areas, coal in Antarctica). But he could not explain a mechanism, and most geologists rejected drift.

New evidence from the ocean floor after the Second World War:

- Harry Hess (1960) proposed sea-floor spreading: new ocean floor forms at mid-ocean ridges from rising mantle material and spreads outwards, and is destroyed in ocean trenches. He used maps of the sea floor (ridges, trenches) and high heat flow at ridges.
- Vine and Matthews (1963) explained the magnetic stripes on the sea floor: as basalt lava cools at a ridge, iron minerals record the direction of the Earth's magnetic field. The field reverses every few hundred thousand years, so the sea floor has stripes of normal and reversed magnetism, symmetrical on either side of the ridge, like a tape recorder. This confirmed sea-floor spreading.
- J. Tuzo Wilson (1965) identified transform faults, which offset ridges, and described the plates as rigid units with three kinds of boundary; he also explained hotspot chains (such as Hawaii) as plates moving over a fixed hotspot. These completed the theory of plate tectonics.

Summary of evidence:

| Evidence | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| Jigsaw fit of continents | Continents were once joined |
| Fossil distributions | Land animals and plants could not have crossed oceans, so the continents were joined |
| Heat flow | High at ridges (hot rising mantle), low at trenches (cold sinking slabs) |
| Magnetic stripes | Symmetrical pattern of reversals either side of ridges: new crust forms at ridges and spreads |
| Age of ocean floor | Youngest at ridges, older away from them; nowhere older than about 180 million years, so ocean floor is destroyed; thin sediment at ridges |
| GPS data | Satellites measure present plate movement directly: centimetres per year, in the directions predicted |
| Global distributions | Earthquakes, volcanoes and young mountain belts are concentrated in narrow belts that outline the plates |

Rate calculation: rate = distance ÷ time.

### Worked example

Basalt 150 km from a ridge axis is 6 million years old. Rate of spreading on that side = 150 km ÷ 6 Ma = 25 km per million years = 2.5 cm per year (because 25 km = 2 500 000 cm, divided by 1 000 000 years). The total opening rate (both sides) is 5 cm per year.

### Common mistakes

- Saying Wegener proposed plate tectonics. He proposed continental drift without a workable mechanism.
- Saying magnetic stripes show the continents' positions. They record reversals of the field in new crust.

## 2.2f Plate boundaries

### What you need to know

- Relative plate movements produce different magma types, structures and topography at different boundaries:
  - Divergent: basalt extrusion, sea-floor spreading, basaltic magma by partial melting of the upper mantle, ocean ridges, high heat flow, rift valleys, abyssal plain (example: Mid-Atlantic Ridge).
  - Conservative: earthquakes, transform faults (example: San Andreas fault zone).
  - Convergent: oceanic-oceanic (island arc and trench, examples: Java-Sumatra, Caribbean); oceanic-continental (active continental margin, subduction zone, Benioff zone, partial melting giving andesitic and granitic magma, example: the Andes); continental-continental (mountain building, folding, thrust faulting, crustal melting giving granite, regional metamorphism, example: the Himalaya).
- Interpret plate movements and boundary types from magmatic, seismic and topographic data on maps and diagrams.

### Explained

Divergent (constructive) boundaries: plates move apart. As the lithosphere thins and pressure on the hot mantle drops, the upper mantle partially melts (only a small fraction melts), producing basaltic magma that rises and erupts as basalt (often pillow lava) or intrudes as dykes, forming new oceanic crust. Features: mid-ocean ridges (an underwater mountain chain with a central rift valley), high heat flow, shallow earthquakes, fissure eruptions; away from the ridge, the cooled crust subsides to form the flat, deep abyssal plain covered with fine sediment. On land, continental rifting forms rift valleys bounded by normal faults (East African Rift). Example: Mid-Atlantic Ridge (Iceland is a part above sea level).

Conservative boundaries: plates slide past each other along transform faults. No crust is made or destroyed; no volcanoes. Stress builds up and is released as frequent, often strong, shallow earthquakes. Example: San Andreas fault zone (Pacific and North American plates).

Convergent (destructive) boundaries: plates move towards each other.

1. Oceanic-oceanic: one oceanic plate (usually the older, colder, denser one) subducts beneath the other into the mantle, forming a deep ocean trench. Water released from the sinking slab lowers the melting point of the mantle above, causing partial melting; magma rises to build a curved chain of volcanic islands (an island arc). Examples: Java-Sumatra (Indonesia), Caribbean (Lesser Antilles).
2. Oceanic-continental: dense oceanic lithosphere subducts beneath less dense continental lithosphere, forming a trench offshore and an active continental margin. Earthquakes occur along the sinking slab, getting deeper inland (the inclined zone of earthquakes is the Benioff zone, down to about 700 km). Partial melting produces andesitic magma (explosive composite volcanoes) and granitic magma (large plutons); compression folds and thrusts the crust into fold mountains. Example: the Andes (Nazca plate under the South American plate).
3. Continental-continental: when an ocean between two continents has closed, neither continent subducts (both are too buoyant). The crust is crumpled and thickened into huge fold mountains with folding and thrust faulting. Thickened crust partly melts to form granite; deep rocks undergo regional metamorphism. Few or no volcanoes; shallow to intermediate earthquakes over a wide zone. Example: the Himalaya (India colliding with Asia).

Interpreting data: a linear belt of shallow earthquakes along a ridge with basalt and high heat flow → divergent. Earthquakes getting deeper in one direction, with a trench and andesite volcanoes → subduction (the slab dips towards the deeper earthquakes). Shallow earthquakes, no volcanoes, offset features → conservative. Wide belt of shallow earthquakes, high mountains, no volcanoes → continental collision.

### Common mistakes

- Saying continental plates subduct beneath oceanic plates. Denser oceanic lithosphere subducts.
- Saying subduction melts the sinking plate completely to make magma. Melting is mainly in the mantle wedge above, helped by water from the slab.
- Putting volcanoes at conservative boundaries.

## 2.2g Continuing re-evaluation of plate theory

### What you need to know

- Plate theory is re-evaluated in the light of new evidence, for example seismic tomography and ocean drilling (RRS James Cook; JOIDES Resolution Expedition 360, 2016).

### Explained

- Seismic tomography uses earthquake waves recorded at stations around the world to build 3D images of the mantle, like a medical CT scan. Seismic waves travel faster through cold, dense rock and slower through hot rock. Images show cold subducted slabs sinking deep into the mantle (some to the core-mantle boundary) and hot plumes rising, helping test ideas about mantle convection and driving forces.
- Ocean drilling: scientific drilling ships recover cores of sediment and rock from the ocean floor. They confirmed the ages of ocean crust increase away from ridges and allow study of the lower crust and mantle. JOIDES Resolution Expedition 360 (2016) drilled into lower oceanic crust at Atlantis Bank in the south-west Indian Ocean to understand how slow-spreading ridges form crust and how the mantle is exposed. The research ship RRS James Cook has carried out related seabed surveys and sampling.

The theory is still developing: questions remain about what exactly drives plates, how subduction starts, and the role of mantle plumes. This shows science changes as new evidence is collected.

### Common mistakes

- Treating plate tectonics as finished science with nothing left to discover.

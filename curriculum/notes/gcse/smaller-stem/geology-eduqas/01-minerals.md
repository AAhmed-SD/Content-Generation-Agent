# 1.1 Minerals

Rocks are made of minerals: naturally occurring, inorganic solids with a definite chemical composition and an ordered crystal structure. This topic covers how the named minerals form, the tests used to identify them, and the laboratory techniques that image them at a tiny scale.

## 1.1a How minerals form, and identifying them

### What you need to know

- Minerals form by:
  - crystallisation from a melt (quartz, feldspar, mica, olivine, augite);
  - metamorphic recrystallisation (calcite, garnet);
  - crystallisation from evaporating water (halite);
  - crystallisation as cement from pore waters flowing through sediment (quartz, calcite);
  - crystallisation from hydrothermal fluids in veins and faults (gangue minerals quartz and calcite; ore minerals haematite and galena).
- Use tests of physical properties (colour, hardness, streak, cleavage, lustre) and reaction with 0.5 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid to identify and distinguish the minerals on the data sheet; interpret data sheet information.

### Explained

Ways minerals form:

- From a melt: as magma or lava cools, atoms join into crystals of silicate minerals. Olivine and augite (dark, iron- and magnesium-rich) crystallise at higher temperatures and are typical of basalt and peridotite; feldspar, mica and quartz crystallise at lower temperatures and are typical of granite.
- Metamorphic recrystallisation: heat and pressure rearrange the atoms of existing minerals into new or larger crystals without melting. Calcite in limestone recrystallises into larger interlocking crystals in marble; garnet grows in schists as new mineral from clay minerals at high temperature and pressure.
- From evaporating water: when sea water or salty lake water evaporates, the dissolved salts become concentrated and crystallise. Halite (rock salt) forms this way, as do gypsum and other evaporites.
- As cement: water moving through the pore spaces of buried sediment carries dissolved silica or calcium carbonate, which crystallises between the grains as quartz or calcite cement, binding the sediment into rock.
- From hydrothermal fluids: hot, mineral-rich water, often heated by nearby magma, flows along cracks such as joints and faults. As it cools, minerals crystallise and fill the cracks, forming mineral veins. The economically valuable ore minerals (haematite, an iron ore; galena, a lead ore) are mixed with worthless gangue minerals (quartz, calcite).

Physical tests:

| Property | Meaning and method |
| --- | --- |
| Colour | Often unreliable, because small impurities change it (quartz can be clear, white, pink, purple) |
| Streak | Colour of the mineral's powder, made by scraping it on an unglazed white tile; more reliable than colour. Haematite gives a red-brown streak even when the crystal is black or silvery; galena gives a grey-black streak |
| Hardness | Resistance to scratching, on Mohs' scale from 1 (talc) to 10 (diamond). Simple tools: fingernail about 2.5, copper coin about 3.5, steel pin or knife about 5.5. A mineral that scratches another is harder |
| Cleavage | Tendency to break along flat planes of weakness in the crystal structure. Mica has one perfect cleavage (peels into thin sheets); feldspar two at about 90°; calcite three not at right angles (rhombs); halite and galena three at 90° (cubes); quartz has no cleavage (it fractures with curved, glassy surfaces) |
| Lustre | How the surface reflects light: metallic (galena, some haematite), glassy/vitreous (quartz), pearly (mica, feldspar faces), dull/earthy |
| Acid test | A drop of dilute (0.5 mol dm⁻³) hydrochloric acid fizzes on calcite (carbon dioxide released); no reaction with quartz |
| Density | Felt by "hefting" in the hand: galena (about 7.5 g/cm³) feels much heavier than quartz (2.65 g/cm³) of the same size |
| Taste | Halite tastes salty (only on advice; generally not recommended in the lab) |

Typical identification features:

| Mineral | Key features |
| --- | --- |
| Quartz | Hardness 7 (scratches steel), glassy lustre, no cleavage, no acid reaction, often white or clear |
| Feldspar | Hardness 6, white, cream or pink, two cleavages, blocky crystals |
| Mica | Hardness 2.5 to 3, splits into thin flexible sheets; black (biotite) or silvery (muscovite) |
| Olivine | Hardness 6.5 to 7, olive green, glassy, granular |
| Augite | Hardness 6, dark green to black, two cleavages near 90° |
| Calcite | Hardness 3 (scratched by copper coin), rhombic cleavage, fizzes in dilute acid |
| Garnet | Hardness 6.5 to 7.5, red-brown rounded crystals in metamorphic rocks |
| Halite | Hardness 2.5, cubic crystals and cleavage, salty, dissolves in water |
| Haematite | Red-brown streak, metallic or earthy lustre, dense |
| Galena | Grey metallic lustre, cubic cleavage, very dense, grey-black streak, hardness 2.5 |

### Worked example

A mineral scratches a copper coin but is scratched by a steel pin, has a red-brown streak and feels heavy. Hardness between about 3.5 and 5.5; red-brown streak; dense: haematite.

A white mineral is scratched by a copper coin and fizzes in dilute acid: calcite (not quartz, which is harder and does not fizz).

### Common mistakes

- Relying on colour alone.
- Saying quartz has cleavage.
- Saying calcite is a gangue mineral only. It is gangue in veins, but also forms by other routes (cement, metamorphism).

## 1.1b Modern laboratory techniques

### What you need to know

- Modern laboratory techniques can image mineral samples at a small scale and determine their chemistry, for example the scanning electron microscope (SEM) and the electron microprobe. (Access to these is not required.)

### Explained

- Scanning electron microscope (SEM): a focused beam of electrons is scanned across a sample. Electrons scattered or emitted from the surface are detected to build a highly magnified image (thousands of times), showing tiny crystals, grain surfaces, microfossils and textures that a light microscope cannot resolve.
- Electron microprobe: an electron beam is focused on a tiny spot of a polished sample; the atoms emit X-rays with energies characteristic of each element. Measuring these gives the exact chemical composition of individual mineral grains, or even zones within a single crystal (for example how a crystal's composition changed as a magma cooled).

These techniques let geologists identify minerals too small to test by hand, and understand the conditions in which rocks formed.

### Common mistakes

- Saying an SEM uses light. It uses an electron beam.

## Check questions

1. Name the mineral formed by evaporation of sea water. [1 mark]
2. Describe how minerals form from hydrothermal fluids, naming one ore mineral and one gangue mineral. [4 marks]
3. Explain how quartz and calcite can act as cement in sandstone. [2 marks]
4. Explain why streak is a more reliable property than colour for identifying haematite. [2 marks]
5. Describe two simple tests that would distinguish calcite from quartz. [4 marks]

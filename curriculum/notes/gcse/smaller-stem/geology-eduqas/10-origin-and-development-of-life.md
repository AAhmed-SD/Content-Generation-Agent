# 2.5 The origin and development of life on Earth

The fossil record shows how life has changed over about 3.5 billion years. This topic covers where life probably began, the broad pattern of diversification, mass extinctions (especially the K/Pg event), and famous fossil finds that show exceptional preservation, evolutionary links, the challenges of reconstructing skeletons, and early human relatives, along with the limits of the fossil record.

## 2.5a The origin of life

### What you need to know

- Life probably originated in the oceans or in hydrothermal pools about 3500 Ma (black smokers).

### Explained

The oldest widely accepted evidence of life is about 3500 million years old (3.5 Ga): microscopic fossils of single cells, and stromatolites, layered mounds built by mats of bacteria trapping sediment in shallow water. Chemical signatures in even older rocks may point to life earlier.

Hydrothermal vents (black smokers) on the deep sea floor at mid-ocean ridges are a leading candidate for where life began. Hot, mineral-rich water (heated by magma) gushes out and precipitates black metal sulfides. They provide energy (chemical energy from reactions between the vent fluids and sea water), a supply of elements, and protection from harmful ultraviolet radiation at the surface. Today they support communities of bacteria and animals that do not rely on sunlight. An alternative is warm, shallow hydrothermal pools on land, where repeated wetting and drying could concentrate the chemicals needed.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the first life was on land.

## 2.5b The diversification of life

### What you need to know

- The fossil record shows increasing diversity: single cells, multicellular organisms, animals with hard parts, fish, amphibians, reptiles, mammals, birds and humans.
- Use simple evolutionary tree diagrams (cladograms) to show trends; use the data sheet.

### Explained

Approximate order (Ma = millions of years ago):

| First appearance | Approximate time |
| --- | --- |
| Single-celled life (bacteria) | About 3500 Ma |
| Complex single cells with a nucleus | About 2000 Ma |
| Multicellular organisms (soft-bodied, e.g. Ediacaran fauna) | About 600 Ma |
| Animals with hard parts (shells, skeletons): "Cambrian explosion" | About 540 Ma |
| Fish | About 500 Ma (Ordovician) |
| Land plants | About 470 to 430 Ma |
| Amphibians | About 370 Ma (Devonian) |
| Reptiles | About 310 Ma (Carboniferous) |
| Mammals | About 200 Ma (Triassic–Jurassic) |
| Birds | About 150 Ma (Jurassic) |
| Humans (Homo sapiens) | About 0.3 Ma |

Hard parts (shells, bones, teeth) are far more easily fossilised than soft tissue, so the fossil record becomes much richer from the Cambrian onwards. Life moved from sea to land as plants, then invertebrates, then vertebrates (amphibians) colonised it.

A cladogram is a branching diagram showing how groups are related: groups that share a more recent common ancestor are joined by a more recent branch point. For example, birds branch from within the reptile line (closest to dinosaurs), and mammals branch from an earlier reptile-like ancestor. Reading up the tree shows the order in which features (such as backbone, four limbs, eggs with a shell, feathers) appeared.

### Common mistakes

- Saying humans lived with the dinosaurs.
- Reading a cladogram as a ladder, with modern groups "evolving into" each other.

## 2.5c Mass extinctions

### What you need to know

- Life's development was interrupted by major extinction events, for example the Cretaceous/Palaeogene (K/Pg) mass extinction.

### Explained

A mass extinction is when a large proportion of species dies out in a geologically short time. There have been five major mass extinctions; the largest was at the end of the Permian (about 252 Ma), probably linked to huge volcanic eruptions in Siberia.

The K/Pg (Cretaceous/Palaeogene) extinction, 66 Ma, wiped out about three-quarters of species, including the dinosaurs (except birds), ammonites and many marine reptiles. Evidence for a meteorite impact as the main cause:

- A thin clay layer at the K/Pg boundary worldwide, enriched in iridium, an element rare in the Earth's crust but common in meteorites.
- Shocked quartz (with special deformation features formed only at extreme pressures) and tiny glass spherules in the boundary layer.
- The Chicxulub crater, about 180 km across, buried under the Yucatán Peninsula, Mexico, dated to 66 Ma.
- Tsunami deposits around the Gulf of Mexico.

The impact threw dust and sulfur aerosols into the atmosphere, blocking sunlight, collapsing food chains and causing a sudden cold period, followed by warming. Massive volcanic eruptions in India (the Deccan Traps) at about the same time may also have contributed.

After a mass extinction, surviving groups diversify into empty niches: mammals diversified rapidly after the K/Pg extinction.

### Common mistakes

- Saying only the dinosaurs died out at the K/Pg boundary.

## 2.5d Major fossil finds and the incomplete fossil record

### What you need to know

- Major finds show: rare and exceptional preservation (Burgess Shale fauna); links between groups seen in morphology of modern reptiles and birds (Archaeopteryx); the need to interpret complex skeletons from incomplete, disarticulated remains (dinosaurs); features of early hominids ("Lucy").
- Evaluate the significance of the incompleteness of the fossil record.

### Explained

- Burgess Shale (Canada, about 508 Ma, Middle Cambrian): fine mud slumped rapidly onto animals and buried them in oxygen-poor conditions, preserving soft bodies (legs, guts, gills) as thin films. It shows the great variety of strange animals that appeared in the Cambrian, many with no close living relatives. This exceptional preservation is very rare and shows how much is normally lost.
- Archaeopteryx (Germany, about 150 Ma, Late Jurassic, in fine Solnhofen limestone): has feathers and wings like a bird, but also reptile (dinosaur) features: teeth in its jaws, claws on its wings, and a long bony tail. It is a transitional fossil linking reptiles (theropod dinosaurs) and birds.
- Dinosaurs: skeletons are usually found incomplete and the bones scattered (disarticulated) by scavengers, currents or decay. Palaeontologists reconstruct them by comparing bones with other specimens and with living animals (birds and reptiles), using uniformitarianism. Reconstructions have changed over time (for example, Iguanodon's thumb spike was first placed on its nose), showing interpretation can be revised as more complete finds appear.
- "Lucy" (Australopithecus afarensis, Ethiopia, about 3.2 Ma): about 40% of a skeleton of an early hominin. Her pelvis and leg bones show she walked upright on two legs, but her brain was small, about the size of a chimpanzee's, and her arms long. This showed upright walking evolved before large brains.

The incomplete fossil record: only a tiny fraction of organisms become fossils. Fossilisation needs rapid burial, hard parts, and the right conditions; most remains are eaten, decay or are destroyed. Many fossils are then lost by erosion, metamorphism or subduction, or are not exposed or found. Soft-bodied organisms and land animals in eroding areas are especially under-represented. So gaps in the record (missing transitional forms) are expected, the first and last appearances of species are approximate, and diversity in the past is underestimated. Exceptional sites (Lagerstätten) such as the Burgess Shale give rare, detailed windows.

### Common mistakes

- Calling Archaeopteryx a modern bird.
- Saying gaps in the fossil record prove evolution did not happen. Gaps are expected from how fossils form.

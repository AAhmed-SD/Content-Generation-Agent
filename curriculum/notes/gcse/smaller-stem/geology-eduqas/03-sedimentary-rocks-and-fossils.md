# 1.3 Sedimentary rocks and their fossil content

Sedimentary rocks record the surface environments of the past: rivers, deserts, beaches, reefs, deep seas and ice. This topic covers how sediment is made and moved, what grain size, shape and sorting reveal, porosity and permeability, the named sedimentary rocks and how to identify them, depositional environments and graphic logs, sedimentary structures, and fossils as environmental clues.

## 1.3a Weathering, erosion, transport and deposition

### What you need to know

- Rock is broken down by weathering and erosion into particles of various sizes and dissolved materials, which are transported and deposited to form new sediments.
- Distinguish weathering from erosion and evaluate their significance in the characteristics and history of sedimentary rock.

### Explained

- Weathering is the breakdown of rock in place, without movement:
  - Physical (mechanical): freeze-thaw (water expands by about 9% as it freezes in cracks, prising rock apart), thermal expansion and contraction, plant roots. Produces angular fragments with unchanged minerals.
  - Chemical: reactions with water, oxygen and acids. Rainwater with dissolved carbon dioxide (weak carbonic acid) dissolves calcite in limestone; feldspar decomposes to clay minerals; iron minerals oxidise (rust). Quartz is very resistant, so it survives to form sand grains.
  - Biological: plant roots, burrowing animals and organic acids.
- Erosion is the removal of material by a moving agent: rivers, the sea, wind, ice and gravity. Moving particles also wear each other down (abrasion and attrition).
- Transport: particles are carried by rolling, bouncing, in suspension, or dissolved in solution.
- Deposition: when the transporting agent loses energy (a river slows entering a lake or the sea; wind drops), the particles settle out, largest first. Dissolved material is deposited by evaporation (evaporites) or by organisms (shells forming limestone).
- Lithification: buried sediment is compacted (squeezed, water expelled) and cemented (minerals crystallise between grains, 1.1) into rock.

Significance: rocks dominated by angular fragments (breccia) suggest little transport after physical weathering; clean, rounded quartz sand suggests long transport and strong chemical weathering that removed less stable minerals.

### Common mistakes

- Using "weathering" and "erosion" interchangeably. Weathering does not involve movement.

## 1.3b Grain size, shape, sorting and energy

### What you need to know

- The grain size, shape and sorting of sediment depend on the energy of the transporting medium and the depositional environment (screes, rivers, shallow and deep seas, wind-formed dunes).
- Interpret distance of transport from shape and sorting, and energy of the environment from grain size.

### Explained

- Grain size reflects energy: fast-flowing water or strong wind can move large particles; only fine particles are carried by slow water and settle in calm water. So coarse sediments (gravel) show high energy (fast rivers, storm beaches); fine mud shows very low energy (deep sea, lakes, lagoons).
- Grain shape (roundness and sphericity): angular grains have travelled little (scree at the foot of a cliff); grains become more rounded the further and longer they are transported, as corners are knocked off. Wind-blown sand grains are very well rounded and often "frosted" by impacts.
- Sorting: well sorted means grains are all of a similar size (the transporting agent selected one size range, such as wind-blown dune sand or beach sand); poorly sorted means a mixture of sizes (dumped quickly: scree, glacial till, flash flood deposits).

| Environment | Energy | Typical sediment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scree | Gravity; no transport | Very angular, poorly sorted, coarse |
| River channel | Moderate to high | Rounded pebbles and sand, moderately sorted |
| Shallow sea / beach | Moderate (waves) | Well sorted, rounded sand; shells |
| Deep sea | Very low | Fine mud and clay |
| Desert dunes (wind) | Low to moderate, selective | Very well sorted, very well rounded, frosted sand |

### Common mistakes

- Saying rounded grains have travelled a short distance.
- Mixing up sorting (range of sizes) with roundness (shape).

## 1.3c Porosity and permeability

### What you need to know

- The porosity and permeability of sedimentary rock depend on the characteristics of the original sediment and the degree of compaction and cementation.
- Distinguish permeable from impermeable rocks by observing water dropped on specimens and/or immersing them.

### Explained

- Porosity: the percentage of a rock's volume that is pore space. Porosity (%) = volume of pores ÷ total volume × 100.
- Permeability: how easily a fluid (water, oil, gas) can flow through the rock, which needs connected pores (or fractures).

Factors:

- Sorting: well-sorted sediment has high porosity; in poorly sorted sediment, small grains fill the gaps between large ones.
- Grain shape and packing: rounded grains pack with more space than angular grains that interlock.
- Grain size: clay and mud can have high porosity but very low permeability, because the pores are tiny and water clings to the grains.
- Compaction and cementation: burial squeezes grains closer together and cement fills pores, reducing both porosity and permeability. An uncemented sandstone is porous and permeable; a well-cemented one much less so.

Tests: drop water onto a dry specimen: on a permeable rock (sandstone, chalk) it soaks in quickly; on an impermeable rock (shale, granite) it beads up or runs off. Immersion: weigh a dry rock, immerse it in water (bubbles show air escaping from pores), then weigh it again; the gain in mass in grams equals the volume of water absorbed in cm³, from which porosity can be calculated.

### Worked example

A dry sandstone cube of volume 50 cm³ has mass 110 g. After soaking, its mass is 118 g. Water absorbed = 8 g = 8 cm³. Porosity = 8 ÷ 50 × 100 = 16%.

### Common mistakes

- Saying a porous rock must be permeable (clay is porous but impermeable).

## 1.3d Identifying sedimentary rocks

### What you need to know

- Breccia, conglomerate, sandstone, shale, evaporites and limestone have diagnostic properties: colour, texture, reaction with acid, mineralogy and other features.
- Identify them using sediment comparators (grain size, shape and sphericity); coarse > 2 mm, fine < 1/16 mm; reaction with 0.5 mol dm⁻³ HCl.
- Construct and apply a classification system or key.

### Explained

Sedimentary rocks are made of grains (clasts) or crystals; clastic rocks are often fragmental, with grains held by cement; layered; may contain fossils.

| Rock | Grain size | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Breccia | Coarse (clasts over 2 mm) | Angular fragments, poorly sorted |
| Conglomerate | Coarse (over 2 mm) | Rounded pebbles in a finer matrix |
| Sandstone | Medium (1/16 to 2 mm) | Sand grains (mostly quartz), feels gritty; red if iron-oxide cement; does not fizz unless calcite-cemented |
| Shale | Fine (under 1/16 mm) | Clay and silt; splits into thin layers (fissile); grey or black; soft |
| Limestone | Varies | Mainly calcite; fizzes strongly in dilute HCl; often contains fossils or shell fragments; chalk is a fine white limestone |
| Evaporites (halite, gypsum) | Crystalline | Formed by evaporation; halite tastes salty and dissolves; gypsum is soft (scratched by a fingernail) |

A sediment comparator is a card with grains of known sizes and shapes; a sample is compared with it using a hand lens to estimate grain size, roundness and sphericity.

Simple key:

1. Does it fizz in dilute acid? Yes → limestone. No → go to 2.
2. Is it made of interlocking crystals that are soft or salty? Yes → evaporite. No → go to 3.
3. Are grains over 2 mm? Yes → go to 4. No → go to 5.
4. Are the clasts angular? Yes → breccia. No (rounded) → conglomerate.
5. Can individual grains be seen or felt (gritty)? Yes → sandstone. No (smooth, fine, splits into layers) → shale.

### Common mistakes

- Calling a coarse rock with rounded clasts a breccia.
- Forgetting that a sandstone with calcite cement can also fizz slightly.

## 1.3e Environments of deposition and graphic logs

### What you need to know

- Rock type depends on environment: shallow marine (limestone, sandstone, conglomerate); deep marine (turbidites, black shale); terrestrial: rivers and deltas (shale, sandstone, conglomerate, coal); deserts by wind and water (breccia, desert sandstone); evaporating saline water (halite, gypsum); ice (glacial till/tillite).
- Use textures, structures, mineralogy and fossils to interpret environments; analyse sedimentary rocks on maps, cross-sections and graphic logs; construct a simple graphic log from bed thickness and grain size data.

### Explained

| Environment | Rocks | Clues |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shallow marine | Limestone (warm, clear water, reefs), sandstone (beach and offshore), conglomerate (beach) | Marine fossils (corals, shells), ripple marks, cross bedding, well-sorted sand |
| Deep marine | Turbidites (repeated graded beds of sandstone and shale from underwater avalanches), black shale (fine mud in still, oxygen-poor water) | Graded bedding, fine lamination, graptolites in black shale, no shallow-water fossils |
| Rivers and deltas | Conglomerate (channels), sandstone (channels and bars), shale (floodplains), coal (swamp plants on deltas) | Cross bedding, channel shapes, plant fossils, desiccation cracks on floodplains |
| Desert | Breccia (scree, flash floods), desert sandstone (dunes) | Red colour (iron oxide), large-scale cross bedding, frosted well-rounded grains, desiccation cracks, no fossils |
| Evaporating saline water | Halite, gypsum | Shallow seas or salt lakes in hot dry climates |
| Ice | Glacial till / tillite | Very poorly sorted mixture of clay to boulders, angular clasts, sometimes scratched (striated) |

A graphic log is a vertical diagram of a sequence of sedimentary beds, drawn from the bottom (oldest) upwards. The vertical axis is thickness (to scale); the width of each bed shows its grain size (wider = coarser: clay, silt, sand, gravel); symbols show the rock type, structures and fossils. Logs show how environments changed over time, for example a coarsening-upward sequence as a delta built out into the sea.

### Worked example

Field data (bottom to top): shale 2.0 m (clay); sandstone 1.5 m (medium sand) with cross bedding; conglomerate 0.5 m (pebbles); coal 0.3 m. Draw columns to scale (for example 1 cm = 0.5 m), with widths increasing from shale to sandstone to conglomerate, a narrow column for coal, and symbols. Interpretation: deposits of an advancing river or delta, ending in a swamp.

### Common mistakes

- Drawing a graphic log from the top down.
- Ignoring fossils and structures when deciding the environment.

## 1.3f Sedimentary structures

### What you need to know

- Lamination and bedding, cross bedding, graded bedding, ripple marks and desiccation cracks are characteristic of their environments.

### Explained

| Structure | Description | Environment and meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bedding and lamination | Layers (beds over 1 cm; laminae under 1 cm) separated by bedding planes | Each layer is a change in deposition; fine lamination shows calm water |
| Cross bedding | Inclined layers within a bed, cut off at the top | Migrating ripples or dunes in rivers, shallow seas or deserts (large-scale in desert dunes); shows current direction (the layers slope down in the direction of flow) and which way up the rock is |
| Graded bedding | Grains coarsest at the base of a bed, getting finer upwards | A sudden flow that slowed down, such as a turbidity current in deep water; shows the way up |
| Ripple marks | Small ridges on a bedding surface | Symmetrical ripples: waves moving back and forth (shallow water); asymmetrical: one-way current (river, wind), with the steep side facing downstream |
| Desiccation (mud) cracks | Polygonal cracks in mudstone, often filled by sediment above | Wet mud dried out in the open air: floodplain, lake margin, desert; shows the way up (cracks taper downwards) |

### Common mistakes

- Saying cross bedding is caused by folding. It forms during deposition.

## 1.3g Fossils as environmental indicators

### What you need to know

- Reef-building corals (marine, shallow, warm); trilobites and ammonites (marine); plants (terrestrial, indicating past climate); trace fossils (tracks indicating terrestrial, burrows indicating shallow water).
- Identify trilobites, ammonites, corals, plants and trace fossils from their morphology.

### Explained

| Fossil | Morphology | Environment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reef-building coral | Colonies of many small cups (corallites) with radiating partitions (septa), or solitary horn shapes | Shallow (light needed for algae living with them), warm, clear, marine water |
| Trilobite | Extinct arthropod; body in three lobes lengthwise (axial lobe and two side lobes) and three parts from head to tail (head shield / cephalon, segmented thorax, tail / pygidium); compound eyes | Marine (Palaeozoic) |
| Ammonite | Extinct cephalopod; coiled, chambered shell with complex suture lines where internal walls meet the shell (2.3) | Marine, swimming (Mesozoic) |
| Plants | Leaves, stems, bark impressions, often carbonised | Land (terrestrial); leaf types indicate the climate (large ferns of coal swamps suggest warm, humid tropics) |
| Trace fossils | Evidence of activity rather than the body: footprints, tracks, burrows | Footprints of land animals: terrestrial (or very shallow water); burrows: usually shallow water sea floor |

The fossils present are clues; the best interpretations combine fossils with rock type and structures.

### Common mistakes

- Saying ammonites lived on land.
- Treating any fossil as proof of deep water: corals indicate shallow water.

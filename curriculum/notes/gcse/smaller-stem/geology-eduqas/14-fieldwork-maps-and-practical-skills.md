# Fieldwork, geological maps and practical skills

Every Eduqas GCSE Geology student must spend at least two days in the field, including at least one directed investigation to answer a geological problem. Both exams test practical and field skills (at least 15% of the marks) and maths (at least 10%), and Component 2 is built entirely around a simplified geological map. This file covers the fieldwork requirement, locating and measuring in the field, recording observations, geological maps and cross-sections, geological histories, laboratory tests and data handling, and the main maths skills.

## Fieldwork and directed investigations

### What you need to know

- At least two days of fieldwork, with at least one directed investigation; follow the Geological Code of Conduct and safety rules.
- Example investigations: determining the environments of deposition of a sedimentary sequence; determining the effects of tectonic stress at a location; determining the geological history of an area from field observations.

### Explained

A directed investigation follows a scientific method: a geological question (for example "In what environment did these beds form?"); a plan of what to observe and measure and where; collection of data (logs, measurements, sketches, photographs); analysis; and a conclusion supported by evidence, with an evaluation of its reliability (how many exposures, how representative, sources of error).

Safety and conduct (see also 1.5a): hard hats near faces, goggles for hammering, appropriate clothing and footwear, awareness of tides and weather, group working and communication, landowner permission, minimal hammering and collecting, leaving gates and sites as found.

### Common mistakes

- Drawing conclusions from one exposure without considering whether it is representative.

## Locating and measuring in the field

### What you need to know

- Locate geological features on a base map using navigation, basic survey skills and GPS; give six-figure grid references.
- Identify folds, faults and sedimentary structures in the field.
- Measure dip and strike (dip angle, dip direction, strike direction) of bedding planes or faults with a compass clinometer.
- Use sampling along a transect for data collection, for example to build a graphic log.

### Explained

Six-figure grid references: on an Ordnance Survey map, read the easting first (along the bottom: the vertical grid line to the left of the point, plus tenths across the square), then the northing (up the side: the horizontal line below the point, plus tenths up). For example 345 672 is 5 tenths east of line 34 and 2 tenths north of line 67, locating a point to within 100 m. GPS receivers give grid references directly but may be inaccurate under cliffs or in valleys.

Dip and strike measurement is described in 1.5a. Take several readings on each surface and average them, because bedding surfaces are uneven.

Sampling along a transect: measure the thickness of each bed in a vertical sequence (using a tape and, for tilted beds, correcting for dip, or measuring perpendicular to bedding), and record grain size, structures and fossils at set intervals or in each bed, to construct a graphic log (1.3e).

### Common mistakes

- Giving the northing before the easting.

## Recording observations

### What you need to know

- Record observations of rocks, minerals, fossils and structures in the field, in hand specimens, in photomicrographs and from images (including images from Mars).
- Produce full rock descriptions of macro and micro features from hand specimens and unfamiliar field exposures.
- Produce annotated field sketches and annotated drawings of fossils and igneous rocks.
- Apply classification systems and keys.

### Explained

A full rock description, in a logical order:

1. Colour (fresh and weathered surfaces).
2. Texture: crystalline (interlocking) or fragmental (grains and cement); grain or crystal size (use a grain size scale or comparator; coarse, medium, fine); shape (angular to rounded) and sorting; alignment (foliation) or random orientation.
3. Mineral composition (identified with a hand lens and simple tests: hardness, acid, cleavage, lustre).
4. Structures (bedding, cross bedding, graded bedding, vesicles, foliation) and fossils.
5. Reaction with dilute hydrochloric acid.
6. Name and rock group, with reasons.

Annotated field sketch: a simple line drawing (not artistic) of the exposure showing beds, folds, faults, intrusions and unconformities; include a title, location (grid reference), orientation (compass direction faced), scale (approximate height or a hammer for scale), and labels with notes (annotations) explaining features and interpretations. Annotated drawings of fossils or rocks similarly include scale and labelled diagnostic features.

Photomicrographs are photos of thin slices of rock seen through a microscope; they show crystal shapes, sizes, textures and grain relationships, for example interlocking crystals in granite, rounded grains with cement in sandstone, aligned mica in schist.

### Common mistakes

- Forgetting scale and orientation on a sketch.
- Describing colour only, without texture and minerals.

## Geological maps, cross-sections and histories

### What you need to know

- Analyse simplified geological maps and cross-sections (Component 2 is based on one).
- Complete a partly constructed geological map from a written description.
- Construct a geological history of a small area in the field and of an area shown on a map.

### Explained

A geological map shows the rock outcrops at the surface (where each rock unit would be seen if soil were removed), coloured or patterned by rock type and age, with a key (usually oldest at the bottom), plus symbols: dip and strike, fault lines, fold axes, and boundaries (geological contacts). It is drawn on a topographic base with contours.

Reading the map:

- Horizontal beds: boundaries follow contour lines.
- Vertical beds and dykes: straight outcrops, unaffected by topography.
- Dipping beds: boundaries cross contours; in a valley, the outcrop "V" points in the direction of dip (unless the beds dip less steeply than the valley floor).
- Width of outcrop depends on bed thickness, dip and slope: thicker beds and gentler dips give wider outcrops.
- Folds: outcrops repeated symmetrically; oldest in the centre of an eroded anticline, youngest in a syncline (1.5b).
- Faults: outcrops end abruptly or are offset across a line (1.5c).
- Unconformity: younger beds cut across older boundaries and structures (1.5d).
- Intrusions: dykes as narrow bands crossing other outcrops; plutons as large outcrops surrounded by metamorphic aureoles (1.2e, 1.4c).

Cross-sections show the vertical slice along a line on the map: draw the topographic profile from the contours, mark where boundaries cross the line, and draw beds going down at the dip shown (on a simplified section, using the dip symbols).

Geological history: list events from oldest to youngest using the principles in 2.3: deposition of each unit, folding or tilting, intrusions and metamorphism, faulting, uplift and erosion, deposition above unconformities, and present-day erosion. Interpret each sedimentary unit's environment from its rock type and fossils (1.3).

### Worked example

A map key lists: shale (oldest), sandstone, limestone, conglomerate (youngest), and a dolerite dyke. The shale, sandstone and limestone dip 40° west; the conglomerate is horizontal and lies across the eroded edges of the other beds; the dyke cuts the shale, sandstone and limestone but not the conglomerate; a fault offsets the dyke.

History: shale deposited (deep, calm water), then sandstone (shallower, higher energy), then limestone (warm, shallow sea) → tilting 40° west → dyke intruded → faulting (fault offsets the dyke, so younger) → uplift and erosion (unconformity) → conglomerate deposited (high-energy, perhaps a beach or river as the sea returned). The fault must be older than the conglomerate if it does not cut it.

### Common mistakes

- Putting the dyke before the tilting without evidence (the dyke here could cut tilted or untilted beds; check whether it is itself tilted).
- Forgetting the erosion stage at an unconformity.

## Laboratory tests, measurements and data

### What you need to know

- Use physical and chemical tests to identify minerals: density (hefting) and Mohs' hardness (fingernail, copper coin, steel pin) (1.1).
- Use apparatus to measure mass, time, volume, temperature and length (for example the jelly lava flow experiment, 1.2d; porosity by immersion, 1.3c), with attention to accuracy (repeat timings, set square, plumb line).
- Compile and analyse geological data sets, including with a geographic information system (GIS), for example assessing a site for civil engineering using British Geological Survey and Environment Agency data.
- Use ICT (computer modelling, data loggers, software) to collect and process data.

### Explained

Accuracy and reliability: repeat measurements and take a mean; use a set square or plumb line to measure angles and vertical heights accurately; use a data logger or video to time fast events; identify anomalous results; control variables.

GIS (geographic information system) software combines layers of mapped data (geology, faults, boreholes, groundwater, flood risk, landslide records, topography) so a site can be assessed for a project such as a landfill, reservoir or tunnel (4.2g, 4.2h).

## Maths skills (Appendix A)

### What you need to know

- Arithmetic: decimals, standard form, ratios, fractions and percentages, squares and square roots.
- Handling data: significant figures, means, frequency tables, bar charts and histograms, scatter graphs; sampling.
- Algebra and graphs: substitute into and rearrange simple equations; plot and interpret graphs, including gradients.
- Geometry: angles, scale, area and volume, 3D interpretation of maps and sections.

### Explained

Examples of geological calculations: porosity percentage (1.3c); spreading rate = distance ÷ time (2.2); half-life ages (2.3d); ratio of ore to gangue; percentage of calcium carbonate in a sand (mass lost after acid treatment ÷ original mass × 100); map scale (1 : 25 000 means 4 cm = 1 km); mean clast size; vertical exaggeration on cross-sections; throw on a fault; areas of outcrops or craters on photographs.

### Worked example

1. A 20 g sand sample loses 3 g after dissolving its shell fragments in acid. Percentage calcium carbonate = 3 ÷ 20 × 100 = 15%.
2. On a 1 : 50 000 map, two points are 7.4 cm apart. Real distance = 7.4 × 50 000 = 370 000 cm = 3.7 km.
3. An ore sample contains 45 g of galena and 180 g of quartz and calcite. Ore : gangue = 45 : 180 = 1 : 4.
4. Five clasts measure 32, 45, 28, 51 and 39 mm. Mean = 195 ÷ 5 = 39 mm.

### Common mistakes

- Mis-converting map scales (cm to km: divide by 100 000).
- Giving too many significant figures.

## Check questions

1. Give the six-figure grid reference for a point 3 tenths east of easting line 52 and 8 tenths north of northing line 19. [1 mark]
2. List four things that should be included on an annotated field sketch. [4 marks]
3. Write a full rock description of a medium-grained, well-sorted, red rock with rounded quartz grains and large-scale cross bedding that does not react with acid. Name the rock and suggest its environment. [5 marks]
4. On a geological map, a rock boundary follows the contour lines exactly. What does this show? [1 mark]
5. Explain how a fold can be recognised on a geological map. [3 marks]
6. On a 1 : 25 000 map, a dyke outcrop is 2.2 cm long. Calculate its length in km. [2 marks]
7. Describe how the reliability of the jelly lava flow investigation could be improved. [2 marks]
8. Explain how a GIS can be used to assess a potential reservoir site. [2 marks]
9. A cross-section shows: limestone, overlain by shale, both folded into a syncline; a granite intrusion with an aureole cutting both; an erosion surface; horizontal sandstone above. Write the geological history. [5 marks]

## Mark scheme

1. 523 198 (1).
2. Any four: title (1); location / grid reference (1); direction faced / orientation (1); scale (1); labels / annotations of features and interpretations (1).
3. Colour red (1); texture: medium-grained, well sorted, rounded grains, fragmental (1); mineral: quartz; no reaction with acid (1); structure: large-scale cross bedding (1); desert / dune sandstone, formed by wind in a hot desert (1).
4. The beds are horizontal (1).
5. Outcrops repeated symmetrically on either side of an axis (1); dip directions on either side point away from (anticline) or towards (syncline) the axis (1); oldest beds in the core = anticline, youngest = syncline (1).
6. 2.2 × 25 000 = 55 000 cm (1) = 0.55 km (1).
7. Repeat each test several times and calculate a mean (1); control other variables, e.g. same volume / same surface / time each flow precisely (1).
8. Combines layers of data (geology, faults, permeability, boreholes, landslides) (1) to identify a site with impermeable, stable rock and no faults (1).
9. Deposition of limestone, then shale (1); folding into a syncline (1); intrusion of granite with contact metamorphism forming the aureole (1); uplift and erosion forming an unconformity (1); deposition of horizontal sandstone (1).

# 1.2 Igneous rocks and processes

Igneous rocks form when molten rock (magma underground, lava at the surface) cools and crystallises. This topic covers how to describe and classify them, why crystal size depends on cooling rate, how magma viscosity controls volcanic eruptions and volcano shape (with the jelly lava flow investigation), and the different igneous bodies and their structures.

## 1.2a and 1.2b Describing and classifying igneous rocks

### What you need to know

- Igneous rocks have diagnostic properties: colour and texture (crystal size, equicrystalline or porphyritic, crystal orientation).
- Peridotite, basalt, andesite and granite can be classified by texture and mineralogy.
- Identify the named rocks in hand specimen, exposures, diagrams and photomicrographs from colour, crystal size (coarse > 3 mm, fine < 1 mm), random orientation, phenocrysts and groundmass, and mineralogy.

### Explained

Texture terms:

- Crystalline and interlocking: crystals grew into each other as the melt solidified, leaving no gaps. Crystals are randomly oriented (pointing in all directions), unlike the aligned crystals in many metamorphic rocks.
- Crystal size: coarse-grained (crystals over 3 mm, easily seen); medium (1 to 3 mm); fine-grained (under 1 mm, need a hand lens or microscope).
- Equicrystalline (equigranular): crystals are all about the same size.
- Porphyritic: large crystals (phenocrysts) set in a much finer groundmass. This shows two stages of cooling: slow cooling deep down grew the large crystals, then the magma rose and the rest cooled quickly.
- Glassy: no crystals, from very fast cooling (obsidian; not a named rock in this spec).

Mineralogy and colour: rocks rich in silica and pale minerals (quartz, feldspar) are light-coloured (felsic, or acid); rocks rich in iron and magnesium minerals (olivine, augite) are dark (mafic, or basic; ultramafic for peridotite).

| Rock | Crystal size | Colour | Main minerals | Silica content |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Granite | Coarse | Pale: pink, white, grey with black specks | Quartz, feldspar, mica | High (acid / felsic) |
| Andesite | Fine, often porphyritic (feldspar or augite phenocrysts) | Medium grey | Feldspar, augite (some mica) | Intermediate |
| Basalt | Fine | Dark grey to black | Feldspar, augite, sometimes olivine | Low (basic / mafic) |
| Peridotite | Coarse | Dark green to black | Olivine, augite | Very low (ultrabasic / ultramafic) |

So the classification uses two axes: crystal size (coarse = intrusive, cooled slowly underground; fine = extrusive or small intrusions, cooled quickly) and composition (pale silica-rich to dark iron-magnesium rich).

### Common mistakes

- Calling any dark rock basalt: check crystal size (peridotite is dark but coarse).
- Describing granite crystals as aligned.

## 1.2c Crystal size and cooling rate

### What you need to know

- Crystal size in igneous rocks is related to the cooling rate of the magma.

### Explained

As magma cools, crystals start to grow from many small centres. If it cools slowly (deep underground, insulated by surrounding rock, over thousands to millions of years), there is time for atoms to move to a few growing crystals, so they become large: a coarse-grained rock such as granite. If it cools quickly (lava at the surface, in air or water, in days to years), many crystals start but have little time to grow: a fine-grained rock such as basalt. Extremely rapid cooling forms glass.

Within one body, the edges cool fastest (against cold country rock), so they are finer-grained (a chilled margin) than the centre.

### Common mistakes

- Linking crystal size to depth without explaining cooling rate.

## 1.2d Magma viscosity, eruptions and volcano shape

### What you need to know

- Magma viscosity affects the type of volcanic activity and the shape of volcanoes: relatively passive fissure eruptions and violent central vent eruptions.
- Recognise volcano shapes from diagrams and photographs.
- Investigate factors affecting the length of lava flows using a jelly lava flow simulation, including viscosity (related to temperature) and slope angle.

### Explained

Viscosity is resistance to flow ("thickness"). Silica-rich magma is more viscous because silica forms linked chains; hotter magma is less viscous.

- Basaltic magma (low silica, about 1100 to 1200 °C): low viscosity, runny. Gas escapes easily, so eruptions are relatively gentle (effusive), with lava fountains and long lava flows. It often erupts from long cracks (fissure eruptions), forming wide lava plateaus, or builds broad, gently sloping shield volcanoes (Hawaii, Iceland).
- Andesitic and rhyolitic magma (higher silica, cooler, about 800 to 1000 °C): high viscosity, sticky. Gas is trapped until pressure builds up, giving violent explosive eruptions from a central vent, with ash, pumice and pyroclastic flows. Lava does not travel far, so steep-sided cone-shaped composite (strato-) volcanoes form from layers of ash and lava (for example Mount St Helens, volcanoes of the Andes).

Jelly lava flow investigation: pour a fixed volume of jelly (or syrup) down a sloping board and measure the length of the flow after a fixed time (or the time to travel a fixed distance).

- Independent variable: temperature of the jelly (affecting viscosity) or slope angle (measured with a protractor or set square).
- Dependent variable: flow length (ruler) or time (stopwatch).
- Control variables: volume of jelly, surface, pouring height, time allowed.
- Repeat and average results; plot a graph of flow length against temperature or angle.
- Expected results: warmer (less viscous) jelly and steeper slopes give longer flows. This models why runny basaltic lava travels far and builds gentle slopes, and viscous lava forms short, thick flows near the vent.

### Worked example

Flow lengths (cm) at 30° slope: jelly at 40 °C: 32, 35, 33; at 20 °C: 14, 12, 13. Means: 33.3 cm and 13.0 cm. The warmer, less viscous jelly flowed about 2.6 times as far.

### Common mistakes

- Saying viscous magma produces long lava flows.
- Saying the jelly experiment's temperature affects slope rather than viscosity.

## 1.2e Igneous bodies, structures and field relationships

### What you need to know

- Igneous bodies are distinguished by structure (columnar jointing, pillow lava), form (lava flows, sills, dykes, plutons) and field relationships.
- Identify them in specimens, exposures, diagrams and photographs; analyse simplified geological maps and cross-sections to interpret their modes of formation.

### Explained

Forms:

| Body | Description | Crystal size |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lava flow | Extrusive: erupted at the surface and flowed over the land or sea floor; its top is often rubbly or full of gas bubbles (vesicles), and weathered or eroded before the next layer covered it | Fine |
| Sill | Intrusive: a sheet of magma injected along (parallel to) the bedding of the surrounding rocks; concordant | Medium to fine (thicker sills coarser in the centre) |
| Dyke | Intrusive: a vertical or steep sheet cutting across the bedding; discordant; often in swarms | Medium to fine |
| Pluton (batholith) | Large intrusive body, kilometres across, cooled deep down; usually granite | Coarse |

Structures:

- Columnar jointing: as a thick lava flow or sill cools, it contracts and cracks into regular columns, usually six-sided, at right angles to the cooling surfaces (Giant's Causeway, Fingal's Cave).
- Pillow lava: lava erupted under water forms rounded, pillow-shaped blobs with a glassy chilled skin, stacked on each other. Shows eruption under water, typically at mid-ocean ridges.

Field relationships distinguish a sill from a buried lava flow, which can look similar on a map:

| Feature | Sill (intrusive) | Lava flow (extrusive) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Baking (contact metamorphism) | Rocks above and below are baked | Only the rocks below are baked |
| Chilled margins | Top and bottom are finer-grained | Only the base is chilled; top cooled in air |
| Top surface | Smooth contact | Vesicular, weathered or rubbly top |
| Fragments | Pieces of the rocks above may be included (xenoliths) | Pieces of the lava may be eroded into the beds above |
| Cross-cutting | May step across from one layer to another (transgressive) | Follows the land surface |

Plutons are surrounded by a metamorphic aureole (1.4) and their contacts cut across the surrounding rocks. On a map, a dyke appears as a narrow straight band cutting across other outcrops; a pluton as a large rounded outcrop; a sill as a band following the bedding.

### Common mistakes

- Calling a sill a dyke (or the reverse): a sill follows bedding; a dyke cuts across it.
- Saying a lava flow bakes rocks above it. It bakes only the ground it flowed over.

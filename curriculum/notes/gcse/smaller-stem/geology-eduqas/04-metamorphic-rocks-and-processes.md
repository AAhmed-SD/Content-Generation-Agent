# 1.4 Metamorphic rocks and processes

Metamorphic rocks are existing rocks changed by heat and/or pressure without melting. This topic covers what metamorphism does, the textures and minerals of slate, schist, marble and metaquartzite, and how contact and regional metamorphism differ, including metamorphic aureoles.

## 1.4a Metamorphism and recrystallisation

### What you need to know

- Metamorphic rocks result from increased temperature and/or pressure acting on pre-existing rocks, causing recrystallisation to form new minerals and textures.
- Identify the named rocks in hand specimen from crystal size (coarse, fine), crystal orientation (aligned, random) and reaction with dilute hydrochloric acid.

### Explained

When rocks are buried deeply, squeezed in mountain building, or heated by nearby magma, their minerals are no longer stable. In the solid state, atoms move and rearrange:

- Existing minerals grow into larger, interlocking crystals (recrystallisation), for example calcite in limestone becomes coarser in marble.
- New minerals grow that are stable at higher temperature and pressure, for example clay minerals in mudstone change to mica, then garnet.
- Under directed pressure, platy minerals (mica, clay) grow lined up at right angles to the pressure, giving an aligned texture.
- Fossils, sedimentary structures and original grains are usually destroyed.

The rock does not melt; if it did, it would form igneous rock. The original rock is called the parent rock (protolith). All metamorphic rocks are crystalline, with interlocking crystals.

### Common mistakes

- Saying metamorphic rocks form by melting.
- Saying metamorphism happens at the surface. It needs heat or pressure beyond surface conditions.

## 1.4b and 1.4c Metamorphic textures and mineralogy

### What you need to know

- Slate, schist, marble and metaquartzite have diagnostic textures (crystal size and orientation): non-foliated texture and foliated texture (slaty cleavage and schistosity).
- Schist, marble and metaquartzite have diagnostic mineralogy.
- Identify the features of a metamorphic aureole on diagrams, maps and cross-sections; use characteristics to interpret contact and regional metamorphism.

### Explained

Foliated textures (minerals aligned in parallel planes, from directed pressure):

- Slaty cleavage: very fine-grained clay and mica crystals aligned so the rock splits into thin, flat, hard sheets, at an angle to the original bedding (often seen as faint bedding stripes crossing the cleavage). Forms at low temperature and pressure.
- Schistosity: coarser, visible, aligned flakes of mica give a shiny, wavy surface; the rock splits along the mica layers. Forms at higher temperature and pressure.

Non-foliated texture: crystals are interlocking and randomly oriented, with no alignment. Typical of rocks made of mainly one mineral that does not form flat crystals (calcite, quartz), or of rocks heated without directed pressure.

| Rock | Parent rock | Texture | Crystal size | Minerals and tests |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Slate | Shale / mudstone | Foliated: slaty cleavage | Very fine (crystals invisible) | Clay minerals and fine mica; dark grey; rings when tapped; no fizz |
| Schist | Shale / mudstone (at higher grade) | Foliated: schistosity | Medium to coarse | Mica (shiny), quartz, often garnet crystals (porphyroblasts); no fizz |
| Marble | Limestone | Non-foliated, interlocking crystals | Medium to coarse, sugary | Calcite; fizzes in dilute HCl; scratched by steel; often white, fossils destroyed |
| Metaquartzite | Sandstone (quartz-rich) | Non-foliated, interlocking crystals | Medium | Quartz; very hard (scratches steel); no fizz; breaks through grains, not round them |

Metaquartzite and sandstone: both mostly quartz, but in sandstone the rock breaks around the grains (gritty surface), while in metaquartzite the grains are fused, so fractures pass straight through them (smooth, glassy surface).

Contact metamorphism:

- Cause: heat from an igneous intrusion (pluton, dyke or sill) affecting surrounding rocks. Pressure is low.
- Extent: a zone around the intrusion called a metamorphic aureole, from centimetres (dykes) to kilometres (large plutons) wide. The degree of change is greatest next to the contact and decreases outwards.
- Textures: non-foliated, because there is no directed pressure. Shale becomes hard, splintery "baked" rock (hornfels) with spotted texture further out; limestone becomes marble; sandstone becomes metaquartzite.
- On a map: a ring or band (often shown by a special ornament) surrounding the outcrop of a pluton, wider where the contact dips gently and narrower where it is steep.

Regional metamorphism:

- Cause: high pressure (including directed pressure) and high temperature over very large areas, during mountain building at convergent plate boundaries (2.2).
- Extent: hundreds to thousands of square kilometres.
- Textures: foliated. With increasing grade (temperature and pressure), mudstone changes progressively: slate → (phyllite) → schist → (gneiss). Limestone and sandstone become marble and metaquartzite but may show some alignment.

### Common mistakes

- Saying marble is foliated.
- Confusing slaty cleavage (a metamorphic structure) with mineral cleavage (a property of a crystal) or with bedding.
- Saying contact metamorphism produces schist.

4. Explain why slate splits into thin flat sheets. [2 marks]
5. Explain how metaquartzite can be distinguished from sandstone in hand specimen. [2 marks]

7. Compare contact and regional metamorphism in terms of cause, extent and texture. [6 marks]

## Mark scheme

1. Changes to existing rocks caused by increased temperature and/or pressure (1), in the solid state without melting, forming new minerals / textures (1).
2. Marble (1); fizzes in dilute hydrochloric acid / scratched by steel / interlocking calcite crystals (1).
3. Slaty cleavage: very fine-grained aligned minerals (crystals not visible) (1); schistosity: coarser visible aligned mica flakes, shiny wavy surface (1); schistosity forms at higher temperature / pressure (1).
4. Directed pressure aligned fine platy minerals (mica / clay) (1) at right angles to the pressure, so the rock splits along these planes (1).
5. Metaquartzite: fractures pass through the grains, giving a smooth / glassy surface; grains interlocking (1). Sandstone: breaks around grains, gritty surface; may have cement / pores (1).
6. Schist (1); shale / mudstone (1).
7. Cause: contact = heat from an intrusion (1); regional = heat and pressure during mountain building / plate collision (1). Extent: contact = small aureole around an intrusion (1); regional = very large areas (1). Texture: contact = non-foliated (1); regional = foliated (1).
8. The contact dips more gently on the wider side (1), so the heated zone outcrops over a wider area at the surface / the aureole is cut obliquely (1). Accept: larger part of the pluton under that side.

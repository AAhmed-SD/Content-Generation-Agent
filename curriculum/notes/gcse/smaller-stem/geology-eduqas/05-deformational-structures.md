# 1.5 Deformational structures

Sedimentary rocks are deposited in nearly flat layers. When they are found tilted, bent, broken or eroded, the rocks record tectonic forces. This topic covers measuring dip and strike, folds, faults and unconformities, and how to recognise them in the field, in photographs, and on maps and cross-sections.

## 1.5a Evidence of tectonic activity; field safety; dip and strike

### What you need to know

- The rock record provides evidence of tectonic activity.
- Describe safety precautions for visiting field exposures.
- Measure strike and dip; analyse strike and dip measurements to describe and interpret rock structures in 3D.

### Explained

Tectonic activity is recorded by tilted beds, folds, faults, unconformities, cleavage and metamorphism (1.4).

Field safety (in line with the Geological Code of Conduct): wear a hard hat near cliffs and quarry faces, and stout boots; high-visibility clothing in quarries; goggles when hammering (and only hammer where permitted; collect from loose material where possible); check tide times on coasts and do not get cut off; keep away from unstable cliff edges and the base of overhangs; check weather; work in groups and tell someone your plans; carry a first-aid kit and phone; get landowner permission; follow the Countryside Code and do not damage sites.

Dip and strike describe the orientation of a planar surface (a bedding plane, fault or cleavage):

- Dip: the angle of greatest slope, measured downwards from the horizontal (0° flat to 90° vertical), together with the direction in which the surface slopes down (dip direction, a compass bearing).
- Strike: the compass direction of a horizontal line on the surface. It is at right angles to the dip direction.
- Measured with a compass clinometer: the clinometer (a pendulum or bubble against a protractor) measures the dip angle; the compass measures the strike or dip direction. Written as, for example, 25°/140° (dip 25° towards bearing 140°, south-east).
- Map symbol: a T-shaped (or arrow) symbol; the long line shows strike, a short tick or arrow points in the dip direction, with the dip angle written beside it.

Analysing dip data: beds all dipping the same way show a tilted sequence; dips facing away from each other on either side of a line show an anticline (antiform); dips facing towards each other show a syncline (synform); a sudden change in dip or strike across a line may show a fault or unconformity.

### Worked example

At outcrop A the bedding dips 30° towards 090° (east); at outcrop B, 200 m east, it dips 30° towards 270° (west). The beds dip towards each other, so there is a synform between A and B, with its axis running north-south.

### Common mistakes

- Measuring the dip in the direction of strike, which gives 0°.
- Giving dip without the direction.

## 1.5b Folding

### What you need to know

- Folding is caused by compressional tectonic stress.
- Interpret horizontal beds, dipping beds and folded beds (antiform, synform, axial plane trace, limb) in exposures, diagrams, photographs, maps and cross-sections.

### Explained

When rocks are squeezed (compression), especially when warm and deep or under slow, steady stress, they bend (ductile deformation) rather than break, forming folds.

- Antiform: an arch-shaped fold, with limbs dipping away from the centre (hinge). If the oldest rocks are in the core it is an anticline.
- Synform: a trough-shaped fold, with limbs dipping towards the centre. If the youngest rocks are in the core it is a syncline.
- Limbs: the two sides of the fold.
- Hinge: the line of greatest curvature.
- Axial plane: the imaginary surface that divides the fold into two halves as symmetrically as possible. The axial plane trace is the line where it meets the ground surface (shown on maps by a line with outward arrows for an antiform, inward for a synform).
- Fold types: symmetrical (limbs dip at equal angles, vertical axial plane), asymmetrical (one limb steeper), overturned (one limb tilted past vertical), recumbent (lying on its side). The steeper limb and inclined axial plane show the direction the compression came from.

On a geological map of eroded folds, beds outcrop as repeated bands symmetrical about the fold axis: in an eroded antiform the oldest beds are in the middle; in an eroded synform the youngest beds are in the middle.

Horizontal beds outcrop following the contours on a map; vertical beds outcrop in straight lines regardless of topography; dipping beds cross contours at an angle (they "V" in valleys).

### Common mistakes

- Swapping antiform (arch) and synform (trough).
- Saying folds are formed by tension. They form by compression.

## 1.5c Faulting

### What you need to know

- Faulting is caused by tectonic stress: compressional, tensional and shear.
- Interpret normal faults, reverse/thrust faults, strike-slip faults and fault displacement in exposures, diagrams, photographs, maps and cross-sections.

### Explained

A fault is a fracture along which the rocks on either side have moved relative to each other. Rocks fault (brittle deformation) when stress is applied quickly or near the surface where they are cold and brittle. Faults often show crushed rock (fault breccia), polished, scratched surfaces (slickensides) and dragged beds.

On a dipping fault, the block above the fault plane is the hanging wall; the block below is the footwall.

| Fault | Stress | Movement | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | Tension (pulling apart) | Hanging wall moves down relative to the footwall | Crust is extended (lengthened); typical at divergent boundaries and rift valleys |
| Reverse | Compression | Hanging wall moves up relative to the footwall; fault plane steep | Crust is shortened; beds repeated |
| Thrust | Compression | A low-angle reverse fault (dip under 45°); older rocks pushed over younger | Large shortening in mountain belts |
| Strike-slip (transform) | Shear (sideways) | Blocks slide horizontally past each other; fault plane near vertical | Offsets features sideways (for example the San Andreas Fault) |

Displacement (throw) is the amount of movement: vertical throw is the vertical offset of a bed across the fault; in a strike-slip fault the horizontal offset is measured. On maps, faults are drawn as heavy lines; outcrops of beds are offset across them; a downthrow side may be marked with a tick. A rift valley is a block dropped down between two normal faults (a graben); a raised block is a horst.

### Worked example

On a cross-section, the top of a sandstone bed is at 120 m below the surface on one side of a normal fault and 165 m below on the other. Throw = 165 − 120 = 45 m, downthrown on the hanging-wall side.

### Common mistakes

- Mixing up normal and reverse: in a normal fault the hanging wall goes down (extension).
- Calling a reverse fault with a gentle dip a normal fault because the hanging wall "looks lower".

## 1.5d Unconformities

### What you need to know

- Unconformities are gaps in the rock record. Angular unconformities form by a sequence: deformation, uplift, erosion, then later deposition.
- Identify them in the field, diagrams, photographs, maps and cross-sections; use them to interpret geological history.

### Explained

An unconformity is a surface of erosion or non-deposition separating older rocks below from younger rocks above. It represents a time gap, which may be millions of years, during which rocks were eroded or not deposited.

Angular unconformity: the older beds below are tilted or folded at a different angle from the younger beds above. Sequence of events:

1. Deposition of the lower sediments (horizontal).
2. Lithification.
3. Deformation: folding or tilting (and perhaps faulting, metamorphism, intrusion).
4. Uplift above sea level.
5. Erosion, producing an irregular surface that cuts across the tilted beds.
6. Subsidence (or sea level rise) and deposition of new horizontal sediments on the eroded surface.

Features: the lower beds are cut off at the unconformity; the base of the upper sequence often has a basal conglomerate containing pebbles of the older rocks; faults and dykes in the lower sequence may stop at the unconformity (showing they are older than the upper beds). Hutton's unconformity at Siccar Point, Scotland, convinced him of the vast length of geological time (2.3).

On a geological map, the younger beds cross the outcrops of several older beds at an angle; their base line "cuts across" older boundaries and faults.

### Common mistakes

- Missing out uplift or deformation in the sequence of events.
- Saying an unconformity is a fault. There is no movement along it; it is a buried erosion surface.

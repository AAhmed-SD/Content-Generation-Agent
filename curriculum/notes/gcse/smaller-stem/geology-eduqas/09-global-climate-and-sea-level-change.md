# 2.4 Global climate and sea level change

Rocks hold a record of the Earth's climate going back hundreds of millions of years. This topic covers evidence for icehouse and greenhouse periods, how Britain's climate changed as it drifted across latitudes, evidence for sea level change, sources of carbon dioxide, past carbon dioxide levels, feedback in the climate system, ice sheets and sea level, and carbon capture and storage.

## 2.4a Icehouse and greenhouse conditions

### What you need to know

- Evidence for global climate change through geological time, from icehouse to greenhouse conditions: glacial deposits near the equator (Carboniferous tillites), limestone outside the tropics (Cretaceous limestones and chalk).

### Explained

- Icehouse periods: large ice sheets exist at the poles (as now). Global temperatures are lower and sea level lower.
- Greenhouse periods: no permanent polar ice; warm climates extend to high latitudes; sea level is high.

Evidence: tillites (lithified glacial till: poorly sorted mixtures of clay to boulders, with scratched clasts) and scratched rock surfaces from the Late Carboniferous and Permian are found in South America, Africa, India and Australia, regions which were then close together near the South Pole in Gondwana. Some glacial deposits, such as those from the late Precambrian, are even found in rocks that formed near the equator, suggesting very cold global conditions. Thick Cretaceous chalk and limestone (which form in warm, shallow seas) occur well outside the tropics across northern Europe, including the white cliffs of southern England, showing warm seas reached high latitudes and sea level was very high: a greenhouse world.

Care is needed: plate movements change a region's latitude (2.4b), so evidence must be compared with where the continent was at the time.

### Common mistakes

- Assuming tillite near today's equator means the whole Earth froze, without considering plate movement.

## 2.4b Britain's changing latitude and climate

### What you need to know

- Evidence for changes in the climate of the British area caused by changes in its latitude.
- Interpret evidence from rocks, fossils, maps and diagrams for changes in latitude from the Lower Palaeozoic to the Cenozoic; use the data sheet.

### Explained

Plate movements carried the British area northwards from the southern hemisphere to its present position at about 50 to 60° N.

| Time | Approximate latitude | Rocks and fossils | Climate and environment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lower Palaeozoic (Cambrian to Silurian) | About 30 to 60° S (southern Britain on a separate microcontinent from Scotland, across the Iapetus Ocean) | Deep water shales with graptolites, turbidites, volcanic rocks | Temperate to cool oceans |
| Devonian | About 10 to 20° S | Old Red Sandstone: red river and desert sandstones, fish fossils | Hot, semi-arid continental interior |
| Carboniferous | Equator | Reef limestones with corals; coal seams from tropical swamp forests | Tropical, warm and humid |
| Permian and Triassic | About 10 to 30° N | New Red Sandstone: desert dune sandstones, breccias, evaporites (halite, gypsum) | Hot desert, like today's Sahara |
| Jurassic | About 30 to 40° N | Shallow marine limestones, shales with ammonites | Warm shallow seas |
| Cretaceous | About 40 to 45° N | Chalk | Warm, very high sea level |
| Cenozoic | About 50 to 55° N | Clays and sands; later glacial deposits (Quaternary) | Cooling; ice ages in the last 2.6 million years |

Rocks such as coal (tropical swamps), evaporites and desert sandstones (latitudes of hot deserts around 15 to 30°) and reef limestones (tropical seas) give evidence of past latitude. Palaeomagnetism also records latitude.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the British climate changed only because the global climate changed. Changing latitude was a major cause.

## 2.4c Sea level change

### What you need to know

- Evidence for changes in sea level, for example drowned forests.

### Explained

- Drowned (submerged) forests: tree stumps rooted in peat are exposed at low tide on some British beaches (for example in Cardigan Bay and Cornwall). Trees grew on dry land; they are now below high tide, showing sea level has risen (after the last ice age, as ice sheets melted) or the land has sunk.
- Raised beaches: old wave-cut platforms and beach deposits above present sea level (as in western Scotland) show relative sea level has fallen, because the land rose after the weight of the ice sheets was removed (isostatic rebound).
- Rock evidence: marine sediments overlying land sediments (a transgression) show a sea level rise; the reverse (a regression) a fall. Chalk deposited across much of Europe shows very high Cretaceous sea level.
- Drowned river valleys (rias) and submerged landscapes under the North Sea (Doggerland) are other examples.

### Common mistakes

- Saying a raised beach shows sea level rise.

## 2.4d, 2.4e and 2.4f Carbon dioxide, past levels and feedback

### What you need to know

- The major sources of atmospheric carbon dioxide are volcanic emissions and the burning of fossil fuels; evaluate their relative roles in current climate change.
- Evidence for past changes in atmospheric carbon dioxide from ice cores and sedimentary rocks.
- Positive feedback (reduction of ice-cap albedo accelerating warming) and negative feedback (carbon dioxide dissolving in sea water and being taken up by organisms to form limestone) on atmospheric carbon dioxide, with roles of subduction, volcanic emissions, chemical weathering and marine storage.

### Explained

Sources: volcanoes release carbon dioxide from the Earth's interior, but at present the total is small: on average roughly 0.3 to 0.4 billion tonnes per year. Burning fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) releases about 35 billion tonnes per year, around a hundred times more. So human activity is by far the main cause of the current rapid rise in atmospheric carbon dioxide (from about 280 ppm before industrialisation to over 420 ppm). In the distant past, huge volcanic outpourings (flood basalts) released enough carbon dioxide to cause major warming.

Evidence of past carbon dioxide:

- Ice cores: snow falling on ice sheets traps tiny bubbles of air as it compacts into ice. Cores drilled from Antarctica and Greenland contain air going back about 800 000 years. The carbon dioxide in the bubbles is measured directly; it rises and falls with temperature through the ice ages (about 180 ppm in glacial periods, 280 ppm in warm interglacials).
- Sedimentary rocks: evidence over millions of years comes from rock types (widespread limestone, coal, evaporites), the chemistry of carbonates, fossil plant leaves (the density of breathing pores, stomata, falls when carbon dioxide is high), and fossil soils.

Feedback:

- Positive feedback amplifies change. Warming melts ice and snow, which are white and reflect much sunlight (high albedo). The exposed darker ocean or land absorbs more sunlight, causing more warming and more melting.
- Negative feedback reduces change. Carbon dioxide dissolves in sea water (more dissolves as levels rise); marine organisms use dissolved carbon to build calcium carbonate shells and skeletons, which become limestone, storing carbon for millions of years. Chemical weathering of rocks (faster in warm, wet climates) uses up atmospheric carbon dioxide and its products are carried to the sea and stored in carbonates. This slowly lowers carbon dioxide.

The long-term carbon cycle: carbon is stored in limestone and organic-rich sediments (marine storage); when plates subduct, some of this carbon is carried into the mantle and later returned to the atmosphere by volcanic emissions. This cycle, over millions of years, has kept the climate within limits suitable for life.

### Worked example

If fossil fuels release 35 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide per year and volcanoes 0.35 billion tonnes, the ratio is 35 ÷ 0.35 = 100: human emissions are about 100 times volcanic emissions.

### Common mistakes

- Saying volcanoes produce more carbon dioxide than human activity.
- Mixing up positive feedback (amplifies) with "good" feedback.

## 2.4g and 2.4h Ice sheets, sea level and carbon capture

### What you need to know

- Global warming and cooling affect the size of continental ice sheets and global sea level; investigate past and current changes using the internet, maps and aerial images.
- Carbon sequestration (capture and storage) is a geological strategy for reducing atmospheric carbon dioxide.

### Explained

Ice on land (Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets, glaciers) holds water taken from the oceans. When climate cools, ice sheets grow and sea level falls: at the peak of the last glaciation, about 20 000 years ago, ice covered much of Britain and sea level was about 120 m lower than today (Britain was joined to Europe). When climate warms, ice on land melts and sea level rises. Warming water also expands (thermal expansion). Melting floating sea ice does not raise sea level much, because it already displaces its own weight of water. Satellite images, aerial photographs and maps show glaciers retreating and ice sheets thinning; tide gauges and satellites measure present sea level rise (a few millimetres per year).

Carbon capture and storage (CCS): carbon dioxide is captured from power stations or industry (or directly from air), compressed and pumped deep underground into suitable rock:

- Porous, permeable reservoir rocks, such as depleted oil and gas fields or deep saline aquifers (sandstones), with an impermeable cap rock (shale, salt) above to stop it escaping, the same geology that trapped oil and gas (4.2).
- Basalts, where the carbon dioxide reacts with the rock to form solid carbonate minerals, locking it away permanently.

Challenges: cost, energy needed, risk of leaks, need for monitoring, and suitable sites.

### Common mistakes

- Saying melting Arctic sea ice is the main cause of sea level rise.

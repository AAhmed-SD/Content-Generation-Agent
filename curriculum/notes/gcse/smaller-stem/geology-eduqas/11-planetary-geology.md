# 3.1 Planetary geology

Comparing the Earth with the Moon, Mars and other bodies helps explain both. This topic covers similarities and differences between the Earth and its neighbours, what meteorites tell us about the Earth's interior, using Earth landforms to interpret landforms elsewhere (uniformitarianism), how other bodies reveal processes hidden on Earth, and the effect of impacts on Earth's history. For astronomy background, see [Astronomy 09 Exploring the Moon](../astronomy-edexcel/09-exploring-the-moon.md) and [Astronomy 11 Exploring the Solar System](../astronomy-edexcel/11-exploring-the-solar-system.md).

## 3.1a Earth and its planetary neighbours

### What you need to know

- Similarities and differences between the Earth and its planetary neighbours: rocks, landscapes, atmosphere, temperature, pressure and gravity.

### Explained

| | Earth | Moon | Mars | Venus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rocks | Igneous, sedimentary, metamorphic; granite continents, basalt ocean floor | Basalt (maria), anorthosite (highlands), impact breccias; no sedimentary rocks formed by water | Basalt crust; sedimentary rocks deposited by water (sandstones, mudstones, evaporites) | Mostly basalt |
| Landscape | Mountains, ocean basins, rivers, few craters (erased by erosion and plate tectonics) | Heavily cratered highlands, smooth maria | Giant volcanoes (Olympus Mons, about 22 km high), huge canyon (Valles Marineris), dry river channels and deltas, craters, polar ice caps, dunes | Volcanic plains, volcanoes, few craters (resurfaced fairly recently) |
| Atmosphere | Nitrogen and oxygen, moderate pressure (1 bar) | Virtually none | Thin carbon dioxide, about 1% of Earth's pressure | Thick carbon dioxide, about 90 times Earth's pressure, sulfuric acid clouds |
| Temperature | Mean about 15 °C; liquid water | −170 °C to +120 °C (no air to even out) | Mean about −60 °C | About 460 °C (greenhouse effect) |
| Gravity | 1 g | About 0.17 g | About 0.38 g | About 0.9 g |
| Plate tectonics | Active | None | None now (single-plate "stagnant lid") | No Earth-style plates |

The Earth is unique in the Solar System in having plate tectonics and liquid water oceans at the surface today, which constantly renew its surface. Smaller bodies (Moon, Mars) lost their internal heat faster, so their geological activity largely stopped, preserving ancient surfaces. Lower gravity on Mars allows taller volcanoes.

### Common mistakes

- Saying Mars has never had liquid water. Its surface shows clear evidence of past water.

## 3.1b Meteorites and the Earth's composition

### What you need to know

- Meteorites provide evidence for the composition of the Earth.

### Explained

We cannot sample the Earth's core or deep mantle directly. Most meteorites come from asteroids, which formed at the same time and from the same material as the Earth (about 4.6 billion years ago):

- Chondrites (stony meteorites from undifferentiated asteroids) have a composition thought to be like the average starting material of the Earth (apart from volatile elements). Comparing the Earth's crust with chondrites suggests heavy elements such as iron and nickel sank into the core.
- Iron meteorites (iron-nickel alloy) are thought to be fragments of the cores of asteroids that melted and separated into core and mantle and were later broken up by collisions. They are good models for the Earth's core.
- Stony-iron meteorites resemble the core-mantle boundary; achondrites (some stony meteorites) resemble mantle or crust.

This supports the idea that the Earth has an iron-nickel core and a silicate mantle. Meteorites also give the age of the Solar System, and so of the Earth: about 4.6 billion years (2.3).

### Common mistakes

- Saying meteorites come from the Earth's core.

## 3.1c and 3.1d Using landforms to interpret other worlds and the Earth

### What you need to know

- The relationship between landforms and processes on Earth is an analogue for interpreting landforms on other planetary bodies (uniformitarianism).
- Planetary landforms provide evidence for unseen Earth processes, for example impact craters on the Moon.
- Use space imagery and planetary exploration data (maps, diagrams, photographs) to interpret landforms and processes, for example on the Moon and Mars.

### Explained

Earth as a key to other planets: if a landform on Mars looks like a landform on Earth whose origin we understand, it probably formed by a similar process.

| Landform on Mars or the Moon | Earth analogue | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Branching channels and fan-shaped deltas on Mars | River valleys and deltas | Water once flowed across Mars |
| Layered sedimentary rocks with cross bedding (photographed by rovers such as Curiosity) | Sedimentary sequences | Deposition in rivers and lakes or by wind |
| Desiccation cracks and evaporite minerals on Mars | Drying lake beds | Lakes that evaporated |
| Olympus Mons, a broad gently sloping volcano | Hawaiian shield volcanoes | Low-viscosity basaltic lava, over a hotspot that stayed in one place (no plate movement) |
| Sand dunes on Mars | Desert dunes | Wind transport |
| Sinuous channels (rilles) on the Moon | Lava channels and collapsed lava tubes in Hawaii | Flowing lava |
| Lunar maria | Flood basalts on Earth | Large outpourings of runny basaltic lava |

Other bodies as a key to Earth: some processes that shaped the early Earth have left little trace here, because erosion, weathering, sedimentation and plate tectonics have destroyed or buried the evidence. The Moon's heavily cratered surface preserves a record of the intense bombardment of the inner Solar System about 4 billion years ago: the Earth must have been struck at least as often, but almost all those early craters on Earth have been erased. Only about 200 impact craters are recognised on Earth, mostly young. So the Moon shows how important impacts were in the Earth's early history.

### Common mistakes

- Using an analogue without explaining the matching process.

## 3.1e Impacts and the evolution of the Earth and its biosphere

### What you need to know

- Impacts from meteorites and comets may have had a significant effect on the evolution of the Earth and its biosphere.

### Explained

- Formation of the Moon: a giant impact of a Mars-sized body with the early Earth probably formed the Moon (see Astronomy 09), and the impact energy melted the Earth's outer layers.
- Early bombardment: frequent large impacts in the first few hundred million years heated and repeatedly disrupted the surface, which may have delayed the start of life or sterilised early habitats.
- Delivery of water and organic molecules: some of the Earth's water and carbon compounds may have come from impacting asteroids and comets.
- Mass extinctions: the Chicxulub impact at 66 Ma caused the K/Pg mass extinction (2.5), ending the age of dinosaurs and allowing mammals to diversify, so it changed the course of evolution.
- Future hazard: near-Earth objects are tracked, and missions such as DART (2022) have tested whether an asteroid's path could be changed.

### Common mistakes

- Saying impacts have had no effect since the early Solar System.

## Check questions

1. Give two differences between the surface of the Moon and the surface of the Earth, and explain one of them. [3 marks]
2. Explain why Mars has much larger volcanoes than the Earth. [2 marks]
3. Explain how iron meteorites provide evidence for the composition of the Earth's core. [3 marks]
4. A rover photographs layered rocks with cross bedding and rounded pebbles on Mars. Interpret the evidence using uniformitarianism. [3 marks]
5. Explain why the Moon provides better evidence than the Earth for the frequency of impacts in the early Solar System. [3 marks]
6. Describe two ways in which impacts may have affected the evolution of life on Earth. [4 marks]

## Mark scheme

1. Any two differences, e.g. Moon heavily cratered, Earth few craters (1); Moon has no atmosphere / water / sedimentary rocks (1); Moon no plate tectonics (1). Explanation, e.g. Earth's craters erased by erosion / plate tectonics while the Moon has no weathering / erosion (1).
2. Mars has lower gravity, so volcanoes can grow taller (1); no plate movement, so the volcano stays over the same hotspot and keeps growing (1).
3. Iron meteorites are fragments of the cores of broken asteroids (1) which formed from the same material as the Earth at the same time (1); so the Earth's core is likely to be mainly iron-nickel (1).
4. Cross bedding and rounded pebbles on Earth form in flowing water (rivers) (1); by uniformitarianism, similar processes formed them on Mars (1); so liquid water once flowed on Mars / Mars had a wetter climate (1).
5. The Moon has no atmosphere / water / weathering or erosion (1) and no plate tectonics (1), so its ancient craters are preserved, while Earth's have been destroyed (1).
6. Any two, each explained: mass extinction such as K/Pg (1)(1); mammals diversified after dinosaurs died out (1)(1); delivery of water / organic molecules (1)(1); early impacts may have sterilised / delayed life (1)(1).

# 3.2.3 to 3.2.7 Shaping, casting and moulding, joining, heat treatment and surface finishing

These processes change a material's shape without removing it, join parts together, change a metal's internal structure, or protect and improve its surface. For each you need to know which materials it suits and how it is done.

## 3.2.3 Shaping

### What you need to know

- Bending, folding, press forming, composite lay-up, punching and stamping.
- Calculations: angles, tolerances, and pressure and force (E10: pressure = force ÷ area); decimals, standard form, squares and square roots.

### Explained

- Bending: forming a bar, tube or rod round a curve. Metal bar is bent in a vice or on a bending jig; tube is bent with a former to stop it kinking. Acrylic and other thermoplastic sheet is heated along a line with a strip heater (line bending) and then bent over a former.
- Folding: making a sharp, straight bend in sheet metal, usually on a folding machine (box and pan folder) or press brake. The inside radius of a fold must not be too tight, or the outer surface cracks. Brittle metals cannot be folded.
- Press forming: a sheet of metal is pressed between a shaped punch (male) and die (female) to make a 3D form, such as a car door panel or a sink. Large forces stretch the sheet into shape. Thermoplastic sheet can be formed in a similar way when heated (and by vacuum forming).
- Composite lay-up: layers of fibre cloth or mat (glass or carbon) are laid into a mould that has been coated with release agent and often a gel coat. Each layer is wetted out with resin and rolled to remove air bubbles. The resin cures to make a rigid shell. Pre-preg sheets can be laid up and cured under heat and pressure in an oven or autoclave for higher strength.
- Punching: a punch forces through sheet into a die to cut out a hole; the piece removed is waste (the slug).
- Stamping (blanking): the same action, but the piece pushed out is the part you want (the blank), for example washers or electrical contacts. Stamping can also mean pressing a pattern or lettering into the surface.

Punching and stamping are very fast, so they suit mass production of sheet metal parts.

### Worked example

A press punches a 10 mm diameter hole. The ram area of the hydraulic cylinder that drives it is 0.005 m² and the oil pressure is 4.0 × 10⁶ Pa.

- Force = pressure × area = 4.0 × 10⁶ × 0.005 = 2.0 × 10⁴ N (20 kN).

A sheet must be folded to 90° ± 1°. A check finds 88.5°. This is outside the tolerance (89° to 91°), so it is rejected or re-worked.

### Common mistakes

- Confusing punching and stamping (blanking). In punching the hole is wanted; in blanking the cut-out piece is wanted.
- Forgetting release agent in composite lay-up; the part sticks to the mould.

## 3.2.4 Casting and moulding

### What you need to know

- Pressure die casting, sand casting and injection moulding: which materials each suits and how each is done.

### Explained

Sand casting (metals such as cast iron, aluminium, bronze):

1. A pattern (a copy of the part, slightly oversized to allow for shrinkage, with tapered sides called draft) is packed in special sand in a two-part box (cope and drag).
2. The pattern is removed, leaving a cavity. A runner (sprue) for pouring and a riser to let gases out and feed shrinkage are cut.
3. Molten metal is poured in and left to solidify.
4. The sand is broken away; the runner and riser are cut off and the part is fettled (cleaned) and machined where needed.

Sand casting suits large parts and small numbers (each mould is used only once), but gives a rough surface and lower accuracy.

Pressure die casting (non-ferrous metals with low melting points: aluminium, zinc, magnesium alloys):

1. Molten metal is forced under high pressure into a steel die (permanent mould) that has been cooled with water.
2. The metal solidifies quickly; the die opens and ejector pins push the part out.

It gives accurate parts with a smooth finish and thin walls, very quickly, so it suits mass production (for example, gearbox housings, toy cars). The steel dies are very expensive.

Injection moulding (thermoplastics, supplied as granules):

1. Granules are fed from a hopper into a heated barrel.
2. A rotating screw carries them forward; heaters and friction melt them.
3. The screw acts as a ram, forcing the molten plastic under pressure into a closed, cooled steel mould.
4. The plastic cools and sets; the mould opens and ejector pins push out the part. The cycle may take only seconds.

It suits very high volumes of complex, accurate parts (housings, bottle caps, gears). The mould is very expensive, so small numbers are uneconomic. Waste (sprues and runners) can be reground and reused.

### Common mistakes

- Saying sand casting moulds are reused. The sand mould is destroyed to remove the casting.
- Using injection moulding for thermosets in an exam answer about granules being remelted; thermosets cannot be remelted.
- Saying die casting is used for steel. The high melting point would destroy the dies.

## 3.2.5 Joining and assembly

### What you need to know

- Permanent and temporary (non-permanent) joining methods.
- Rivets, threaded fastenings, soft soldering, hard soldering (silver soldering), brazing and welding.

### Explained

A permanent joint cannot be taken apart without damaging the parts; a temporary joint can be undone and remade.

| Method | Type | How it works | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rivets | Permanent | A metal pin with a head is put through holes in both parts and the other end is hammered or squeezed to form a second head. Pop (blind) rivets are set from one side with a riveting tool | Aircraft skins, joining sheet metal, where heat would distort parts or parts are different metals |
| Threaded fastenings | Temporary | Nuts and bolts, machine screws, set screws and self-tapping screws clamp parts. Holes may be tapped (internal thread cut) to take a screw | Anything that must be dismantled for maintenance: engine parts, machinery guards |
| Soft soldering | Permanent (can be unsoldered) | A tin-based alloy melting at around 180 to 230 °C flows between the parts; flux cleans the surface | Electronic components on PCBs, copper plumbing |
| Hard soldering (silver soldering) | Permanent | A silver alloy melting at roughly 600 to 800 °C, heated with a brazing torch, with flux | Stronger joints in copper, brass, steel; jewellery; small fittings |
| Brazing | Permanent | A brass (copper-zinc) filler melting at around 900 °C joins steel parts; the parts themselves do not melt | Bicycle frames, joining tubes, repairing cast iron |
| Welding | Permanent | The parts themselves are melted at the joint, usually with filler, and fuse together as they cool. Methods include MIG, TIG, arc and oxy-acetylene; plastics can be welded with heat too | Steel structures, car bodies, pipelines, ships |

Soldering and brazing use a filler that melts at a lower temperature than the parts, so the parts do not melt; joint strength rises from soft solder (weakest) to hard solder to brazing. Welding is strongest because the joint becomes part of the material, but the heat can distort thin parts and alter the metal's properties near the weld.

### Common mistakes

- Saying soldering melts the parts. Only the solder melts; in welding the parts melt.
- Calling rivets temporary. They have to be drilled out to undo them.
- Forgetting flux: without it, oxides stop the solder wetting the surface.

## 3.2.6 Heat and chemical treatment

### What you need to know

- Normalising, annealing, hardening and quenching: what each does and how it is carried out.

### Explained

Heat treatments change a metal's grain structure and therefore its properties.

- Annealing: heat the metal to a set temperature (for steel, just above its upper critical temperature, glowing red; for copper, a dull red; for aluminium, until soap on the surface turns black), hold it there, then cool it very slowly, often in the switched-off furnace or buried in sand or ash. Copper and brass may simply be left to cool. Result: large grains, so the metal is soft, ductile and easy to work. Used to remove work hardening.
- Normalising (steel): heat to just above the upper critical temperature and cool in still air. Cooling is faster than annealing, so grains are finer and more even. Result: relieves internal stresses from forging, welding or cold working and gives a uniform structure that is tougher than annealed steel.
- Hardening: heat medium or high carbon steel to cherry red (above the upper critical temperature) so its structure changes.
- Quenching: then plunge it into cold water, brine or oil. Rapid cooling traps a very hard but brittle structure. Oil quenching is gentler and less likely to crack the part. Quenching is the cooling step of hardening.
- Tempering usually follows hardening: the hardened steel is cleaned bright and gently reheated to a lower temperature (judged by the oxide colours, from pale straw to blue) then quenched. This removes some brittleness and gives toughness. Cutting tools are tempered to pale straw (hard); springs to blue (tough).

Low carbon steel does not have enough carbon to be hardened by quenching. Its surface can be case hardened by adding carbon to the outer layer and then quenching.

| Treatment | Heat to | Cool by | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Annealing | Above critical temperature | Very slowly | Softest, most ductile |
| Normalising | Above critical temperature | In still air | Removes stresses, refines grain, tougher |
| Hardening and quenching | Above critical temperature | Very fast in water or oil | Very hard, brittle |
| Tempering | Lower temperature (about 230 to 300 °C) | Quench | Less brittle, tougher |

### Common mistakes

- Mixing up annealing (slow cooling, soft) and quenching (fast cooling, hard).
- Saying any steel can be hardened by quenching. It needs enough carbon.
- Missing out tempering when asked how to make a hard but not brittle chisel.

## 3.2.7 Surface finishing

### What you need to know

- Painting, dip coating, electroplating, galvanising and polishing: how each is done and why.

### Explained

Finishes protect a surface from corrosion and wear, and improve appearance. Preparation is essential: the surface must be clean, degreased and free of rust or the finish will not stick.

- Painting: a primer is applied to help adhesion and protect bare metal, then undercoat and top coats, by brush, roller or spray. Cheap and in any colour, but it chips, and rust can spread under damaged paint. Powder coating, where dry powder is sprayed electrostatically then baked to a tough skin, is an industrial version.
- Dip coating (plastic dip coating): a metal part is heated (to about 180 °C) and dipped into a fluidised bath of thermoplastic powder (air blown through the powder makes it behave like a liquid). The powder melts onto the hot part, giving a thick, soft-feel, coloured, insulating coating. Used on tool handles, dish racks and wire fencing.
- Electroplating: the part is the cathode (negative) in a solution of a metal salt, with an anode of the plating metal. A current deposits a thin layer of metal (chromium, nickel, zinc, silver or gold) onto the part. Gives corrosion resistance and a decorative or hard-wearing surface; for example chrome-plated taps or gold-plated connectors.
- Galvanising: steel is cleaned (pickled in acid) and dipped into molten zinc at about 450 °C. The zinc coats and bonds to the steel. Zinc protects twice: as a barrier, and because zinc is more reactive than iron, it corrodes in preference (sacrificial protection), so even a scratched area does not rust. Used on crash barriers, nails, buckets, fencing.
- Polishing: abrasives of finer and finer grades (emery cloth, then polishing compounds on a buffing wheel) remove scratches to give a smooth, shiny surface. It improves appearance and can reduce friction, but gives no protection to metals that corrode, so polished steel is often lacquered or oiled. Polishing suits metals that resist corrosion (stainless steel, brass, aluminium) and acrylic edges.

### Common mistakes

- Saying galvanising is a paint. It is a zinc coating applied by hot dipping.
- Saying polishing protects steel from rust. It does not on its own.
- Forgetting surface preparation in a "describe how to finish" answer.

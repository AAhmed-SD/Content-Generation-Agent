# 3.1.1 Engineering materials and their properties

Engineers choose a material by matching its properties to the job. This file covers the mechanical properties used to describe materials, then the four families in the spec: metals and alloys, polymers, composites, and other materials (timber and ceramics). For each you need to recognise the material from its appearance and properties, know how it behaves when it is handled and machined, and know how its properties can be changed.

## 3.1.1 Materials and their properties

### What you need to know

- Materials can be identified by their appearance and by their properties.
- The key mechanical properties: toughness, brittleness, ductility, malleability, hardness, strength and stiffness.
- How materials behave when they are handled and when they are machined (cut, drilled, turned, milled).

### Explained

| Property | Meaning | High in | Low in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strength | How much force a material can take before it breaks or permanently deforms. Tensile strength resists pulling; compressive strength resists squashing | Steel (tension), concrete (compression) | Lead, polystyrene |
| Stiffness | How well it resists bending or stretching under load (it springs back only a tiny amount) | Steel, carbon-fibre composite | Rubber, low-density polythene |
| Hardness | How well it resists scratching, wear and denting | Ceramics, hardened tool steel | Lead, pure aluminium |
| Toughness | How much energy it absorbs before it breaks; it resists cracking under sudden impact | Low carbon steel, nylon, polycarbonate | Glass, cast iron, acrylic |
| Brittleness | Breaks suddenly with little or no bending; the opposite of tough | Cast iron, glass, ceramics, acrylic | Copper, low carbon steel |
| Ductility | Can be pulled out into a thin wire without breaking | Copper, aluminium, gold | Cast iron |
| Malleability | Can be hammered, pressed or rolled into shape without cracking | Lead, copper, aluminium | Cast iron, high carbon steel |

Hardness and toughness are often confused. A hard material resists surface damage; a tough material resists breaking. Many hard materials (glass, a fully hardened file) are brittle, so they are hard but not tough.

Strength and stiffness are also different. A thin steel ruler is strong (hard to snap) but not very stiff in that shape (it bends easily). Stiffness depends on both the material and the shape of the part.

Identifying materials by appearance:

- Metals are shiny when cut or polished, cold to touch, and heavy for their size (apart from aluminium, which is light). A magnet picks up most ferrous metals.
- Copper is pinkish-orange; brass is yellow-gold; bronze is a darker brown-gold; aluminium is light, silver-grey; lead is dull grey, very heavy and soft enough to scratch with a fingernail.
- Thermoplastics are often brightly coloured or clear and can be smooth and glossy.
- Composites often show their structure: woven fibres in carbon fibre and GRP, layers in plywood, flakes in OSB.

Handling and machining behaviour: soft, ductile metals such as aluminium and copper machine easily but can clog cutters; cast iron machines well because the graphite flakes break chips off cleanly; hardened steel is very hard to cut and is usually machined before hardening. Thermoplastics can melt and smear if cutting speeds are too high. Composites can splinter or delaminate at the edges, and carbon-fibre dust is hazardous, so dust extraction and masks are needed.

### Common mistakes

- Using "strong" for every good property. Say which property you mean: strong in tension, hard, tough or stiff.
- Saying hard means tough. Glass is hard but brittle.
- Forgetting to link the property to the use in a "justify the choice" question. Name the property and say why the product needs it.

## 3.1.1.1 Metals and alloys

### What you need to know

- Ferrous metals contain iron: cast iron, low carbon steel, high carbon steel, stainless steel.
- Non-ferrous metals contain no iron: aluminium, copper, lead, zinc, and the alloys brass and bronze.
- An alloy is a mixture of a metal with at least one other element.
- Properties can be changed by alloying, by changing grain size through heating, by cold working (work hardening), by hardening and quenching, and by changing the carbon content of steel.
- Corrosion and how it affects metals.

### Explained

| Metal | Type | Key properties | Typical uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cast iron | Ferrous (about 2 to 4% carbon) | Hard, brittle, strong in compression, casts well, machines well | Engine blocks, machine beds, vices, manhole covers |
| Low carbon (mild) steel | Ferrous (under about 0.3% carbon) | Tough, ductile, malleable, easy to weld, rusts; cannot be hardened much by heat treatment | Car bodies, girders, nuts and bolts, brackets |
| High carbon steel | Ferrous (about 0.7 to 1.4% carbon) | Very hard after heat treatment but more brittle; harder to bend | Cutting tools, chisels, springs, drill bits |
| Stainless steel | Ferrous alloy (iron, chromium, nickel) | Resists corrosion, hard, tough, hard to machine | Sinks, cutlery, surgical tools, food plant |
| Aluminium | Non-ferrous | Light (low density), good conductor, corrosion resistant (forms an oxide layer), ductile, malleable | Aircraft panels, drinks cans, heat sinks, bike frames (as alloys) |
| Copper | Non-ferrous | Excellent electrical and thermal conductor, ductile, malleable, corrosion resistant | Wiring, PCB tracks, pipes, heat exchangers |
| Lead | Non-ferrous | Very dense, very soft and malleable, corrosion resistant, toxic | Roof flashing, radiation shielding, battery plates |
| Zinc | Non-ferrous | Corrosion resistant, low melting point | Coating steel (galvanising), die-cast parts |
| Brass | Non-ferrous alloy (copper and zinc) | Corrosion resistant, machines very well, attractive gold colour, low friction | Plumbing fittings, electrical terminals, screws |
| Bronze | Non-ferrous alloy (copper and tin) | Tough, hard wearing, corrosion resistant (including sea water) | Bearings, bushes, ship propellers, statues |

How properties are changed:

- Alloying. Mixing in other elements changes the properties. Pure metals have regular rows of atoms that slide over each other easily. Atoms of a different size disrupt the rows, so layers slide less and the alloy is harder and stronger. Chromium and nickel in stainless steel also give corrosion resistance.
- Carbon content. More carbon in steel makes it harder and stronger but less ductile and more brittle. Low carbon steel bends; high carbon steel can be hardened to cut other metals.
- Grain size. Metals are made of crystals called grains. Heating and then cooling slowly lets large grains grow, giving a softer, more ductile metal. Faster cooling gives smaller grains, which are generally harder and stronger. (The heat treatments are in file 04.)
- Cold working (work hardening). Bending, hammering or rolling a metal at room temperature distorts and squashes the grains. The metal becomes harder and stronger but more brittle. Bend a piece of copper back and forth and it gets stiffer, then cracks. Annealing reverses this.
- Hardening and quenching. High carbon steel heated to red heat then plunged into water or oil becomes very hard but brittle. Tempering afterwards trades some hardness for toughness.
- Corrosion. Iron and steel rust when exposed to both water and oxygen; the rust flakes off, so corrosion continues and the part weakens. Aluminium, copper and zinc form a thin oxide layer that sticks and protects the metal underneath. Corrosion is prevented by coatings (paint, galvanising, plating), by choosing a resistant metal, or by keeping parts dry and oiled.

### Common mistakes

- Calling stainless steel non-ferrous. It is mostly iron, so it is ferrous.
- Saying "aluminium is strong" as the reason for using it in aircraft. The key property is low density (light) with a good strength-to-weight ratio when alloyed.
- Saying all ferrous metals are magnetic. Some stainless steels are not.
- Mixing up brass (copper and zinc) and bronze (copper and tin).

## 3.1.1.2 Polymers

### What you need to know

- Thermoplastics: ABS, acrylic (PMMA), nylon, polycarbonate, polystyrene.
- Thermosetting polymers: epoxy resin, polyester resin, melamine resin (melamine-formaldehyde), polyurethane, vulcanised rubber.
- The effects of heat on thermoplastics and thermosetting polymers.

### Explained

The difference comes from the structure. In a thermoplastic, long polymer chains are tangled but not joined to each other. Heating gives the chains enough energy to slide apart, so the plastic softens and can be reshaped; cooling sets it again. This can be repeated, so thermoplastics can usually be recycled.

In a thermosetting polymer, the chains are joined to each other by strong cross-links that form when the polymer is first cured (set). Heating cannot break these links without breaking down the material, so a thermoset does not soften. Too much heat makes it char and burn. Thermosets cannot be remoulded, so they are much harder to recycle, but they cope with heat and keep their shape.

| Polymer | Type | Properties | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ABS | Thermoplastic | Tough, impact resistant, good surface finish, easily moulded | Phone cases, appliance housings, 3D printer filament, toy bricks |
| Acrylic (PMMA) | Thermoplastic | Clear like glass but lighter, hard, scratches, brittle, can be laser cut and line bent | Displays, signs, light diffusers, guards |
| Nylon | Thermoplastic | Tough, low friction, wear resistant, self-lubricating | Gears, bearings, bushes, cable ties |
| Polycarbonate | Thermoplastic | Extremely tough and impact resistant, transparent, heat resistant for a thermoplastic | Safety glasses, riot shields, machine guards, headlamp lenses |
| Polystyrene | Thermoplastic | Rigid, light, cheap; expanded form is a good insulator | Packaging, disposable cups, model kits |
| Epoxy resin | Thermoset | Strong adhesive, good electrical insulator, resists chemicals | Adhesives, PCB base board (with glass fibre), matrix for carbon fibre |
| Polyester resin | Thermoset | Rigid, cheap, sets at room temperature with a hardener | Matrix for GRP boat hulls and car panels |
| Melamine resin | Thermoset | Hard, scratch and heat resistant | Laminate worktops, heat-resistant handles, tableware |
| Polyurethane | Thermoset (rigid forms) | Can be a rigid or flexible foam or a tough coating | Insulation foam, varnishes, wheels, seals |
| Vulcanised rubber | Thermoset (cross-linked with sulfur) | Elastic, tough, hard wearing, keeps its shape when warm | Tyres, drive belts, seals, hoses |

Vulcanisation is a good example of how cross-links change properties: raw natural rubber goes sticky when warm and brittle when cold; sulfur cross-links make it elastic and stable over a wide temperature range.

### Common mistakes

- Saying thermosets "melt at a higher temperature". They do not melt at all; they char.
- Saying thermoplastics are weaker than thermosets as a rule. Polycarbonate and nylon are very tough. The real difference is how they respond to heat.
- Forgetting that a thermoset is set by a chemical curing reaction, usually with a hardener or catalyst, not by cooling.

## 3.1.1.3 Composites

### What you need to know

- A composite is two or more materials combined so that the result has better properties than either on its own.
- Fibre-reinforced polymers: carbon-fibre reinforced polymer (CFRP) and glass-reinforced plastic (GRP).
- Manufactured boards: plywood, MDF (medium density fibreboard), OSB (oriented strand board).
- Structural (reinforced) concrete.
- Properties can be changed by the direction of the reinforcement, the matrix material, the amount of reinforcement, and the size and shape of the reinforcement.

### Explained

Most composites have a reinforcement (fibres, strands, layers or bars that carry the load) held in a matrix (the material that binds them and spreads the load between them).

| Composite | Reinforcement | Matrix | Properties | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CFRP | Carbon fibres | Epoxy resin | Very high strength and stiffness for its weight, expensive | Racing car bodies, bike frames, aircraft parts, sports equipment |
| GRP | Glass fibres | Polyester resin | Strong, light, corrosion proof, cheaper than CFRP, can be moulded to complex shapes | Boat hulls, car body panels, water tanks |
| Plywood | Thin wood veneers (plies), grain at right angles in each layer | Adhesive | Strong in all directions along the sheet, resists splitting, stable | Furniture, floors, formwork for concrete |
| MDF | Fine wood fibres | Resin adhesive | Smooth, even, no grain, machines and paints well; swells if wet; weak at screws in edges | Flat-pack furniture, panels, templates |
| OSB | Large wood strands in layers | Resin adhesive | Strong, cheap, rough surface, uses waste timber | Roof and floor decking, wall sheathing |
| Reinforced concrete | Steel bars (rebar) | Concrete | Concrete is strong in compression; the steel adds tensile strength | Beams, floors, foundations, bridges |

How the properties can be changed:

- Direction of reinforcement. Fibres carry load best along their length. All fibres in one direction gives maximum strength in that direction only; woven or layered fibres at different angles give strength in several directions (as in plywood's alternating grain).
- Matrix. Changing the resin changes heat resistance, cost and toughness. Epoxy is stronger and sticks better than polyester but costs more.
- Amount of reinforcement. More fibre (up to a limit) gives a stronger, stiffer part. Too little resin leaves dry fibres that are not bonded.
- Size and shape of reinforcement. Long continuous fibres give higher strength than short chopped fibres; chopped strand mat is cheaper and easier to shape. Larger strands in OSB versus tiny fibres in MDF give different strengths and surface finishes. Ribbed rebar grips the concrete better than smooth bar.

### Common mistakes

- Saying concrete is strong in tension. It cracks under tension; the steel is there to carry the tension.
- Calling MDF a natural timber. It is a manufactured board, a composite.
- Saying carbon fibre is used because it is cheap. It is chosen for strength-to-weight ratio despite its cost.

## 3.1.1.4 Other materials

### What you need to know

- Structural timber and its grades.
- Ceramics.

### Explained

Structural timber is solid wood used to carry loads in buildings, such as joists, rafters and studs. Wood is a natural composite of cellulose fibres in a lignin matrix, so it is strongest along the grain. Every piece is different: knots, splits and the slope of the grain weaken it. So structural timber is strength graded: each piece is checked (by eye or by a machine that measures its stiffness) and given a strength class. In the UK common classes are C16 and C24 for softwoods; the number relates to the bending strength, so C24 can carry more load than C16. Grading lets engineers use calculated loads safely. Timber is renewable, light for its strength and easy to work, but it can rot, burn and change size with moisture.

Ceramics are inorganic, non-metallic materials made by shaping and then firing at high temperature, for example clay products, porcelain, glass and engineering ceramics such as alumina and silicon carbide. Their properties: very hard, wear resistant, strong in compression, resist high temperatures, good electrical and thermal insulators, chemically inert, but brittle (low toughness) and weak in tension. Uses: spark plug insulators, cutting tool tips, brake discs on high-performance cars, furnace linings, circuit-board substrates, tiles.

### Common mistakes

- Saying timber is equally strong in all directions. It is much stronger along the grain.
- Describing ceramics as tough. They are hard, but they shatter under impact.

## Mark scheme

1. The ability to be drawn or stretched out into a thin wire without breaking (1).
2. Hard: resists scratching and wear (1). Not tough: absorbs little energy, so it shatters on impact / is brittle (1).
3. Ferrous metal named, e.g. low carbon steel (1) with matching use and property, e.g. car body, malleable so it can be pressed (1). Non-ferrous alloy named, e.g. brass (1) with matching use and property, e.g. plumbing fittings, corrosion resistant / machines well (1). Accept bronze, any correct pairing.
4. Different-sized atoms disrupt the regular layers of atoms (1), so layers cannot slide over each other as easily (1).
5. Work hardening (1). The grains are distorted / squashed (1), making the metal harder and less ductile, so it becomes brittle and cracks (1).
6. Aluminium forms a thin oxide layer that sticks to the surface (1) and stops oxygen and water reaching the metal underneath; rust on steel flakes off, exposing fresh metal (1).
7. Thermoplastic: chains not joined to each other (1); on heating the chains slide apart so it softens and can be reshaped (1). Thermoset: chains joined by cross-links (1); on heating it does not soften but chars / burns (1).
8. Melamine is a thermoset so it will not soften near the hot kettle (1); it is hard / heat resistant / a good thermal insulator so it is safe to hold (1).
9. A loaded beam bends, so the bottom is stretched / in tension (1). Concrete is weak in tension (1). Steel is strong in tension, so it carries the tensile load and stops cracks (1).
10. Any two: change the direction / layout of the fibres (1); change the matrix resin (1); change the amount / proportion of fibre (1); change fibre length or form, e.g. continuous versus chopped (1).
11. Each piece varies because of knots / grain / defects (1), so grading gives a known strength class so loads can be calculated safely (1).
12. Any two: good electrical insulator (1); withstands high temperatures (1); hard / wear resistant (1); chemically inert / does not corrode (1).

## 4.2.2.4 Chlorophyll and other plant pigments

*Refs: AQA 8465 4.2.2.4, RP9.*

### What you need to know

- Leaves contain several pigments, not just one: chlorophylls (green) and carotenoids (yellow and orange).
- Pigments can be separated and identified by chromatography.
- Chromatography has a **stationary phase** and a **mobile phase**; substances separate because they are shared differently between the two phases.
- Calculate and use **Rf values**: Rf = distance moved by the substance ÷ distance moved by the solvent.
- A pure substance gives one spot in every solvent; a mixture may give several.

### Explained

Different pigments absorb different colours (wavelengths) of light. Chlorophyll absorbs mostly red and blue light and reflects green, which is why leaves look green. Carotenoids absorb blue and green light. Having several pigments lets the leaf use a wider range of the light that falls on it for photosynthesis. In autumn, chlorophyll breaks down first, so the yellow and orange carotenoids show.

The main pigments usually seen on a leaf chromatogram are:

| Pigment | Colour |
| --- | --- |
| Carotene | Orange-yellow |
| Xanthophylls | Yellow |
| Chlorophyll a | Blue-green |
| Chlorophyll b | Yellow-green |

In the non-water solvents used for leaf pigments, carotene usually travels furthest, so it has the largest Rf value. The order of the others depends on the solvent and paper.

### Worked example

On a leaf chromatogram the solvent front is 8.0 cm from the baseline. An orange spot is at 7.6 cm and a blue-green spot at 3.6 cm.

Rf (orange) = 7.6 ÷ 8.0 = 0.95. Rf (blue-green) = 3.6 ÷ 8.0 = 0.45.

Rf values have no units and are always less than 1.

### Practical

**Required practical activity 9** (paper chromatography; the Synergy version places it with plant pigments). The general method, Rf calculation and risks are in Chemistry 4.8.1.3. For leaf pigments:

1. Grind a few fresh leaves (spinach works well) with a little sand and propanone (or ethanol) in a mortar and pestle to extract the pigments.
2. Draw a pencil baseline about 2 cm from the bottom of the chromatography paper.
3. Use a fine capillary tube to put a small spot of extract on the baseline. Let it dry and add more spots on top, to build a small, concentrated spot.
4. Stand the paper in a beaker with a little solvent (below the baseline). Cover the beaker.
5. When the solvent is near the top, remove the paper, mark the solvent front, and let it dry. Work quickly: pigments fade in bright light.
6. Measure from the baseline to the centre of each spot and calculate Rf values.

Propanone and many chromatography solvents are highly flammable and give off harmful vapour: no naked flames, work in a well-ventilated room, and wear eye protection.

### Common mistakes

- Making the spot too large or too dilute, so the pigments smear together.
- Using a water-based solvent: leaf pigments do not dissolve well in water, so they barely move.
- Saying plants have only one pigment.

## 4.3.2.1 Absorption and emission of radiation by atoms

*Refs: AQA 8465 4.3.2.1.*

### What you need to know

- Electrons in atoms are at particular energy levels (Physics 4.4.1.2).
- Atoms gain energy by being heated, by an electric current, or by absorbing electromagnetic radiation. Some electrons move to higher energy levels.
- When electrons drop back to lower levels, the atom gives out electromagnetic radiation.
- The **frequency** of the radiation depends on the **size of the energy jump**: a bigger jump gives a higher frequency.
- Atoms of some elements, such as neon and sodium, give out visible light; others, such as mercury, give out mainly ultraviolet.

### Explained

Each element has its own set of energy levels, so it can only give out certain frequencies. That is why every element has its own colour of light and why flame tests and street lights have characteristic colours.

- **Neon** signs: an electric current through neon gas makes it glow red-orange.
- **Sodium** street lamps: sodium vapour gives a strong yellow-orange light.
- **Mercury** vapour: an electric current makes mercury atoms give out mostly ultraviolet radiation. In a fluorescent tube, a coating on the inside of the glass absorbs this ultraviolet and gives out visible light.

Higher frequency means more energy is carried, so ultraviolet comes from larger energy jumps than red light.

### Common mistakes

- Saying an electron gives out radiation when it moves to a higher level. It absorbs energy going up and emits radiation coming down.
- Thinking all atoms give out the same colour. The energy levels, and so the frequencies, are different for each element.

## 4.4.1.7 The water cycle

*Refs: AQA 8465 4.4.1.7.*

### What you need to know

- Water exists as a solid (glaciers, ice sheets), a liquid (oceans, rivers, lakes, underground in aquifers) and a gas (water vapour in the atmosphere).
- It cycles through the environment by melting, freezing, evaporation and condensation; precipitation falls as rain, sleet or snow.
- Explain why the water cycle matters to living things.

### Explained

The Sun heats water in oceans, lakes and soil, and it evaporates. Plants also release water vapour from their leaves (transpiration). Water vapour rises, cools and condenses into tiny droplets, forming clouds. When droplets or ice crystals grow large enough, they fall as precipitation. On land, water runs off into rivers, soaks into the ground to refill aquifers, or is frozen into ice and snow, which melts later. Rivers and groundwater return water to the sea.

Why living things depend on water:

- It is the **solvent** in which the chemical reactions in cells take place.
- It **transports** dissolved substances into and out of cells and around organisms (blood plasma, xylem and phloem).
- It is a **reactant or product** in key reactions: a product of respiration, a reactant in photosynthesis, and a reactant when large food molecules are broken down in digestion.
- Rivers, lakes and seas are **habitats** for many organisms.

The water cycle also provides fresh water on land, because evaporation leaves salts behind in the sea.

### Common mistakes

- Saying water "disappears" when it evaporates. It changes state and stays in the cycle.
- Leaving out transpiration as a way water returns to the atmosphere.
- Mixing up condensation (gas to liquid, forming clouds) and precipitation (water falling from clouds).

## 4.6.3.3 The Earth's magnetism

*Refs: AQA 8465 4.6.3.3.*

### What you need to know

- A compass needle is a small bar magnet that lines up with the Earth's magnetic field. This shows the Earth has a magnetic field, so its core must be magnetic (Physics 4.7.1.2).
- The field is probably caused by movements in the liquid, iron-rich **outer core**.
- The magnetic poles move slowly, and the field **reverses** direction from time to time, at irregular intervals. The last reversal was about 800 000 years ago.
- These changes show the core's magnetism is **dynamic** (always changing), not like a fixed permanent magnet.

### Explained

The Earth's outer core is very hot liquid metal, mostly iron. It flows as the Earth spins and as heat escapes from the inner core. Moving, conducting liquid can produce a magnetic field, a bit like a current in a coil. A solid permanent magnet would keep a fixed field, but the Earth's field changes: the magnetic poles wander by many kilometres a year, and over hundreds of thousands of years the north and south magnetic poles swap over. The time between reversals is not regular, so a reversal cannot be predicted from a pattern.

Scientists cannot drill to the core, so ideas about it come from indirect evidence such as compass behaviour, measurements of the field over time, and models. This is an example of a question where the data that would settle it cannot be collected directly, because of the scale and depth involved.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the Earth's core is a giant bar magnet. A permanent magnet could not reverse its field; the core is too hot to stay permanently magnetised.
- Saying reversals happen at regular intervals.
- Forgetting that the north-seeking pole of a compass points to the Earth's north geographic pole because the magnetic pole there behaves as a south magnetic pole.

## 4.7.1.3 Circular motion (HT only)

*Refs: AQA 8465 4.7.1.3.*

### What you need to know

- Velocity is speed in a given direction, so it is a vector.
- An object moving in a circle at **constant speed** has a **changing velocity**, because its direction is always changing.
- Give examples (qualitative only).

### Explained

Because the velocity changes, the object is accelerating, and there must be a resultant force. This force always acts towards the centre of the circle, at right angles to the motion, so it changes the direction but not the speed.

| Example | What provides the force towards the centre |
| --- | --- |
| Moon orbiting the Earth; satellites | Gravity |
| Ball whirled on a string | Tension in the string |
| Car going round a roundabout at steady speed | Friction between tyres and road |
| Person on a fairground ride | Push (contact force) of the seat or wall |

If the force stops (the string breaks), the object carries on in a straight line in the direction it was moving at that moment, as Newton's first law predicts. Orbits are covered in Physics 4.8.1.3.

### Common mistakes

- Saying an object at constant speed in a circle is not accelerating. Its velocity changes, so it is accelerating.
- Saying the object flies outwards when the string breaks. It moves off along the tangent.

## 4.9 Key ideas

*Refs: AQA 8465 4.9.*

### What you need to know

AQA lists a small number of big ideas in each science that run through the whole course and can be assessed on any paper. Being able to link a topic to its key idea helps with longer, synoptic questions.

### Explained

**Biology key ideas, with examples.**

- The structure of a molecule suits its function: enzymes' active sites; DNA's base pairs.
- Cells are the basic units of life, and they form tissues, organs and organ systems: the circulatory and digestive systems.
- Organisms form populations, communities and ecosystems, interacting with each other, the environment and humans: food webs, competition, deforestation.
- Organisms depend on each other and are adapted to their surroundings: predators and prey, extremophiles.
- Life depends on photosynthesis, which traps light energy to make organic compounds from carbon dioxide and water.
- Organic compounds are used as fuels in respiration, which releases energy for the other reactions of life.
- Chemicals cycle through ecosystems: the carbon and water cycles, decay.
- An organism's characteristics depend on its genome and its environment.
- Evolution by natural selection explains both biodiversity and how organisms are related.

**Chemistry key ideas, with examples.**

- Matter is made of atoms; about 100 kinds occur naturally, called elements.
- Elements show repeating (periodic) patterns in their properties, explained by their atomic structure: Group 1 and Group 7 trends.
- Atoms bond by transferring electrons (ionic bonding) or sharing them (covalent bonding).
- The shapes of molecules and the arrangement of giant structures control their properties: diamond and graphite.
- Reactions have energy barriers (activation energy), so they happen at different rates.
- Reactions involve one of three things: transferring protons (acids and bases), transferring electrons (redox, electrolysis) or sharing electrons (covalent bond formation, as in polymerisation).
- Energy is conserved in reactions: exothermic and endothermic changes balance with the surroundings.

**Physics key ideas, with examples.**

- Models are used to explain things: the particle model of matter, and wave models of light and sound.
- Cause and effect links quantities: resultant force causes acceleration; changes in nuclei cause radioactive emissions.
- Fields explain action at a distance: gravitational, magnetic and electric effects.
- Differences drive change: a pressure difference drives fluid flow; a temperature difference drives heat transfer; a potential difference drives current.
- Proportionality appears in many models: weight ∝ mass; extension ∝ force (up to the limit of proportionality).
- Physical laws are written as mathematical equations.

### Common mistakes

- Treating each topic as separate. Long questions often expect you to use an idea from one topic (such as the particle model) to explain another (such as gas pressure or diffusion).

## Mark scheme

1. Different pigments absorb different wavelengths / colours of light (1); so a wider range of light energy can be used for photosynthesis (1).
2. 6.3 ÷ 9.0 (1) = 0.70 (1).
3. Any two: no naked flames (flammable); ventilated room (harmful vapour); eye protection; cover the beaker.
4. Electricity / heating gives electrons energy and they move to higher levels (1). When they drop back they emit radiation (1). Sodium's energy jumps are particular sizes, so the light has particular frequencies (yellow-orange) (1).
5. Mercury atoms give out mostly ultraviolet, which cannot be seen (1). The coating absorbs the ultraviolet and gives out visible light (1).
6. Any two ways: evaporation from oceans / lakes / soil; transpiration from plants; (also credit sublimation from ice) (2). Any two reasons: solvent for reactions in cells; transports dissolved substances; reactant in photosynthesis / product of respiration; reactant in digestion (2).
7. The field changes: the poles move slowly (1); the field reverses at irregular intervals (1); a permanent magnet would have a fixed field, and the core is liquid metal whose movement can produce a changing field (1).
8. Its direction keeps changing, so its velocity changes (1); a change in velocity is an acceleration (1). Friction between the tyres and the road provides the force towards the centre (1).

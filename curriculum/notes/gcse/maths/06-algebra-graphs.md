# Algebra: graphs

This file covers coordinates, straight-line graphs (gradient, intercept, parallel and perpendicular lines), quadratic graphs, the shapes of standard graphs, transformations of graphs, graphs in real contexts, gradients and areas under curves, rates of change, and the equation of a circle.

## Overview

Coordinates, straight lines, quadratic, cubic and reciprocal graphs, and real-life graphs are on both tiers. Higher only: perpendicular lines, turning points by completing the square, exponential and trigonometric graphs, translations and reflections of graphs, gradients and areas under curves, instantaneous rate of change, and the equation of a circle and its tangents.

### Board differences

- AQA and Edexcel: A8 to A16, plus R14 and R15 (gradient as rate of change), which sit in the Ratio section of the DfE content.
- OCR J560: section 7 (7.01 graphs of equations and functions, 7.02 straight lines, 7.03 transformations, 7.04 interpreting graphs). OCR splits A12 into 7.01c (polynomials and reciprocal), 7.01d (exponential, Higher) and 7.01e (trigonometric, Higher), and lists the circle equation as 7.01f.
- Eduqas C300QS: FA8 to FA12 and FA13 (which matches A14) on Foundation; HA8 to HA16 on Higher. Rate of change is FR15 and HR15, and instantaneous rate of change is HR16. Eduqas also uses the words "stationary points" alongside turning points.
- OCR and the DfE content separate sketching (showing key features freehand) from plotting (using a table of values on graph paper).

## A8 Coordinates

*Refs: AQA A8 · Edexcel A8 · OCR 7.01a · Eduqas FA8, HA8. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- Read and plot coordinates (x, y) in all four quadrants.
- Find the midpoint of a line segment.

### Explained

The x-coordinate is across (horizontal) and the y-coordinate is up or down (vertical). "Along the corridor, then up the stairs."

The midpoint of the line from (x₁, y₁) to (x₂, y₂) is the mean of the coordinates:

```latex
\left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2},\ \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)
```

### Worked example

Find the midpoint of A(−3, 7) and B(5, −1).

1. x: (−3 + 5)/2 = 1. y: (7 + (−1))/2 = 3.
2. Midpoint (1, 3).

M(4, 2) is the midpoint of PQ. P is (1, −3). Find Q.

1. From P to M: x goes up 3, y goes up 5.
2. From M to Q do the same: Q = (7, 7).

### Common mistakes

- Plotting (y, x) instead of (x, y).
- Subtracting instead of adding when finding a midpoint.

## A9 Straight-line graphs

*Refs: AQA A9 · Edexcel A9 · OCR 7.01b, 7.02a, 7.02b · Eduqas FA9, HA9. Tier: F & H (part Higher only).*

### What you need to know

- Plot straight-line graphs from their equations using a table of values.
- Recognise and use y = mx + c, where m is the gradient and c the y-intercept.
- Lines with the same gradient are parallel.
- (HT only) Perpendicular lines have gradients that multiply to −1: if one has gradient m, the other has gradient −1/m.
- Find the equation of a line through two points, or through one point with a given gradient.
- Lines such as x = 3 (vertical) and y = −2 (horizontal).

### Explained

Rearrange into y = mx + c to read off m and c. For 2y − 6x = 8: 2y = 6x + 8, so y = 3x + 4, gradient 3, intercept 4.

To find the equation through two points:

1. Gradient m = change in y ÷ change in x.
2. Substitute one point into y = mx + c to find c.

(HT only) Perpendicular: flip the gradient and change its sign. Gradient 2/3 becomes −3/2; gradient −4 becomes 1/4.

### Worked example

Find the equation of the line through (2, 5) and (6, 17).

1. m = (17 − 5)/(6 − 2) = 12/4 = 3.
2. 5 = 3 × 2 + c, so c = −1.
3. y = 3x − 1.

Find the equation of the line parallel to y = −2x + 7 that passes through (4, 3).

1. Gradient −2. 3 = −8 + c, so c = 11.
2. y = −2x + 11.

(HT only) Find the equation of the line perpendicular to y = ½x + 1 that passes through (2, −3).

1. Perpendicular gradient: −1 ÷ ½ = −2.
2. −3 = −2 × 2 + c, so c = 1.
3. y = −2x + 1.

### Common mistakes

- Reading the gradient before rearranging: in 2y = 6x + 8 the gradient is 3, not 6.
- Calculating the gradient as change in x ÷ change in y.
- (HT only) Using −m instead of −1/m for a perpendicular gradient.
- Mixing up x = 3 (vertical) and y = 3 (horizontal).

## A10 Gradient and intercept

*Refs: AQA A10 · Edexcel A10 · OCR 7.02a · Eduqas FA10, HA10. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- Identify and interpret the gradient and y-intercept of a linear graph, from the graph and from its equation.

### Explained

Gradient measures steepness: how much y goes up for each 1 that x goes across. A positive gradient slopes up left to right; a negative one slopes down. In a real context the gradient is a rate (for example pounds per hour), and the intercept is the starting value (for example a fixed call-out fee).

### Worked example

A plumber's charge C (in £) for h hours is shown by a straight line through (0, 45) and (4, 165). Interpret the gradient and intercept.

1. Intercept 45: a fixed charge of £45 before any work.
2. Gradient (165 − 45)/4 = 30: £30 per hour.
3. Equation C = 30h + 45.

### Common mistakes

- Reading the gradient from scales without checking each square's value.
- Describing the gradient without units or context.

## A11 Quadratic graphs: roots, intercepts and turning points

*Refs: AQA A11 · Edexcel A11 · OCR 7.01c · Eduqas FA11, HA11. Tier: F & H (part Higher only).*

### What you need to know

- A quadratic graph y = ax² + bx + c is a parabola: ∪-shaped if a > 0, ∩-shaped if a < 0.
- Identify roots (where the graph crosses the x-axis), the y-intercept and the turning point from a graph.
- Find the roots algebraically by solving y = 0.
- (HT only) Find the turning point by completing the square: y = (x + p)² + q has turning point (−p, q).

### Explained

The graph is symmetrical about a vertical line through its turning point. That line is halfway between the roots, so if the roots are 1 and 5, the line of symmetry is x = 3.

The y-intercept is the value of c, because putting x = 0 leaves y = c.

(HT only) In y = (x + p)² + q, the squared bracket is never negative, so the smallest y is q, when x = −p. That gives the minimum point (−p, q).

### Worked example

For y = x² − 6x + 5, find the roots, the y-intercept and the turning point.

1. Roots: x² − 6x + 5 = 0, (x − 1)(x − 5) = 0, x = 1 or 5.
2. y-intercept: 5, so the point (0, 5).
3. Line of symmetry x = 3; y = 9 − 18 + 5 = −4. Turning point (3, −4).

(HT only) Find the turning point of y = x² + 8x + 21 by completing the square.

1. x² + 8x + 21 = (x + 4)² − 16 + 21 = (x + 4)² + 5.
2. Turning point (−4, 5). It is a minimum.

### Common mistakes

- Reading roots from the y-axis instead of the x-axis.
- (HT only) Getting the sign of the x-coordinate wrong: (x + 4)² + 5 has its turning point at x = −4.

## A12 Recognising and sketching standard graphs

*Refs: AQA A12 · Edexcel A12 · OCR 7.01c, 7.01d, 7.01e · Eduqas FA12, HA12. Tier: F & H (part Higher only).*

### What you need to know

- Recognise, sketch and interpret graphs of linear functions, quadratic functions, simple cubic functions and the reciprocal function y = 1/x (x ≠ 0).
- (HT only) Exponential functions y = kˣ for positive k.
- (HT only) The trigonometric functions y = sin x, y = cos x and y = tan x for angles of any size, in degrees.

### Explained

| Graph | Shape and key features |
| --- | --- |
| y = mx + c | Straight line |
| y = x² | ∪ shape, minimum at (0, 0), symmetric about the y-axis |
| y = −x² | ∩ shape, maximum at (0, 0) |
| y = x³ | Rises from bottom left to top right, flattens through (0, 0) |
| y = −x³ | Falls from top left to bottom right |
| y = 1/x | Two separate curves in the 1st and 3rd quadrants; never touches either axis (the axes are asymptotes) |
| (HT) y = kˣ, k > 1 | Passes through (0, 1), always above the x-axis, rises steeply to the right, gets close to the x-axis to the left |
| (HT) y = kˣ, 0 < k < 1 | Passes through (0, 1), falls to the right |
| (HT) y = sin x | Wave between −1 and 1, period 360°, through (0, 0), max at (90°, 1), min at (270°, −1) |
| (HT) y = cos x | Wave between −1 and 1, period 360°, starts at (0, 1), min at (180°, −1) |
| (HT) y = tan x | Period 180°, through (0, 0), (45°, 1); asymptotes at x = 90°, 270°, … |

(HT only) The symmetry of the trig graphs gives more than one solution to equations such as sin x = 0.5. If one solution is 30°, another in 0° to 360° is 180° − 30° = 150°. For cos x = k, the second solution is 360° minus the first.

### Worked example

Match each equation to its description: y = 4 − x², y = 2/x, y = x³ + 1.

1. y = 4 − x² is a ∩ parabola with maximum (0, 4).
2. y = 2/x is a reciprocal graph in the 1st and 3rd quadrants.
3. y = x³ + 1 is a cubic crossing the y-axis at 1.

(HT only) Given sin 30° = 0.5, find all solutions of sin x = 0.5 for 0° ≤ x ≤ 360°.

1. x = 30° and x = 180° − 30° = 150°.

(HT only) A population is modelled by P = 200 × 1.5ᵗ. Find P when t = 0 and t = 3.

1. t = 0: 200 × 1 = 200.
2. t = 3: 200 × 3.375 = 675.

### Common mistakes

- Drawing a quadratic with a pointed V at the bottom. It should be a smooth curve.
- Letting y = 1/x cross the axes.
- (HT only) Drawing y = 2ˣ through the origin. It passes through (0, 1).

## A13 Translations and reflections of graphs

*Refs: AQA A13 · Edexcel A13 · OCR 7.03a · Eduqas HA13. Tier: Higher only.*

### What you need to know

- (HT only) Sketch translations and reflections of a given graph y = f(x).

### Explained

(HT only)

| New graph | Transformation |
| --- | --- |
| y = f(x) + a | Translation up by a: vector (0, a) |
| y = f(x + a) | Translation left by a: vector (−a, 0) |
| y = −f(x) | Reflection in the x-axis |
| y = f(−x) | Reflection in the y-axis |

Changes outside the bracket affect y and do what you expect. Changes inside the bracket affect x and do the opposite of what you expect: f(x + 3) moves left 3, not right.

### Worked example

(HT only) The graph y = f(x) has a maximum point at (2, 5). Write down the maximum point of:

1. y = f(x) − 4: (2, 1).
2. y = f(x − 3): (5, 5).
3. y = f(−x): (−2, 5).
4. y = −f(x): this is now a minimum point at (2, −5).

(HT only) y = x² is translated to y = (x + 1)² − 3. Describe the transformation.

1. Translation by the vector (−1, −3).

### Common mistakes

- (HT only) Moving f(x + a) to the right.
- (HT only) Changing the x-coordinate in y = f(x) + a.

## A14 Graphs in real contexts

*Refs: AQA A14 · Edexcel A14 · OCR 7.01b, 7.04a, 7.04b · Eduqas FA13, HA14. Tier: F & H (part Higher only).*

### What you need to know

- Plot and interpret graphs, including reciprocal graphs, and graphs of non-standard functions in real contexts.
- (HT only) Exponential graphs in real contexts.
- Use graphs to find approximate solutions to problems, including simple kinematic problems with distance, speed and acceleration.
- Distance-time graphs: the gradient is the speed. Velocity-time graphs: the gradient is the acceleration.

### Explained

Read the axes first: what is on each, and what are the units? In a distance-time graph a horizontal line means stopped, and a steeper line means faster. In a velocity-time graph a horizontal line means constant speed, and a sloping line means accelerating or decelerating.

Conversion graphs (for example pounds to euros) are straight lines through the origin, since 0 of one unit is 0 of the other.

Graphs of filling containers: a narrow part of a container fills quickly (steep graph); a wide part fills slowly (shallow graph).

### Worked example

A cyclist rides 12 km in 40 minutes, stops for 20 minutes, then rides home at a steady speed, arriving 30 minutes later. Find the speed on each stage in km/h.

1. Out: 12 km in 2/3 hour: 12 ÷ 2/3 = 18 km/h.
2. Stop: 0 km/h.
3. Home: 12 km in 0.5 hour: 24 km/h.

A car speeds up from 0 to 20 m/s in 8 s. Find its acceleration.

1. Gradient of velocity-time graph = 20/8 = 2.5 m/s².

### Common mistakes

- Forgetting to convert minutes to hours when finding speed in km/h.
- Reading a velocity-time graph as if it were a distance-time graph.

## A15 Gradients and areas under graphs

*Refs: AQA A15 · Edexcel A15 · OCR 7.04b, 7.04c · Eduqas HA15. Tier: Higher only.*

### What you need to know

- (HT only) Calculate or estimate gradients of graphs and areas under graphs, including curves.
- (HT only) Interpret them in context, for example distance-time graphs, velocity-time graphs, and financial graphs. (This does not use calculus.)
- (HT only) The area under a velocity-time graph is the distance travelled.

### Explained

(HT only) For a curve, estimate the gradient at a point by drawing a tangent (a straight line that just touches the curve there) and finding its gradient.

(HT only) Estimate the area under a curve by splitting it into strips, usually trapezia, and adding their areas. Each trapezium has area ½(a + b)h. If the curve bends away from the straight tops of the trapezia (curves upwards, like y = x²), the estimate is an overestimate; if it bulges above them, it is an underestimate.

### Worked example

(HT only) A velocity-time graph passes through (0, 0), (10, 8), (20, 12) and (30, 14), with t in seconds and v in m/s. Estimate the distance travelled in 30 s using three strips.

1. Strip 1: ½(0 + 8) × 10 = 40.
2. Strip 2: ½(8 + 12) × 10 = 100.
3. Strip 3: ½(12 + 14) × 10 = 130.
4. Total ≈ 270 m. The curve bulges above the chords (it is levelling off), so this is an underestimate.

(HT only) A tangent drawn to a distance-time curve at t = 5 s passes through (2, 4) and (8, 22). Estimate the speed at t = 5 s.

1. Gradient = (22 − 4)/(8 − 2) = 3 m/s.

### Common mistakes

- (HT only) Drawing a chord (a line crossing the curve twice) instead of a tangent when asked for the gradient at a point.
- (HT only) Using the wrong strip width when the scale is not 1 unit per square.

## A16 Equation of a circle and tangents

*Refs: AQA A16 · Edexcel A16 · OCR 7.01f, 7.02b · Eduqas HA16. Tier: Higher only.*

### What you need to know

- (HT only) The circle with centre the origin and radius r has equation

```latex
x^2 + y^2 = r^2
```

- (HT only) Find the equation of the tangent to such a circle at a given point.

### Explained

(HT only) The equation comes from Pythagoras: any point (x, y) on the circle is r away from (0, 0).

(HT only) A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point where it touches. So:

1. Find the gradient of the radius from (0, 0) to the point.
2. The tangent's gradient is the negative reciprocal.
3. Use the point to find c in y = mx + c.

### Worked example

(HT only) Write the equation of a circle with centre (0, 0) and radius 6.

1. x² + y² = 36.

(HT only) Find the equation of the tangent to x² + y² = 25 at the point (3, 4).

1. Gradient of radius: 4/3.
2. Tangent gradient: −3/4.
3. 4 = −3/4 × 3 + c, so c = 4 + 9/4 = 25/4.
4. y = −3/4 x + 25/4, or 3x + 4y = 25.

### Common mistakes

- (HT only) Giving the radius as 25 for x² + y² = 25. The radius is √25 = 5.
- (HT only) Using the radius gradient for the tangent.

## R14 Gradient as a rate of change; proportion graphs

*Refs: AQA R14 · Edexcel R14 · OCR 7.04a, 7.04b · Eduqas FR15, HR15. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- Interpret the gradient of a straight-line graph as a rate of change.
- Recognise and interpret graphs that show direct and inverse proportion.

### Explained

The gradient tells you how fast one quantity changes compared with the other: litres per minute, metres per second, pounds per item.

Direct proportion (y = kx) is a straight line through the origin. Inverse proportion (y = k/x) is a reciprocal curve that gets closer to both axes. More on proportion is in [09-ratio-and-proportion.md](09-ratio-and-proportion.md).

### Worked example

A bath fills in a straight line from 20 litres at 0 minutes to 140 litres at 8 minutes. Find the rate of filling.

1. Gradient = (140 − 20)/8 = 15 litres per minute.

### Common mistakes

- Giving a rate without units.
- Thinking any straight line shows direct proportion. It must pass through the origin.

## R15 Instantaneous and average rates of change

*Refs: AQA R15 · Edexcel R15 · OCR 7.04b · Eduqas HR16. Tier: Higher only.*

### What you need to know

- (HT only) The gradient at a point on a curve is the instantaneous rate of change; find it by drawing a tangent.
- (HT only) The gradient of a chord between two points is the average rate of change over that interval.
- (HT only) Apply these in numerical, algebraic and graphical contexts. (No calculus is needed.)

### Explained

(HT only) On a distance-time curve, the chord from t = 2 to t = 6 gives the average speed over those 4 seconds. The tangent at t = 4 gives the speed at that moment.

### Worked example

(HT only) The height of a plant is h = 0.5t² cm after t weeks. Find the average rate of growth between t = 2 and t = 4.

1. h(2) = 2 and h(4) = 8.
2. Average rate = (8 − 2)/(4 − 2) = 3 cm per week.

### Common mistakes

- (HT only) Confusing average rate (chord) with instantaneous rate (tangent).

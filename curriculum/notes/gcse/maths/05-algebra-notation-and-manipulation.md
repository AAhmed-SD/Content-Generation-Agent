# Algebra: notation, manipulation and functions

This file covers how algebra is written, substituting into expressions and formulae, the words expression, equation, formula, identity and inequality, simplifying, expanding and factorising (including quadratics and algebraic fractions), changing the subject of a formula, algebraic proof, and functions.

## Overview

Notation, substitution, simplifying, expanding two brackets, factorising x² + bx + c and the difference of two squares, and changing the subject are on both tiers. Higher only: expanding three or more brackets, factorising ax² + bx + c, algebraic fractions, completing the square, algebraic proof, and formal function notation including inverse and composite functions.

- AQA and Edexcel: A1 to A7.

- Eduqas C300QS: FA1 to FA7 and HA1 to HA7. FA4 includes expressions with surds on Foundation, like the DfE wording.

## A1 Algebraic notation

*Refs: AQA A1 · Edexcel A1 · OCR 6.01b, 6.01c, 6.01d, 6.01e, 6.01g · Eduqas FA1, HA1. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- ab means a × b; 3y means y + y + y and 3 × y; a² means a × a; a³ means a × a × a; a²b means a × a × b; a/b means a ÷ b.
- Coefficients are written as fractions rather than decimals (½x rather than 0.5x is common, and x/2 means the same).
- Brackets show what is grouped: 3(x + 2) means 3 × (x + 2).

### Explained

Algebra leaves out the × sign and writes the number first: 5 × n × m becomes 5mn. Letters are usually written in alphabetical order. A power applies only to what it touches: 3x² means 3 × x × x, but (3x)² means 3x × 3x = 9x².

### Worked example

Write each expression in its simplest algebraic form.

1. m × m × m × 4 = 4m³.
2. p ÷ 6 = p/6.
3. y × 2 × x × y = 2xy².

### Common mistakes

- Writing x² as 2x. x² is x times x; 2x is x plus x.
- Treating 3x² as (3x)².

## A2 Substitution

*Refs: AQA A2 · Edexcel A2 · OCR 6.02b, 6.02d, 6.02e · Eduqas FA2, HA2. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- Substitute numbers, including negatives and fractions, into expressions and formulae, including scientific formulae.

### Explained

Replace each letter with its value in brackets, then follow the order of operations. Brackets help with negative numbers: if x = −3, then x² = (−3)² = 9, and 2x² = 2 × 9 = 18.

### Worked example

Find the value of 4a − b² when a = 2.5 and b = −3.

1. 4 × 2.5 − (−3)².
2. 10 − 9 = 1.

Use E = ½mv² to find E when m = 6 and v = 4.

1. E = 0.5 × 6 × 4² = 0.5 × 6 × 16 = 48.

### Common mistakes

- Squaring a negative without brackets on a calculator: −3² on many calculators gives −9.
- Doing 2x² as (2x)².

## A3 Expressions, equations, formulae, identities and inequalities

*Refs: AQA A3 · Edexcel A3 · OCR 6.01a, 6.01b, 6.01d, 6.01e, 6.02d, 6.02e · Eduqas FA3, HA3. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- A term is a single number, letter or product such as 5, x or 3ab². Terms are separated by + and − signs.
- A factor is something that divides exactly; in 6xy the factors include 2, 3, x and y.
- An expression is a collection of terms with no equals sign: 3x + 7.
- An equation has an equals sign and is true only for certain values: 3x + 7 = 19.
- A formula links two or more variables: A = πr².
- An identity is true for every value of the variable, and is shown with ≡: 2(x + 3) ≡ 2x + 6.
- An inequality uses <, >, ≤ or ≥: 3x + 7 > 19.

### Explained

A quick test: can you solve it? If yes, it is an equation. If it is true whatever the value of the letter, it is an identity. If it is a rule for working out one quantity from others, it is a formula.

### Worked example

Choose the correct word for each.

1. 5(x − 1) ≡ 5x − 5: identity.
2. v = u + at: formula.
3. 4y − 3: expression.
4. 4y − 3 = 9: equation.

### Common mistakes

- Calling an expression an equation.
- Using = instead of ≡ when the question asks you to show an identity.

## A4 Simplifying, expanding and factorising

*Refs: AQA A4 · Edexcel A4 · OCR 6.01b, 6.01c, 6.01d, 6.01e, 6.01g, 6.02c · Eduqas FA4, HA4. Tier: F & H (part Higher only).*

### What you need to know

- Collect like terms.
- Multiply a single term over a bracket.
- Take out common factors.
- Expand products of two binomials, such as (x + 3)(x − 5).
- (HT only) Expand products of three or more binomials, such as (x + 1)(x − 2)(x + 4).
- Factorise quadratics of the form x² + bx + c, including the difference of two squares.
- (HT only) Factorise quadratics of the form ax² + bx + c.
- Simplify expressions with sums, products and powers, using the laws of indices.
- Expressions may involve surds.
- (HT only) Simplify and manipulate algebraic fractions.
- (HT only) Complete the square: write x² + bx + c as (x + p)² + q.

### Explained

Like terms have exactly the same letters and powers. 3x²y and −5x²y are like terms; 3x²y and 3xy² are not.

Index laws in algebra:

```latex
x^a \times x^b = x^{a+b} \qquad x^a \div x^b = x^{a-b} \qquad (x^a)^b = x^{ab}
```

So (2x³y)² = 4x⁶y², and 12a⁵b² ÷ 4a²b = 3a³b.

Expanding two brackets: multiply every term in the first bracket by every term in the second (FOIL or a grid), then collect like terms.

```latex
(x + a)(x + b) = x^2 + (a + b)x + ab
```

Factorising is expanding in reverse.

- Common factor: take out the highest common factor of every term. 6x²y − 9xy² = 3xy(2x − 3y).
- x² + bx + c: find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b.
- Difference of two squares:

```latex
a^2 - b^2 = (a + b)(a - b)
```

- (HT only) ax² + bx + c: find two numbers that multiply to ac and add to b. Split the middle term with them, then factorise in pairs.

(HT only) Three brackets: expand two of them first, then multiply the result by the third.

(HT only) Algebraic fractions follow the same rules as number fractions. Factorise everything first, then cancel common factors (whole brackets only), add over a common denominator, or flip and multiply to divide.

(HT only) Completing the square: halve the coefficient of x to get p, then subtract p² to balance.

```latex
x^2 + bx + c = \left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 + c
```

If the x² term has a coefficient, take it out of the x terms first: 2x² + 12x + 5 = 2(x² + 6x) + 5 = 2[(x + 3)² − 9] + 5 = 2(x + 3)² − 13.

### Worked example

Simplify 5a + 3b − 2a + 7 − 4b.

1. 5a − 2a = 3a; 3b − 4b = −b.
2. 3a − b + 7.

Expand and simplify 3(2x − 1) − 2(x − 4).

1. 6x − 3 − 2x + 8.
2. 4x + 5.

Expand and simplify (x − 6)(x + 2).

1. x² + 2x − 6x − 12.
2. x² − 4x − 12.

Factorise x² + 2x − 15.

1. Two numbers that multiply to −15 and add to 2: +5 and −3.
2. (x + 5)(x − 3).

Factorise 49 − y².

1. 7² − y² = (7 + y)(7 − y).

(HT only) Factorise 6x² + 7x − 3.

1. ac = 6 × (−3) = −18. Two numbers multiplying to −18 and adding to 7: 9 and −2.
2. 6x² + 9x − 2x − 3.
3. 3x(2x + 3) − 1(2x + 3).
4. (2x + 3)(3x − 1).

(HT only) Expand and simplify (x + 2)(x − 1)(2x + 3).

1. (x + 2)(x − 1) = x² + x − 2.
2. (x² + x − 2)(2x + 3) = 2x³ + 3x² + 2x² + 3x − 4x − 6.
3. 2x³ + 5x² − x − 6.

(HT only) Simplify (x² − 9)/(x² + x − 6).

1. Top: (x + 3)(x − 3). Bottom: (x + 3)(x − 2).
2. Cancel (x + 3): (x − 3)/(x − 2).

(HT only) Write 2/(x + 1) + 3/(x − 2) as a single fraction.

1. Common denominator (x + 1)(x − 2).
2. [2(x − 2) + 3(x + 1)] / [(x + 1)(x − 2)].
3. (5x − 1) / [(x + 1)(x − 2)].

(HT only) Write x² − 10x + 7 in the form (x + p)² + q.

1. Half of −10 is −5: (x − 5)² = x² − 10x + 25.
2. x² − 10x + 7 = (x − 5)² − 25 + 7 = (x − 5)² − 18.

### Common mistakes

- Writing 3x + 2y = 5xy. Unlike terms cannot be combined.
- Sign errors when a negative multiplies a bracket: −2(x − 4) = −2x + 8.
- Writing (x + 3)² = x² + 9. Always write it as (x + 3)(x + 3) = x² + 6x + 9.
- Not factorising fully: 4x² + 8x = 2x(2x + 4) is not complete; it is 4x(x + 2).
- (HT only) Cancelling terms rather than factors: (x + 3)/(x + 5) cannot be simplified to 3/5.
- (HT only) Forgetting to subtract p² when completing the square.

## A5 Using and rearranging formulae

*Refs: AQA A5 · Edexcel A5 · OCR 6.02b, 6.02c, 6.02d, 6.02e · Eduqas FA5, HA5. Tier: F & H.*

### What you need to know

- Understand and use standard formulae, such as area and volume formulae, and the kinematics formulae:

```latex
v = u + at \qquad s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 \qquad v^2 = u^2 + 2as
```

- Here u is initial velocity, v final velocity, a acceleration, t time and s displacement. These are given on the exam paper when needed.
- Rearrange formulae to change the subject.
- (HT only) Rearrange formulae where the subject appears twice, or where the subject is in a power or a root, by collecting terms and factorising.

### Explained

Changing the subject uses the same balancing steps as solving an equation: do the same to both sides, undoing operations in the reverse order to how they were applied to the new subject.

To deal with a square or root on the subject, undo it last (take the square root or square both sides). When taking a square root, the answer can be ±, but in a formula for a length or time only the positive root makes sense.

(HT only) If the subject appears in two terms, get all those terms on one side, everything else on the other, factorise out the subject, then divide.

### Worked example

Make r the subject of A = πr².

1. Divide by π: r² = A/π.
2. Square root: r = √(A/π).

A car starts at 4 m/s and accelerates at 2.5 m/s² for 6 s. Find its final speed and the distance it travels.

1. v = u + at = 4 + 2.5 × 6 = 19 m/s.
2. s = ut + ½at² = 4 × 6 + 0.5 × 2.5 × 36 = 24 + 45 = 69 m.

Make x the subject of y = (3x − 4)/5.

1. Multiply by 5: 5y = 3x − 4.
2. Add 4: 5y + 4 = 3x.
3. Divide by 3: x = (5y + 4)/3.

(HT only) Make a the subject of 3a + b = ac − 2.

1. Get a-terms on one side: 3a − ac = −2 − b.
2. Factorise: a(3 − c) = −2 − b.
3. Divide: a = (−2 − b)/(3 − c), which can also be written (b + 2)/(c − 3).

### Common mistakes

- Undoing operations in the wrong order.
- Dividing only one term on a side by a number: from 5y = 3x − 4, writing x = 5y/3 − 4.
- (HT only) Leaving the subject on both sides of the final answer.

## A6 Identities, algebraic argument and proof

*Refs: AQA A6 · Edexcel A6 · OCR 6.01a · Eduqas FA6, HA6. Tier: F & H (part Higher only).*

### What you need to know

- Know the difference between an equation and an identity.
- Show that two expressions are equivalent by algebra.
- Use algebra to support and construct arguments.
- (HT only) Construct algebraic proofs, for example about odd, even and consecutive numbers.

### Explained

To show an identity, start with one side and transform it into the other. Do not start by assuming they are equal.

Useful ways to write numbers (n is any integer):

| Number | Algebra |
| --- | --- |
| Even number | 2n |
| Odd number | 2n + 1 |
| Consecutive integers | n, n + 1, n + 2 |
| Consecutive even numbers | 2n, 2n + 2 |
| Consecutive odd numbers | 2n + 1, 2n + 3 |
| Multiple of 5 | 5n |
| Square number | n² |

To prove an expression is a multiple of k, show it can be written as k × (an integer).

A counter-example is one case that shows a statement is false. Testing a few numbers that work is not a proof.

### Worked example

Show that (x + 4)² − (x − 4)² ≡ 16x.

1. (x + 4)² = x² + 8x + 16.
2. (x − 4)² = x² − 8x + 16.
3. Subtract: x² + 8x + 16 − x² + 8x − 16 = 16x. ✓

Show that the statement "n² + n + 41 is always prime" is false.

1. Try n = 41: 41² + 41 + 41 = 41(41 + 1 + 1) = 41 × 43, which is not prime.

(HT only) Prove that the sum of any three consecutive integers is a multiple of 3.

1. Let the integers be n, n + 1, n + 2.
2. Sum = 3n + 3 = 3(n + 1).
3. n + 1 is an integer, so the sum is a multiple of 3.

(HT only) Prove that the difference between the squares of two consecutive odd numbers is a multiple of 8.

1. Let the numbers be 2n + 1 and 2n + 3.
2. (2n + 3)² − (2n + 1)² = (4n² + 12n + 9) − (4n² + 4n + 1) = 8n + 8.
3. 8n + 8 = 8(n + 1), a multiple of 8.

### Common mistakes

- Using the same letter for two different odd numbers: 2n + 1 and 2n + 1 are the same number.
- Checking with examples and calling it a proof.
- Not writing a concluding sentence that links the algebra to the statement.

## A7 Functions

*Refs: AQA A7 · Edexcel A7 · OCR 6.05a · Eduqas FA7, HA7. Tier: F & H (part Higher only).*

### What you need to know

- Interpret simple expressions as functions with inputs and outputs (function machines).
- (HT only) Use function notation: f(x) = 3x − 1 means the function f multiplies the input by 3 and subtracts 1.
- (HT only) The inverse function f⁻¹(x) reverses f.
- (HT only) The composite function fg(x) means apply g first, then f: fg(x) = f(g(x)).

### Explained

A function machine is a chain of operations. To work backwards, reverse each operation and do them in reverse order.

(HT only) To find f⁻¹(x):

1. Write y = f(x).
2. Rearrange to make x the subject.
3. Swap x and y (or simply write the result in terms of x).

(HT only) For fg(x), put the whole expression g(x) into f wherever there is an x. In general fg(x) and gf(x) are different.

### Worked example

A function machine does "× 4, then − 3". The output is 29. Find the input.

1. Reverse: + 3 gives 32, ÷ 4 gives 8.

(HT only) f(x) = 2x + 5 and g(x) = x². Find f(−3), fg(x), gf(x) and f⁻¹(x).

1. f(−3) = 2(−3) + 5 = −1.
2. fg(x) = f(x²) = 2x² + 5.
3. gf(x) = g(2x + 5) = (2x + 5)².
4. y = 2x + 5, so x = (y − 5)/2. f⁻¹(x) = (x − 5)/2.

(HT only) Solve fg(x) = 23 using the functions above.

1. 2x² + 5 = 23, so x² = 9, x = 3 or x = −3.

### Common mistakes

- Doing the operations in the wrong order when reversing a function machine.
- (HT only) Applying f first in fg(x). The function nearest x acts first.
- (HT only) Thinking f⁻¹(x) means 1/f(x).

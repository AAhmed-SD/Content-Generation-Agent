# Ethical, legal, cultural and environmental impacts

This file covers the ethical, legal, cultural, environmental and privacy issues raised by digital technology; personal data, consent and data protection; artificial intelligence, machine learning and robotics; intellectual property (copyright, patents, trademarks, licences, open source, proprietary and Creative Commons); the laws named by the boards; and professional codes of conduct.

## Overview

Questions here are often extended answers: you are given a technology or scenario and asked to discuss its impact. Good answers give both sides, use the scenario, name the right law where one applies, and reach a conclusion.

### Board differences

- AQA 8525: 3.8 (Paper 2). Ethical, legal and environmental impacts and risks, including privacy. Questions draw on cyber security, mobile technologies, wireless networking, cloud storage, hacking, wearable technologies, computer-based implants and autonomous vehicles. General principles only; no named laws. Includes the debate between personal privacy and governments' wish to access data to fight terrorism and crime.
- OCR J277: 1.6.1 (J277/01). Ethical, legal, cultural, environmental and privacy issues. Laws: Data Protection Act 2018, Computer Misuse Act 1990, Copyright Designs and Patents Act 1988. Software licences: open source and proprietary, and recommending one for a scenario.
- Edexcel 1CP2: 5.1 and 5.2 (Paper 1). Environmental issues (energy use, manufacture, replacement cycle, disposal); personal data (privacy, ownership, consent, misuse, data protection); AI, machine learning and robotics (accountability, safety, algorithmic bias, legal liability); intellectual property (copyright, patents, trademarks, licensing). Edexcel does not name specific Acts.
- Eduqas C500QS: section 11 (Component 1). Ethical, legal, cultural, environmental and privacy issues. Laws: GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018, Computer Misuse Act 1990, Copyright Designs and Patents Act 1988, Creative Commons licensing, Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000, Telecommunications Regulations 2000, Freedom of Information Act 2000. Professional standards and codes of conduct.

## Ethical, cultural and privacy issues

*Refs: AQA 3.8 · OCR 1.6.1 · Edexcel 5.2.1 · Eduqas 11: Impacts.*

### What you need to know

- Ethical issues: whether a use of technology is right or wrong. Examples: monitoring employees, automating jobs, collecting data without clear consent, using personal data to target adverts, hacking.
- Cultural issues: how technology changes the way people live, work and relate. Examples: the digital divide (people without devices, internet access or skills are left behind, by income, age or location); changes to work (remote working, job losses and new jobs from automation); social media and wellbeing; online communication replacing face-to-face; the spread of misinformation; global sharing of culture.
- Privacy issues: people's right to control information about themselves. Examples: location tracking by phones and wearables; CCTV and facial recognition; cookies and online tracking; data breaches; smart speakers listening; governments and security services wanting access to private communications versus citizens' right to privacy.
- Personal data (Edexcel): any information that identifies a living person.
  - Ownership: who owns data collected about you, such as your fitness data held by an app company?
  - Consent: people should know and agree to what is collected and why; long terms and conditions mean consent is often not truly informed.
  - Misuse: selling data, using it for something other than the stated purpose, identity theft after breaches.
  - Data protection: keeping it accurate, secure and only as long as needed.

### Worked example

Discuss the impact of wearable fitness trackers (an AQA-style context).

1. Benefits: users monitor health and activity; doctors can spot problems early; motivates exercise.
2. Privacy: they collect location, heart rate and sleep data, which could reveal where someone lives or their health; it may be shared with insurers or advertisers.
3. Security: data stored in the cloud could be leaked in a breach.
4. Legal: as personal (health) data, the company must protect it under data protection law and get consent.
5. Environmental: short replacement cycles add to e-waste.
6. Conclusion: useful, but users should check privacy settings and companies must be clear about data use.

## Artificial intelligence, machine learning and robotics (Edexcel)

*Refs: Edexcel 5.2.2.*

### What you need to know

- AI: computer systems doing tasks that normally need human intelligence (recognising speech or images, making decisions). Machine learning: systems that improve by finding patterns in large amounts of training data, rather than following rules written by a programmer. Robotics: machines that carry out physical tasks, often with sensors and AI.
- Accountability: when an AI system makes a decision (rejecting a loan, flagging a patient), who is responsible: the developer, the company using it, or the user? Decisions made by machine learning can be hard to explain.
- Safety: autonomous vehicles, medical robots and industrial robots must not harm people; they must be tested for situations they were not trained on.
- Algorithmic bias: if the training data is biased (for example mostly from one group of people), the system's decisions can be unfair to others, such as facial recognition that works worse on some skin tones, or recruitment tools favouring one gender.
- Legal liability: the law must decide who pays damages when an AI or robot causes harm, for example in a crash involving a self-driving car.

### Common mistakes

- Saying "the AI is responsible". Machines cannot be held legally responsible; people and companies are.

## Environmental issues

*Refs: AQA 3.8 · OCR 1.6.1 · Edexcel 5.1.1 · Eduqas 11: Impacts.*

### What you need to know

- Energy consumption: devices, and especially data centres running cloud services, streaming and AI, use large amounts of electricity, much still made from fossil fuels; they also need cooling.
- Manufacture: uses scarce raw materials (rare earth metals, cobalt, gold) mined with environmental damage and sometimes poor working conditions; manufacturing uses energy and water.
- Replacement cycle: people replace phones and laptops every few years for new features, or because software stops being supported, creating more waste and more manufacturing.
- Disposal: e-waste contains toxic substances (lead, mercury, cadmium) that can leak into soil and water if sent to landfill; it is often shipped to poorer countries and broken up unsafely. It also contains valuable materials that could be recycled.
- Positive impacts: technology can reduce travel (video calls, remote working), cut paper use, monitor the environment, and make systems such as heating and transport more efficient (smart meters, route planning).
- Reducing the impact: keep devices longer; repair and refurbish; recycle properly; use energy-efficient hardware and renewable-powered data centres; turn devices off.

## Intellectual property and software licences

*Refs: OCR 1.6.1 · Edexcel 5.2.3 · Eduqas 11: Legislation.*

### What you need to know

- Intellectual property (IP): creations of the mind (software, music, designs, inventions, brand names) that can be owned.
- Copyright: protects original creative works (including program code, images, music, text) automatically when they are created. Others may not copy, share, adapt or sell them without permission.
- Patent: protects an invention (how something works, such as a new technical process) for a limited time, but must be applied for and granted. In the UK, software as such is generally not patentable, but some inventions involving software are.
- Trademark: protects a brand's name, logo or slogan, so others cannot use it to pass off their products. Registered with ®.
- Licensing: the owner grants others permission to use their work under set conditions.
- Proprietary (closed source) software: the source code is not available; users buy a licence to use it, usually with restrictions (number of devices, no copying or modifying). Often comes with support and regular updates. Examples: most commercial office suites and games.
- Open source software: the source code is available; users may use, study, change and share it (subject to the licence, which may require changes to be shared too). Usually free; supported by a community. Examples: Linux, Firefox, LibreOffice, Python.
- Creative Commons (Eduqas): licences that let creators allow others to reuse their work freely under chosen conditions: Attribution (credit the creator), ShareAlike (share adaptations under the same licence), NonCommercial (no commercial use), NoDerivatives (no changes).

| | Open source | Proprietary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source code | Available, can be changed | Not available |
| Cost | Usually free | Usually paid |
| Support | Community, forums | Company support |
| Customisation | Can be adapted | Cannot be changed |
| Risk | Quality and updates vary | Locked to one company |

### Worked example

Recommend a licence: a small charity needs a word processor for 20 volunteers and has no budget; it has a volunteer who can install software.

1. Open source: free, so no licence cost for 20 users.
2. Its community provides updates and help; the volunteer can install it on all machines.
3. A drawback is no guaranteed professional support, but basic word processing needs little support.

## Legislation

*Refs: OCR 1.6.1 · Eduqas 11: Legislation · Edexcel 5.2.1, 5.2.3.*

### What you need to know

- Data Protection Act 2018 and UK GDPR (OCR, Eduqas): control how organisations collect, store and use personal data. Personal data must be:
  1. Used fairly, lawfully and transparently.
  2. Collected for specified, explicit purposes and not used for others.
  3. Adequate, relevant and limited to what is needed.
  4. Accurate and kept up to date.
  5. Kept no longer than necessary.
  6. Kept secure (protected against unauthorised access, loss or damage).
  The organisation (data controller) must be able to show it complies (accountability). People (data subjects) have rights, including to see the data held about them, have it corrected, have it erased in some cases, and object to its use. The Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) enforces the law and can fine organisations.
- Computer Misuse Act 1990 (OCR, Eduqas): makes hacking and malware crimes. Main offences:
  1. Unauthorised access to computer material (for example using someone's login without permission).
  2. Unauthorised access with intent to commit or help with further crimes (for example hacking in to steal money).
  3. Unauthorised acts with intent to impair the operation of a computer, or recklessness about doing so (for example spreading malware, deleting data, DoS attacks).
  Later amendments added offences for making or supplying tools for these crimes, and for causing serious damage.
- Copyright Designs and Patents Act 1988 (OCR, Eduqas): protects the creators of original work, including software, from having it copied, shared, adapted or sold without permission. Illegal acts include copying software, sharing music or films online, and using images without permission.
- Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000 (RIPA; Eduqas): sets out when and how public bodies such as the police and security services may carry out surveillance and intercept communications (usually needing a warrant or authorisation), so investigations are lawful and proportionate. It can require communication providers to give access to data, and people to hand over keys to decrypt encrypted data. Parts of it have since been replaced by the Investigatory Powers Act 2016, but the spec names RIPA.
- Telecommunications Regulations 2000 (Eduqas; the Lawful Business Practice regulations): allowed businesses to monitor and record communications on their own systems (emails, calls, internet use) without consent for set reasons, such as checking for crime, misuse or compliance, provided they made reasonable efforts to tell users the monitoring may happen. (They were later replaced by similar 2018 regulations.)
- Freedom of Information Act 2000 (Eduqas): gives anyone the right to request information held by public authorities (government departments, councils, schools, the NHS, police). The authority must normally reply within 20 working days, unless an exemption applies (for example personal data, national security, or if it would cost too much).

### Worked example

Name the law broken in each case.

1. A student guesses a teacher's password and changes their own marks: Computer Misuse Act 1990 (unauthorised access, and with intent to commit a further offence / to alter data).
2. A website shares customers' email addresses with a marketing firm without consent: Data Protection Act 2018 / UK GDPR.
3. Someone uploads a paid game for others to download free: Copyright Designs and Patents Act 1988.
4. (Eduqas) A journalist asks a council how much it spent on consultants: a request under the Freedom of Information Act 2000.

### Common mistakes

- Writing "Data Protection Act" for hacking. Breaking in is Computer Misuse; handling personal data badly is Data Protection.
- Saying copyright must be applied for. It is automatic; patents must be applied for.

## Professional standards (Eduqas)

*Refs: Eduqas 11: Professional standards.*

### What you need to know

- Computing professionals follow codes of conduct and ethics, formal (published by professional bodies such as BCS, The Chartered Institute for IT, or the ACM) and informal (accepted good practice in a workplace).
- Typical principles: act in the public interest (protect health, safety, privacy and the environment); work only within your competence and keep skills up to date; be honest about the limits of your work; respect the law and others' intellectual property; keep information confidential; do not discriminate; take responsibility for your work and report problems.
- Why they matter: they protect the public and users, maintain trust in the profession, set standards employers and clients can rely on, and guide decisions where the law is unclear.

## Mark scheme

1. Any two: more e-waste/toxic waste in landfill (1); more raw materials mined (1); more energy used in manufacture (1).
2. E.g. it invades employees' privacy (1), but the employer may need to check for misuse or protect data, so it should be proportionate and staff should be told (1).
3. The gap between people who have access to digital technology and the internet (and skills) and those who do not (1); cause e.g. cost, rural location without broadband, age/lack of skills (1).
4. Any three of the six principles, e.g. used fairly and lawfully; used only for the stated purpose; adequate and limited to what is needed; accurate and up to date; kept no longer than necessary; kept secure (3).
5. Any two: unauthorised access to computer material (1); unauthorised access with intent to commit further offences (1); unauthorised acts to impair a computer, e.g. spreading malware (1).
6. Open source: source code is available and can be modified and shared, usually free (1). Proprietary: source code is not available; a licence is bought with restrictions on use (1).
7. Unfair results caused by biased training data or design (1); e.g. facial recognition less accurate for some groups / recruitment tool favouring one gender (1).
8. Copyright protects original creative works such as code, music or text from being copied (1); a trademark protects a brand name, logo or slogan from being used by others (1).
9. Gives the public the right to request information (1) held by public authorities (1).
10. Any two: protects the public/users (e.g. their safety and privacy) (1); maintains trust in the profession (1); guides decisions where the law is unclear (1); ensures work is within competence and to a high standard (1).
11. Levels-based. 5–6: balanced discussion across ethical (who the car protects in an unavoidable crash; job losses for drivers; safety benefits from fewer human errors), legal (liability for crashes: maker, software developer or owner; insurance; laws needing updating) and privacy (location and journey data collected, shared or hacked; camera recordings of the public), using examples and reaching a conclusion. 3–4: several relevant points with some explanation, may be one-sided. 1–2: simple points listed.

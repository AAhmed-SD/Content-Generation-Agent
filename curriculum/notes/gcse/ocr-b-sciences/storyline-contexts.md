# OCR B storyline contexts

OCR's Twenty First Century Science courses teach each chapter as a story built around a question, such as "How can we identify the cause of an infection?" or "What happens to products at the end of their useful life?". Most of the science is the same as in the AQA notes, but a few outcomes use contexts that AQA does not cover. This file covers only those: genetic testing, how infections are identified, ways of cutting air pollution, PET bottle recycling and recycling decisions, representative sampling, qualitative and quantitative analysis, and judging the quality of titration data.

## Genetic testing and the human genome in medicine

*Refs: OCR B J257/J260 B1.3.1.*

### What you need to know

- Sequencing the human genome lets scientists find **alleles** (gene variants) linked to diseases.
- **Genetic testing** looks for particular alleles in a person's DNA, from a sample of blood, saliva or cells.
- Testing can inform **family planning** (for example, whether a couple could pass on an inherited disorder) and **healthcare** (for example, choosing the medicine most likely to work for that person).
- Discuss benefits, risks and ethical issues.

### Explained

**Uses of genetic testing.**

- *Carrier testing*: a person with no symptoms finds out whether they carry one copy of a recessive allele, such as the allele for cystic fibrosis. If both partners are carriers, each child has a 1 in 4 chance of having the disorder.
- *Embryo and fetal testing*: cells from an embryo made by IVF, or from a fetus, are tested (embryo screening is in Biology 4.6.1.7).
- *Diagnostic testing*: confirms a diagnosis in someone who already has symptoms.
- *Predictive testing*: shows whether a person has an allele that raises their risk of a disease later in life, such as some alleles linked to breast cancer, so they can have more frequent check-ups or preventive treatment.
- *Testing before prescribing*: some alleles change how a person's body breaks down a medicine, so a test can show which drug or dose will work best and which may cause side effects.

**Limits.** Most diseases depend on many genes and on lifestyle and environment. Finding a risk allele usually raises the chance of a disease; it does not make it certain. Tests can also give false positives and false negatives.

**Issues to discuss.**

- Benefits: early treatment or monitoring; informed choices about having children; better-targeted medicines.
- Risks and concerns: anxiety from a result that may never lead to illness; information about relatives who did not choose to be tested; possible pressure to end a pregnancy; whether insurers or employers could use the results; who should store and see genetic data.

### Common mistakes

- Saying a genetic test shows that a person "will get" a disease. Usually it shows a changed risk.
- Forgetting that a test result also gives information about the person's parents, siblings and children.

## Identifying the cause of an infection

*Refs: OCR B J257 B2.4.1 (separate science only).*

### What you need to know

- Describe ways diseases, including plant diseases, are detected and identified, in the lab and in the field.
- Describe how to use a light microscope to observe microorganisms.

### Explained

To choose a treatment, doctors and plant scientists must know which pathogen is causing a disease. Methods include:

- **Observing symptoms**: many diseases have typical signs (a rash, a fever pattern, mosaic patterns on leaves, galls on stems). In the field, plant growers compare symptoms with guides and photographs.
- **Taking samples**: blood, urine, swabs, tissue, or (for plants) infected leaves, stems or soil.
- **Cell counts**: a raised number of white blood cells suggests an infection.
- **Culturing**: the sample is spread on agar so the pathogen grows into colonies that can be studied (aseptic technique is in Biology 4.1.1.6).
- **Microscopy and staining**: stains make bacteria visible and show their shape; a common stain sorts bacteria into two groups by their cell walls.
- **Testing with antimicrobials**: paper discs soaked in different antibiotics are placed on a culture. A clear zone shows the antibiotic kills or stops the bacterium; the bigger the zone, the more effective it is. This also shows which antibiotic to prescribe.
- **Genome analysis**: DNA from the sample is compared with known pathogen DNA. This identifies the pathogen quickly and exactly, even ones that are hard to grow.
- **Antibody-based tests**: testing kits use monoclonal antibodies that bind only to one pathogen (in Biology 4.3.2.2 and 4.3.3.1); some work in the field.
- **Isolation and reinfection**: the suspected pathogen is grown on its own (isolated), then used to infect a healthy organism (usually a plant or a culture of cells); if the same disease appears, that pathogen is the cause.

**Observing microorganisms with a light microscope.**

1. Using aseptic technique, place a small drop of culture on a clean slide and spread it thin.
2. Let it dry, then pass the slide briefly through a flame to fix the cells to the glass (this also kills them).
3. Add a stain (such as methylene blue) for about a minute, then rinse gently and blot dry.
4. Start with the lowest-power objective, focus with the coarse focus, then move to the highest power and use only the fine focus.

Bacteria are only about 1–5 µm long, so they appear as very small dots, rods or spirals even at the highest power. Viruses are far too small to see with a light microscope.

### Common mistakes

- Saying viruses can be seen with a school light microscope.
- Saying a bigger clear zone means the bacteria are more resistant. It means the antibiotic is more effective (the bacteria are less resistant).
- Using the coarse focus at high power, which can crack the slide.

## Reducing air pollution from fuels

*Refs: OCR B J258/J260 C1.1.8.*

### What you need to know

- The problems caused by carbon monoxide, particulates, sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen are in Chemistry 4.9.3.2.
- Describe ways to cut these emissions, including **catalytic converters**, **low-sulfur petrol** (and diesel) and **gas scrubbers**.

### Explained

| Method | How it works | Pollutants reduced |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Catalytic converter | Exhaust gases pass over a hot honeycomb coated with metal catalysts (platinum, palladium, rhodium). Carbon monoxide is oxidised to carbon dioxide; oxides of nitrogen are reduced to nitrogen; unburnt hydrocarbons are oxidised to carbon dioxide and water. | CO, NOx, unburnt hydrocarbons |
| Low-sulfur fuel | Sulfur compounds are removed from petrol and diesel at the refinery, so little sulfur dioxide forms when the fuel burns. | SO₂ |
| Gas scrubber (flue gas desulfurisation) | In power station chimneys, waste gases are sprayed with a slurry of calcium carbonate or calcium oxide (an alkali/base), which reacts with acidic sulfur dioxide to form a solid calcium salt (which can be made into gypsum for plasterboard). | SO₂ |
| Particulate filter | A filter in the exhaust of diesel vehicles traps soot particles, which are burned off from time to time. | Particulates |

Typical catalytic converter reactions:

- 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂
- 2NO + 2CO → N₂ + 2CO₂

Other approaches: better engine design and more complete combustion (more oxygen, less carbon monoxide and soot), using less fuel (public transport, efficient vehicles), and switching to electric vehicles and renewable electricity, which move or remove the emissions.

### Common mistakes

- Saying catalytic converters remove carbon dioxide. They make more carbon dioxide; they remove CO, NOx and hydrocarbons.
- Saying low-sulfur fuel reduces oxides of nitrogen. NOx comes from nitrogen in the air reacting at high temperatures in the engine, not from the fuel.

## PET bottles and recycling decisions

*Refs: OCR B J258 C4.5.6, C4.5.7; J260 C4.4.5, C4.4.6.*

### What you need to know

- Describe how PET drinks bottles are reused and recycled for other uses, and explain why this is **viable** (practical and worth doing).
- Evaluate factors that affect decisions to recycle products made from crude oil (plastics) and from metal ores.
- Life cycle assessment and the general idea of reducing, reusing and recycling are in Chemistry 4.10.2.

### Explained

PET (poly(ethylene terephthalate)) is a polyester used for most clear drinks bottles. It is a **thermosoftening** plastic: it softens when heated and can be remoulded.

**Recycling process.**

1. Bottles are collected and sorted from other plastics (PET has recycling code 1; machines sort by how the plastic reflects infrared light) and by colour.
2. Caps and labels are removed, and the bottles are washed.
3. They are shredded into flakes, which are washed again and dried.
4. The flakes are melted and formed into pellets.
5. Pellets are made into new bottles, or into fibres for fleece clothing, carpets and filling, or into food trays and packaging straps.

**Why PET recycling is viable.**

- It is thermosoftening, so it can be melted and reshaped.
- Drinks bottles are made of one plastic, are used in huge numbers and are easy to identify, so collection gives a large, fairly pure supply.
- Recycled PET needs much less energy than making new PET from crude oil, and saves a finite resource.
- It reduces plastic in landfill and litter.

Its quality falls a little each time it is melted, so it cannot be recycled for ever; it is often "downcycled" into fibres.

**Factors in recycling decisions.**

| Factor | Plastics (from crude oil) | Metals (from ores) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Energy saved | Some, compared with making new plastic | Large: recycled aluminium needs about 5% of the energy of extracting new aluminium by electrolysis |
| Ease of sorting | Hard: many types look alike and mixed plastics are hard to separate | Iron and steel are separated with magnets; aluminium by other methods |
| Quality after recycling | Falls with each cycle | Most metals can be recycled many times with no loss of quality |
| Value of the material | Usually low, so recycling may not pay | Often high (aluminium, copper), so recycling pays |
| Resource saved | Crude oil (finite) | Ore (finite); less mining, quarrying and waste rock |

Other factors: the energy and cost of collecting and transporting waste; whether there is a market for the recycled product; local facilities; and how much material is contaminated with food or mixed with other materials.

### Common mistakes

- Saying all plastics can be recycled like PET. Thermosetting plastics cannot be melted and remoulded.
- Saying recycling always saves energy. The energy for collection, transport and sorting has to be included.

## Sampling and qualitative or quantitative analysis

*Refs: OCR B J258 C5.2.1, C5.4.1 (both separate science only).*

### What you need to know

- A **representative sample** has the same composition as the whole material being tested.
- **Qualitative analysis** finds out which substances are present.
- **Quantitative analysis** finds out how much of a substance is present.

### Explained

Analysts test only a small portion of a material, so the portion must truly represent the whole. For a lake, samples are taken at several places and depths; for a shipment of soil or ore, small samples are taken from many points and mixed. A sample taken only from the surface, or only from one corner, might give the wrong answer.

The sample must also stay unchanged between collection and testing: containers must be clean, sealed and labelled, and stored to stop reactions or evaporation.

Examples:

- Qualitative: flame tests and precipitate tests to identify ions (Chemistry 4.8.3); chromatography to see whether a food colouring contains a banned dye.
- Quantitative: a titration to find the concentration of an acid (Chemistry 4.4.2.5); measuring the mass of a precipitate; an instrument that measures the concentration of a metal ion.

### Common mistakes

- Thinking a single sample from one place is enough for a large or mixed material.
- Calling a flame test quantitative. It shows which ion is present, not how much.

## Judging the quality of titration data

*Refs: OCR B J258 C5.4.8; J260 C5.3.7.*

### What you need to know

- The titration method is in Chemistry 4.4.2.5 and the [OCR practical activity groups](../ocr-a-sciences/practical-activity-groups.md) (titration).
- Evaluate a set of titration results: identify the rough titre, concordant titres and outliers; decide which to use in the mean; identify sources of error and say how they affect the result.

### Explained

- **Concordant** results agree closely, usually within 0.10 cm³. Only concordant titres are used for the mean. The rough (first) titre usually overshoots the end point, so it is left out.
- Results that are close together are **precise**. They are **accurate** if they are close to the true value, which needs correctly calibrated equipment and good technique.
- A burette is read to the nearest 0.05 cm³. Each titre uses two readings (start and end), so it has an uncertainty of about ±0.1 cm³.

Common sources of error and their effect:

| Problem | Effect on the titre |
| --- | --- |
| Air bubble in the burette tip that comes out during the titration | Titre appears larger than it really is |
| Burette rinsed with water, not the acid it is filled with | Acid is diluted, so more is needed: titre too large |
| Overshooting the end point (adding acid too fast near the end) | Titre too large |
| Reading the burette from above or below eye level (parallax) | Reading too high or too low |
| Pipette not filled exactly to the line | Wrong volume of alkali: titre too large or too small |
| Indicator added in large amounts | Indicator can react slightly and shift the end point |

To improve: rinse equipment with the right solutions, remove air bubbles before starting, add acid drop by drop near the end point while swirling, read at eye level against a white background, and repeat until there are at least two concordant results.

### Worked example

A student records titres of 25.40, 24.70, 24.65, 24.95 and 24.70 cm³ (the first is rough). The concordant titres within 0.10 cm³ are 24.70, 24.65 and 24.70. Mean = (24.70 + 24.65 + 24.70) ÷ 3 = 24.68 cm³. The 24.95 cm³ titre is not concordant and is left out, probably because the end point was overshot.

### Common mistakes

- Including the rough titre in the mean.
- Averaging all results, including ones that do not agree.
- Saying an air bubble makes the titre smaller. When the bubble leaves the tip, the reading falls without acid reaching the flask, so the recorded titre is too big.

# Ideas about Science

OCR's Twenty First Century Science specifications have a chapter called Ideas about Science (IaS). It is the same in Biology B, Chemistry B, Physics B and Combined Science B. It covers how scientists plan investigations, handle data, develop explanations, and how science affects society. There is no separate paper: questions on these ideas are set in the context of any topic, so practise them with real examples from every chapter.

## Overview

The four groups are:

- IaS1: what to think about when planning an investigation (8 outcomes)
- IaS2: what conclusions can be drawn from data (12 outcomes)
- IaS3: how scientific explanations are developed (4 outcomes)
- IaS4: how science and technology affect society (6 outcomes).

### Board differences

- Only OCR B has an Ideas about Science chapter. OCR Gateway and AQA examine the same skills as "Working scientifically", listed separately from the subject content, so their maps do not list them as spec points.
- In J260 the IaS2 heading is worded slightly differently from J257 to J259; the outcomes are the same.

## IaS1 Planning an investigation

*Refs: OCR B J257/J258/J259/J260 IaS1.1 to IaS1.8.*

### What you need to know

- Use a scientific theory to write a **hypothesis** (a testable explanation) and a **prediction** (what you expect to observe if the hypothesis is right), and justify them.
- Choose apparatus and techniques that give **accurate**, **precise** and **valid** data, and explain the choice.
- Know the scientific quantities you are measuring and how each is found (for example, rate = change ÷ time; density = mass ÷ volume).
- Identify the **independent** variable (changed), the **dependent** variable (measured) and the **control** variables (kept the same), and say how each control variable is kept the same.
- Choose a sensible **sample size** and **range** and number of values, and justify them.
- Plan a clear, ordered method to make observations, make or identify a substance, test a hypothesis, collect and check data, or explore something new.
- Identify **hazards** and say how to reduce the risk.
- Communicate the plan with correct terms, diagrams, tables, graphs, numbers and symbols.

### Explained

**Hypothesis and prediction.** A hypothesis explains; a prediction describes an outcome. Example: *Hypothesis*: enzymes work faster when warmer because particles collide more often with more energy, until the enzyme denatures. *Prediction*: the time for starch to disappear will fall as temperature rises from 20 °C to 40 °C, then rise again above about 50 °C.

**Key words about data quality.**

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Accurate | Close to the true value |
| Precise | Repeated measurements are close together (little spread) |
| Repeatable | The same person, method and equipment give similar results |
| Reproducible | A different person, method or equipment gives similar results |
| Valid | The method measures what it claims to, and only the independent variable affects the result |
| Resolution | The smallest change an instrument can show |

**Choosing apparatus.** A measuring cylinder with 1 cm³ divisions is fine for 50 cm³ of acid in a rate experiment, but a burette or pipette is needed in a titration. A gas syringe gives more precise gas volumes than counting bubbles. A digital thermometer reading to 0.1 °C is better than a liquid thermometer reading to 1 °C if temperature changes are small.

**Controlling variables.** Say how, not just what: "keep temperature the same by using a water bath at 30 °C", "use the same mass of magnesium ribbon by weighing 0.10 g on a balance".

**Sample size and range.** Bigger samples reduce the effect of random variation and unusual individuals, but cost more time and money. In biology, a sample must be big enough to be representative (for example at least 10 quadrats). A range should cover enough values to show the pattern, with at least five values, and include extra readings where the graph changes most.

**Hazard and risk.** A hazard is something that could cause harm (concentrated acid, a Bunsen flame, a hot ray box). The risk is the chance of harm happening and how bad it would be. Reduce risk with eye protection, smaller volumes, lower concentrations, heat-proof mats, tied-back hair, and by handling microorganisms with aseptic technique.

### Common mistakes

- Writing a prediction with no reason, when the question asks you to justify it using a theory.
- Saying "a fair test" without naming which variables are controlled and how.
- Mixing up accurate and precise. A set of readings can be precise (close together) but inaccurate (all wrong in the same way, such as a balance that was not zeroed).
- Listing hazards without saying how to reduce the risk.

## IaS2 Processing, presenting and evaluating data

*Refs: OCR B J257/J258/J259/J260 IaS2.1 to IaS2.12.*

### What you need to know

- Present data in suitable tables (independent variable in the first column, units in the headings) and charts.
- Use SI units and IUPAC chemical names; use prefixes (tera, giga, mega, kilo, centi, milli, micro, nano) and powers of ten.
- Convert data between forms (table to graph, graph to words) and between units.
- Give answers to a sensible number of **significant figures** (no more than the data).
- Choose the right graph, label axes with quantity and unit, use sensible scales, plot accurately, draw a line or curve of best fit, and show uncertainty with **range bars**.
- Find patterns and trends, calculate the **mean** and **range**, read values from a graph, find a **gradient**, and use **interpolation** and **extrapolation**.
- Judge data for accuracy, precision, repeatability and reproducibility; spot **random** and **systematic errors**; decide whether to keep or reject an **outlier**.
- Evaluate a method, suggest improvements, and explain how they improve the data; suggest further investigations.
- Draw conclusions from data, using correct scientific terms, and explain how far the data support or contradict a hypothesis.

### Explained

**Prefixes.**

| Prefix | Symbol | Factor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| tera | T | 10¹² |
| giga | G | 10⁹ |
| mega | M | 10⁶ |
| kilo | k | 10³ |
| centi | c | 10⁻² |
| milli | m | 10⁻³ |
| micro | µ | 10⁻⁶ |
| nano | n | 10⁻⁹ |

**Choosing a graph.** Bar chart when the independent variable is categoric (types of metal, blood groups). Line graph or scatter graph when both variables are continuous (temperature, time). Histogram for grouped continuous data (heights of plants in classes).

**Mean, range and outliers.** The mean is the total divided by the number of values. The range is the largest minus the smallest value; range bars on a graph show the spread of repeats. An **outlier** is a value that does not fit the pattern. Check it first: if there is a known reason (a misread scale, a leak), leave it out of the mean and say why. If there is no reason, it may be real, so repeat the measurement rather than just discard it.

**Errors.**

- *Random errors* make readings scatter unpredictably around the true value (for example, reaction time when using a stopwatch). Reduce their effect by repeating and taking a mean.
- *Systematic errors* shift all readings the same way (a balance reading 0.2 g with nothing on it; always reading a meniscus from above). Repeats do not help; fix the cause or correct the readings.
- A *zero error* is a systematic error where an instrument does not read zero when it should.

**Graphs.** The gradient of a straight line = change in y ÷ change in x, with units (for a distance–time graph the gradient is speed in m/s). For a curve, draw a tangent and find its gradient. Interpolation reads between plotted points; extrapolation extends the line beyond them, which is less reliable because the pattern may change.

**Evaluating and concluding.** A conclusion says what the pattern is and explains it with science; it must not go beyond the range tested. Confidence in a hypothesis increases when results match its prediction, are repeatable and reproducible, and have little spread. One set of results that agrees cannot prove a hypothesis; a reliable result that disagrees can show that it needs changing.

### Worked example

A student times how long magnesium takes to react with acid at five temperatures. At 40 °C the repeats are 38 s, 41 s and 59 s.

- The 59 s reading is an outlier. If no reason is known, repeat the measurement. Here the student found the ribbon was folded, so it is excluded.
- Mean = (38 + 41) ÷ 2 = 39.5 s, given as 40 s (2 significant figures, as in the data).
- Range of the remaining repeats = 41 − 38 = 3 s.

Unit conversion: 250 cm³ = 0.25 dm³ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ m³. A cell 20 µm across is 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ m.

Gradient: a straight line on a distance–time graph passes through (2 s, 6 m) and (8 s, 24 m). Gradient = (24 − 6) ÷ (8 − 2) = 18 ÷ 6 = 3 m/s.

### Common mistakes

- Giving a mean to more significant figures than the data, such as 39.6667 s.
- Joining points dot-to-dot instead of drawing a smooth line or curve of best fit.
- Putting units in every cell of a table instead of in the column heading.
- Saying "repeat to make it more accurate". Repeating improves precision and helps spot outliers; it does not remove systematic error.
- Extrapolating far beyond the data as if it were certain.

## IaS3 Developing scientific explanations

*Refs: OCR B J257/J258/J259/J260 IaS3.1 to IaS3.4.*

### What you need to know

- Spot a **correlation** in text, a table or a graph, and tell the difference between a correlation and a cause.
- Explain why a factor can increase the chance of an outcome without always causing it.
- Explain why one person's case is not good evidence for or against a correlation.
- Know that a correlation is more likely to be a cause when there is a plausible **mechanism** that explains it.
- Describe examples of scientific theories that changed when new evidence appeared.
- Describe **peer review**: other scientists in the field check work before it is published.
- Use models (representational, spatial, descriptive, computational, mathematical) to solve problems, make predictions and explain ideas, and identify their limitations.

### Explained

**Correlation and cause.** A correlation means two variables change together. A cause means changing one actually changes the other. Ice cream sales and sunburn are correlated, but ice cream does not cause sunburn; both are caused by sunny weather (a third factor). Smoking and lung cancer are correlated, and smoking is accepted as a cause because large studies controlled for other factors and a mechanism was found: chemicals in smoke damage DNA in lung cells.

**Risk factors.** A risk factor increases the chance of an outcome but does not make it certain. Many smokers never get lung cancer, and some non-smokers do. One case ("my grandfather smoked and lived to 95") does not disprove the correlation, because the evidence is about large groups.

**Theories change.** Examples from the chapters:

- Models of the atom: Dalton's solid spheres, Thomson's plum pudding, Rutherford's nuclear model (after the alpha-scattering results), Bohr's electron shells.
- Mendeleev's periodic table, accepted when the elements he predicted were discovered.
- Germ theory replacing the idea that disease came from bad air.
- Darwin's natural selection, later supported by genetics.

**Peer review.** Before a paper is published in a journal, other experts check the method, data and conclusions. They may ask for changes or reject it. Peer review reduces errors and fraud but does not guarantee a result is right; other scientists still need to repeat the work.

**Models.** Every model simplifies. The particle model explains states of matter but treats particles as solid spheres; ball-and-stick models show bonds but not their real lengths; a computer climate model can predict but depends on its starting data and assumptions. Saying what a model leaves out is part of using it well.

### Common mistakes

- Concluding "A causes B" from a correlation alone.
- Saying peer review proves a claim is true.
- Describing a model without giving any limitation when the question asks for one.

## IaS4 Science, technology and society

*Refs: OCR B J257/J258/J259/J260 IaS4.1 to IaS4.6.*

### What you need to know

- Give examples of science that has greatly improved people's lives, and of risks that new technology has caused.
- For a situation, identify who benefits and who takes the risks, discuss what should be done, explain why people accept some risks, and tell the difference between **perceived risk** and **calculated risk**.
- Explain why different people, places or countries may sensibly make different decisions on the same issue (personal, social, economic and environmental reasons), and make a decision based on evidence.
- Tell the difference between questions science can answer and questions it cannot. Where an ethical issue is involved, state it clearly and summarise the different views.
- Explain why scientists communicate their work to different audiences.

### Explained

**Benefits and risks.** Vaccines, antibiotics, clean water treatment, electricity and fertilisers have transformed health and food supply. New technologies also brought risks: CFCs damaged the ozone layer, overuse of antibiotics led to resistant bacteria, burning fossil fuels is changing the climate.

**Calculated and perceived risk.** Calculated risk comes from data: the number of times something happened divided by the number of times it could have happened (for example deaths per million journeys). Perceived risk is how risky people feel something is. People usually feel risks are bigger when they are new, unfamiliar, imposed on them, or could cause a disaster (nuclear power, flying), and smaller when they are familiar, voluntary or give a benefit they value (driving, sunbathing).

**Different decisions.** A country with plenty of sunshine and space may choose large solar farms; another may choose wind or nuclear power. A wealthy country may ban a cheap pesticide that a poorer country still needs to protect its food supply. Both decisions can be sensible in their own context.

**What science can and cannot answer.** Science can answer "Does this gene variant increase the risk of a disease?" It cannot decide "Should parents be allowed to choose embryos without that variant?", because that depends on values. For an ethical question, say what the issue is (for example, the rights of an embryo against the wish to prevent suffering) and give the main views on each side.

**Communicating science.** Scientists share results so other scientists can check and build on them (journals, conferences), so the public and decision-makers can make informed choices (news, reports), and because much research is publicly funded. The language and detail are changed to suit the audience.

### Worked example

In a year, 1 800 people out of a town of 600 000 were injured cycling. The calculated risk = 1 800 ÷ 600 000 = 0.003, or 3 in 1 000 people per year. If most people in the town believe cycling is far more dangerous than travelling by car, their perceived risk does not match the data, which is something to check before making a decision.

### Common mistakes

- Giving only benefits or only risks when asked to evaluate.
- Treating an ethical question as if an experiment could settle it.
- Saying a decision is "wrong" because another country chose differently, without considering its context.

## Check questions

1. A student investigates how the concentration of sugar solution affects the change in mass of potato cylinders. Name the independent variable, the dependent variable and two control variables. [4 marks]
2. Explain the difference between precise and accurate results. [2 marks]
3. Convert 0.045 kg to grams, and 3.2 mm to micrometres. [2 marks]
4. Repeat readings of a reaction time are 0.31 s, 0.28 s, 0.30 s and 0.52 s. Identify the outlier, then calculate the mean and the range of the other readings. [3 marks]
5. A balance reads 0.3 g when nothing is on it. Name this type of error and explain why repeating the readings would not remove it. [3 marks]
6. Data show that people who carry a lighter are more likely to get lung cancer. Explain why this does not show that lighters cause lung cancer. [2 marks]
7. Describe what happens in peer review and give one reason it is useful. [3 marks]
8. Give one strength and one limitation of the particle model of matter. [2 marks]
9. A new medicine reduces the chance of a heart attack but has rare serious side effects. Suggest why some patients accept the risk and others do not. [3 marks]
10. Give one question about genetic testing that science can answer and one that it cannot, and explain why. [3 marks]

## Mark scheme

1. Independent: concentration of sugar solution (1). Dependent: change in mass of the cylinders (1). Any two controls: length / diameter / starting mass of cylinders; volume of solution; time left in solution; temperature; type of potato (2).
2. Precise: repeat readings are close to each other / small spread (1). Accurate: close to the true value (1).
3. 45 g (1); 3200 µm (1).
4. 0.52 s is the outlier (1). Mean = (0.31 + 0.28 + 0.30) ÷ 3 = 0.30 s (1). Range = 0.31 − 0.28 = 0.03 s (1).
5. Zero error / systematic error (1). Every reading is 0.3 g too high (1); the mean of repeats is still 0.3 g too high, so the balance must be zeroed or 0.3 g subtracted (1).
6. Correlation does not show cause (1); a third factor (smoking) explains both, as smokers carry lighters (1).
7. Before publication, other experts in the same field (1) check the methods, data and conclusions (1). Useful because it finds errors / reduces fraud / makes published work more trustworthy (1).
8. Strength: explains changes of state / density / gas pressure / diffusion (1). Limitation: treats particles as solid spheres / ignores forces between particles / does not show spaces to scale (1).
9. Any three: patients at high risk of a heart attack gain a larger benefit; the side effect is rare (low calculated risk); people differ in how they perceive risk; personal experience (for example, a relative had a heart attack); some prefer lifestyle changes.
10. Can answer: whether a test correctly detects a gene variant / how likely a variant is to cause a disease (1). Cannot answer: whether people should be tested / whether embryos should be selected (1). Because that depends on values and ethics, not on evidence that can be measured (1).

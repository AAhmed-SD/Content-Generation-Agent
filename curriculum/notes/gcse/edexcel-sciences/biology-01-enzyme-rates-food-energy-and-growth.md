# Enzyme rates, food energy, growth charts and DNA extraction (Edexcel gap file)

This file adds the Edexcel content that the AQA GCSE Biology notes do not teach. Enzymes, food tests, mitosis and DNA structure themselves are in the AQA notes; see [map-1BI0.md](map-1BI0.md) for where.

### Board differences

- AQA has no calorimetry of food, no growth percentile charts and no DNA extraction practical. Edexcel examines all three.
- 1.13B and 1.14B are Biology only (not in Combined Science 1SC0).

## 1.11 Rate calculations for enzyme activity

### What you need to know

- Rate is how much change happens per unit of time.
- For an enzyme, rate can be worked out from how fast substrate is used up or product is made.
- When the result is a time to finish (for example, the time for starch to disappear), rate = 1 ÷ time.
- Rate can be read from the gradient of a graph of product (or substrate) against time.

### Explained

Enzyme experiments rarely measure rate directly. You usually measure one of two things:

1. **A quantity changing over time**, such as the volume of oxygen given off by catalase breaking down hydrogen peroxide. Rate = amount of change ÷ time taken, with units such as cm³/s or cm³/min.
2. **The time for a reaction to reach an end point**, such as the time until iodine stops turning blue-black when amylase digests starch. A shorter time means a faster reaction, so rate = 1 ÷ time, with units s⁻¹ (per second).

On a graph of product made against time, the line is steepest at the start, when there is most substrate. The gradient of the straight early part (change in y ÷ change in x) is the initial rate. The line flattens when the substrate runs out, not because the enzyme has stopped working.

### Worked example

Amylase is mixed with starch at pH 7. Samples are tested with iodine every 10 seconds. The blue-black colour is last seen at 110 s and gone at 120 s. Find the rate.

- Take the time as 120 s (the first sample with no starch).
- Rate = 1 ÷ 120 = 0.0083 s⁻¹ (2 s.f.).

A second example: catalase produces 36 cm³ of oxygen in the first 60 s. Rate = 36 ÷ 60 = 0.6 cm³/s.

### Common mistakes

- Writing rate = time. A longer time is a slower rate, so you must use 1 ÷ time.
- Forgetting units. 1 ÷ time in seconds gives s⁻¹.
- Saying the graph levels off because the enzyme is denatured. At a steady temperature and pH it levels off because the substrate is used up.

## 1.13B Core practical: identifying food molecules with chemical reagents (Biology only)

### What you need to know

- Starch: add iodine solution; orange-brown turns blue-black if starch is present.
- Reducing sugars (such as glucose): add Benedict's reagent and heat in a water bath at about 80 °C; blue turns green, yellow or brick red depending on how much sugar there is.
- Protein: add Biuret reagent (or potassium hydroxide then copper sulfate solution); blue turns purple (lilac).
- Fats: shake the food with ethanol, then pour the liquid into water; a cloudy white emulsion shows fat.

### Explained

These tests are qualitative: they show whether a molecule is there. Benedict's is semi-quantitative, because the colour moves from blue through green and yellow to orange and brick red as the sugar concentration rises. Solid foods are ground with a little distilled water first so the molecules are in solution.

The tests for starch, sugars and protein are taught in the AQA note (4.2.2.1 required practical). The one Edexcel names that AQA does not is the **ethanol emulsion test** for fats (AQA uses Sudan III).

### Common mistakes

- Heating the Biuret or iodine test. Only the Benedict's test needs heat.
- Saying sucrose gives a positive Benedict's test. Sucrose is not a reducing sugar.
- Using a naked flame near ethanol. Ethanol is flammable; keep it away from Bunsen burners.

### Practical

- Hazards: Benedict's reagent and Biuret reagent are irritants; ethanol is highly flammable; hot water baths can scald. Wear eye protection.
- Use a control (distilled water) with each reagent so you can compare colours.

## 1.14B Measuring the energy in food by calorimetry (Biology only)

### What you need to know

- Burning a food sample transfers its stored chemical energy to heat, which can be used to warm a known mass of water.
- Energy transferred (J) = mass of water (g) × 4.2 J/g °C × temperature rise (°C).
- Energy per gram of food = energy transferred ÷ mass of food burned.
- The simple method gives a lower value than the true energy content because much of the heat is lost.

### Explained

Set-up: a known volume of water (1 cm³ has a mass of 1 g) in a boiling tube clamped above the food. Record the starting water temperature. Weigh a small piece of dry food, hold it on a mounted needle, light it in a Bunsen flame and quickly put it under the tube. When it stops burning, relight it until it will not burn any more. Record the highest temperature and reweigh what is left of the food.

Why the result is too low:

- Heat goes into the air, the glass and the clamp, not just the water.
- The food may not burn completely (black residue).
- Heat is lost while moving the burning food from the flame to the tube.

A food calorimeter (food burned in oxygen inside a chamber surrounded by water) loses much less heat and gives values closer to the true figure.

### Worked example

0.50 g of a crisp is burned under 20 cm³ of water. The water warms from 19 °C to 43 °C.

- Temperature rise = 43 − 19 = 24 °C.
- Energy = 20 × 4.2 × 24 = 2016 J.
- Energy per gram = 2016 ÷ 0.50 = 4032 J/g ≈ 4.0 kJ/g.

### Common mistakes

- Using the final temperature instead of the temperature rise.
- Dividing by the starting mass of food when some was left unburned. Use the mass actually burned.
- Saying the error is "human error". Name the real source: heat lost to the surroundings or incomplete burning.

### Practical

- Hazards: burning food can drop off the needle; work over a heatproof mat. Some foods (nuts) can trigger allergies; avoid them.
- Keep the distance between flame and tube the same each time, and stir the water before reading the temperature.

## 2.7 Percentile charts to monitor growth

### What you need to know

- A percentile chart shows how a child's measurement (mass, height or head size) compares with other children of the same age and sex.
- The 50th percentile line is the middle value: half of children are above it and half below.
- A child on the 90th percentile is bigger than 90% of children of that age.
- Doctors look for a child who is above the top line or below the bottom line, or whose measurements cross percentile lines quickly, as this can show a problem.

### Explained

Each curved line on the chart is a percentile. Growth is normal if a child follows roughly the same line as they get older, even if that line is low or high, because children differ. What matters more is the pattern over time:

- Steady growth along the 25th percentile is normal.
- A baby who drops from the 75th to the 9th percentile in a few months may not be getting enough food or may be ill.
- Head size growing much faster than the percentile lines can suggest fluid on the brain.

### Worked example

A boy is 12 months old and has a mass of 9.6 kg. On the chart this point lies on the 25th percentile line. What does this mean?

- 25% of 12-month-old boys have a mass below 9.6 kg and 75% have a higher mass. This is in the normal range.

### Common mistakes

- Thinking the 50th percentile is the "correct" size. Any steady line within the chart is normal.
- Reading "90th percentile" as "90% of the average mass".

## 3.6 Extracting DNA from fruit

### What you need to know

- DNA can be extracted from soft fruit (such as strawberries or kiwi) in the lab.
- The steps break the cells open, separate the DNA from other material and make the DNA come out of solution.

### Explained

| Step | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Mash the fruit with a mixture of washing-up liquid and salt solution | Detergent breaks down the cell membranes and nuclear membranes, releasing DNA. Salt makes the DNA strands clump together. |
| Warm in a water bath at about 60 °C for 15 minutes, then cool | Helps break up membranes and slows enzymes that would cut up the DNA. Cooling stops the DNA breaking down. |
| Filter | Removes cell walls and other solid debris; the DNA is in the filtrate. |
| (Optional) add a little protease | Breaks down proteins that are bound to the DNA. |
| Pour ice-cold ethanol gently down the side so it forms a layer on top | DNA does not dissolve in cold ethanol, so it comes out as white strands at the boundary. |

The white, stringy material can be wound onto a glass rod. Strawberries work well because their cells have many copies of each chromosome.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the detergent "dissolves the DNA". It breaks down the lipid membranes.
- Mixing the ethanol in. It must form a separate layer so the DNA collects at the boundary.
- Thinking you can see the double helix. You see millions of DNA molecules clumped together.

## Check questions

1. A student times how long amylase takes to digest starch at pH 5. The starch is gone after 150 s. Calculate the rate of reaction and give its unit. (2 marks)
2. Explain why the volume of product in an enzyme-controlled reaction stops increasing, even though the enzyme is still active. (2 marks)
3. Describe how a student could test a sample of milk to show it contains protein and fat. (4 marks)
4. 0.80 g of bread is burned under 25 g of water. The temperature of the water rises by 18 °C. Calculate the energy released per gram of bread. (3 marks)
5. Give two reasons why the value in question 4 is lower than the value on the bread's food label. (2 marks)
6. A baby girl's mass was on the 75th percentile at 2 months and on the 10th percentile at 6 months. Explain why a doctor would be concerned. (2 marks)
7. In a DNA extraction, explain the purpose of (a) the detergent and (b) the ice-cold ethanol. (2 marks)

## Mark scheme

1. Rate = 1 ÷ 150 (1) = 0.0067 s⁻¹ (accept 6.7 × 10⁻³ /s) (1).
2. The substrate is used up / all substrate molecules have been converted (1); so no more enzyme–substrate complexes can form / no more product can be made (1).
3. Protein: add Biuret reagent (or potassium hydroxide then copper sulfate) (1); turns from blue to purple if protein present (1). Fat: shake with ethanol then add to water (1); cloudy white emulsion if fat present (1).
4. Energy = 25 × 4.2 × 18 = 1890 J (1); ÷ 0.80 (1); = 2362.5 J/g, accept 2360 J/g or 2.4 kJ/g (1).
5. Any two: heat lost to the surroundings/air; heat absorbed by the boiling tube or clamp; food not completely burned; heat lost while moving food to the tube (2).
6. Her mass has dropped across percentile lines / is falling compared with other babies (1); could mean she is not getting enough nutrition or has an illness (1).
7. (a) Breaks down the cell and nuclear membranes to release the DNA (1). (b) DNA is insoluble in cold ethanol so it comes out of solution/precipitates as strands (1).

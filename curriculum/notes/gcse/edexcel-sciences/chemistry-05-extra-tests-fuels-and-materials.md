### Board differences

- Edexcel asks for halogen colours and states, hydrogen halides, noble gas uses, the heat changes of named processes and hydrogen as a car fuel in Combined Science; AQA does not list these.
- Edexcel Chemistry adds tests for the ammonium ion and ammonia gas, problems with polymers, and a core practical (9.28C) on the heat given out by burning different alcohols.

## 6.6 Colours and physical states of the halogens

### What you need to know

| Halogen | State at room temperature | Colour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chlorine, Cl₂ | gas | pale yellow-green |
| Bromine, Br₂ | liquid | red-brown (gives off orange-brown vapour) |
| Iodine, I₂ | solid | dark grey (shiny); gives purple vapour on gentle heating |

### Explained

Going down the group, the molecules get bigger, the forces between molecules get stronger, so melting and boiling points rise: gas → liquid → solid. Colours get darker down the group.

### Common mistakes

- Saying iodine is purple at room temperature. The solid is dark grey; its vapour is purple.

## 6.10 Hydrogen halides

### What you need to know

- Halogens react with hydrogen to form hydrogen halides, e.g. H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl.
- Hydrogen halides are gases that dissolve in water to form acidic solutions: hydrogen chloride gives hydrochloric acid.

### Explained

In water, HCl releases H⁺ ions, which makes the solution acidic (blue litmus turns red). The reactions with hydrogen get less vigorous down the group, like the other halogen reactions.

### Common mistakes

- Calling hydrogen chloride gas an acid when dry. It is acidic once dissolved in water.

## 6.15 Uses of the noble gases

### What you need to know

| Gas | Use | Property used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Helium | balloons, airships | very low density and not flammable |
| Argon | filling filament lamps; shield gas in welding | inert, so the hot metal does not react with oxygen |
| Neon | advertising signs | glows when electricity passes through it |
| Krypton, xenon | some lamps, lasers | inert; give bright light |

### Common mistakes

- Using hydrogen as the "safe" balloon gas. Hydrogen is flammable; helium is used because it is not.

## 7.9 Heat changes in dissolving, displacement, precipitation and neutralisation

### What you need to know

- These changes all transfer heat to or from the surroundings, which can be detected as a temperature change of the solution.
- Neutralisation, displacement and most precipitation reactions are exothermic (temperature rises).
- Dissolving some salts is endothermic (e.g. ammonium nitrate, used in cold packs); dissolving others is exothermic (e.g. anhydrous calcium chloride).

### Explained

Measure the temperature of each solution, mix them in a polystyrene cup with a lid, stir and record the highest (or lowest) temperature. The change in temperature shows whether heat was given out or taken in.

### Common mistakes

- Saying all dissolving is endothermic.

## 8.14 Hydrogen versus petrol as a fuel for cars

### What you need to know

| | Hydrogen | Petrol |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Combustion product | only water | carbon dioxide, water; plus carbon monoxide, soot, nitrogen oxides |
| Energy per kg | about three times more than petrol | lower |
| Storage | gas: needs heavy high-pressure tanks; explosive risk | liquid; easy to store and pour |
| Supply | few filling stations; mostly made from natural gas or by electrolysis, which uses energy | widely available, but non-renewable |

### Explained

Hydrogen burns cleanly at the point of use, but making it can produce carbon dioxide if the energy comes from fossil fuels. Engines burning hydrogen at high temperature can still make some nitrogen oxides from the air.

### Common mistakes

- Saying hydrogen cars produce no pollution at all. Producing the hydrogen may cause pollution, and high-temperature combustion can make nitrogen oxides.

## 9.3C Tests for cations, including the ammonium ion (Chemistry only)

### What you need to know

- Add a few drops of sodium hydroxide solution:

| Ion | Observation |
| --- | --- |
| Al³⁺ | white precipitate that dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide |
| Ca²⁺ | white precipitate that does not dissolve in excess |
| Cu²⁺ | blue precipitate |
| Fe²⁺ | green precipitate |
| Fe³⁺ | brown precipitate |
| NH₄⁺ | no precipitate; on warming, ammonia gas is given off |

- The metal hydroxide tests are in the AQA note 4.8.3.2. The new one here is ammonium: NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O.

### Common mistakes

- Expecting a precipitate with ammonium ions. There is none; you test the gas.

## 9.4C Test for ammonia (Chemistry only)

### What you need to know

- Ammonia gas turns damp red litmus paper blue.
- It has a sharp, choking smell (do not sniff directly).

### Explained

Ammonia is the only common alkaline gas, which is why damp red litmus turning blue identifies it. The paper must be damp, because ammonia has to dissolve to form hydroxide ions.

### Common mistakes

- Using dry litmus paper.

## 9.21C Uses of polymers from their properties (Chemistry only)

### What you need to know

| Polymer | Properties | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Poly(ethene) | flexible, cheap, waterproof | plastic bags, bottles |
| Poly(propene) | tough, flexible, high softening point | crates, ropes, kettles |
| Poly(chloroethene) (PVC) | tough, rigid (or flexible with additives), electrical insulator | window frames, pipes, cable insulation |
| PTFE | very slippery, non-stick, unreactive, high melting point | non-stick pans, plumber's tape |

### Common mistakes

- Giving a use without linking it to a property.

## 9.23C Problems with polymers (Chemistry only)

### What you need to know

- Most addition polymers come from crude oil, a finite resource.
- Most are not biodegradable, so they persist in landfill and in the environment (e.g. ocean plastic).
- Burning them for disposal can release toxic gases (PVC gives hydrogen chloride) and carbon dioxide.

### Common mistakes

- Saying polymers are toxic in use. The problems are mostly about supply and disposal.

## 9.24C Recycling polymers (Chemistry only)

### What you need to know

- Advantages: saves crude oil; less waste in landfill; often uses less energy than making new polymer; less carbon dioxide.
- Disadvantages: polymers must be collected, sorted into types and cleaned, which costs money and energy; recycled polymer may be lower quality; some types cannot be recycled together.

### Common mistakes

- Saying recycling has no costs. Collection, transport and sorting use energy and money.

## 9.28C Core practical: temperature rise from burning alcohols (Chemistry only)

### What you need to know

- Different alcohols are burned in spirit burners to heat a fixed mass of water. The mass of alcohol burned and the temperature rise are measured.
- The longer the alcohol's carbon chain, the more heat is released per gram (and per mole), so the bigger the temperature rise per gram.

### Explained

1. Weigh a spirit burner containing the alcohol, with its cap on.
2. Put 100 cm³ (100 g) of water in a copper can clamped above the burner; record the temperature.
3. Light the burner and heat until the temperature has risen by about 20 °C, stirring the water.
4. Put the cap on to put out the flame (and stop evaporation), then reweigh.
5. Repeat with methanol, ethanol, propanol and butanol, keeping the height of the can and the volume of water the same.

Calculate the temperature rise per gram of alcohol burned and compare. Heat losses to the air, evaporation of alcohol and incomplete combustion (soot on the can) all make the results smaller than the true values.

### Worked example

Burning 0.80 g of ethanol raises 100 g of water by 20 °C.

- Temperature rise per gram = 20 ÷ 0.80 = 25 °C/g.
- Energy transferred to water = 100 × 4.2 × 20 = 8 400 J, so about 10.5 kJ per gram of ethanol.

### Common mistakes

- Leaving the cap off between weighings, so alcohol evaporates and the mass burned appears too high.
- Not keeping the distance between flame and can the same.

### Practical

- Hazards: alcohols are highly flammable and methanol is toxic. Keep stock bottles away from flames; wear eye protection; do not leave burners unattended.

## 9.34C Concentrating ethanol by fractional distillation (Chemistry only)

### What you need to know

- Fermentation produces a dilute ethanol solution (up to about 15%), because higher concentrations kill the yeast.
- Fractional distillation separates ethanol (boiling point 78 °C) from water (100 °C), giving a more concentrated ethanol solution.

### Common mistakes

- Using simple distillation. The boiling points are close enough that a fractionating column is needed for a good separation.

1. Red-brown (1); liquid (1).
2. Argon is inert/unreactive (1); so the hot filament does not react/oxidise/burn (1).
3. Advantage: only water produced/no CO₂ at point of use/more energy per kg (1). Disadvantages, any two: hard to store/needs high-pressure tanks; few filling stations; production uses energy/may use fossil fuels; flammable/explosive (2).

6. 30 ÷ 1.20 = 25 °C/g (1); 100 × 4.2 × 30 (1) = 12 600 J (1).

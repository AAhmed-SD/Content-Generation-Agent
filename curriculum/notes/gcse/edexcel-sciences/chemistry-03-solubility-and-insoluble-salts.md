# Solubility and insoluble salts (Edexcel gap file)

Making a soluble salt from an insoluble base, and titration technique, are in [AQA 8462 4.4.2](../../aqa/gcse/chemistry-8462/4.4-chemical-changes.md). This file adds Edexcel's solubility rules, precipitation and the preparation of an insoluble salt, which AQA does not teach.

### Board differences

- AQA GCSE has no solubility rules and no insoluble salt preparation. Edexcel examines both in Combined Science and Chemistry.

## 3.16 Making a soluble salt from an acid and a soluble reactant

### What you need to know

- When both reactants are soluble (an acid and an alkali, such as hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide), you cannot add an excess and filter it off, because the excess would stay dissolved in the salt solution.
- So a titration is used to find the exact volumes that react.
- The titration is then repeated with the same volumes but **without** the indicator (which would contaminate the salt), and the solution is crystallised.

### Explained

1. Titrate the acid against the alkali with an indicator; record the volume of acid needed.
2. Mix the same volumes again without indicator.
3. Evaporate some water, then leave to crystallise; filter and dry the crystals.

Compare with an insoluble base (such as copper oxide): add excess, filter off the leftover solid, then crystallise (AQA 4.4.2.3).

### Common mistakes

- Leaving the indicator in the final solution.
- Adding excess alkali "to make sure". The excess cannot be removed.

## 3.19 Solubility rules

### What you need to know

| Compounds | Soluble? |
| --- | --- |
| Common sodium, potassium and ammonium salts | all soluble |
| Nitrates | all soluble |
| Common chlorides | soluble, except silver chloride and lead chloride |
| Common sulfates | soluble, except lead sulfate, barium sulfate and calcium sulfate |
| Common carbonates and hydroxides | insoluble, except those of sodium, potassium and ammonium |

### Explained

A useful order to apply them: first look at the metal (or ammonium). If it is sodium, potassium or ammonium, the compound is soluble whatever the negative ion. If not, look at the negative ion and use its rule.

### Common mistakes

- Saying all chlorides are soluble. Silver and lead chlorides are not.
- Saying calcium carbonate is soluble because calcium is a common metal. Carbonates are insoluble except group 1 and ammonium.

## 3.20 Predicting precipitates

### What you need to know

- A **precipitate** is an insoluble solid formed when two solutions are mixed.
- To predict a precipitate: swap partners between the two compounds, then use the solubility rules on the two new compounds.

### Worked example

Will a precipitate form when lead nitrate solution and potassium iodide solution are mixed? Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2KI → PbI₂ + 2KNO₃.

- Potassium nitrate is soluble (all potassium salts and all nitrates).
- Lead iodide: lead is not group 1 or ammonium. Lead halides are insoluble (like lead chloride), so lead iodide precipitates (a yellow solid).

Will a precipitate form with sodium chloride and potassium nitrate? Swapping gives sodium nitrate and potassium chloride, both soluble. No precipitate.

### Common mistakes

- Forgetting that some swaps give no precipitate at all.

## 3.21 Preparing a pure, dry sample of an insoluble salt

### What you need to know

- Mix solutions of two soluble salts that between them contain the ions of the insoluble salt you want.
- Filter to collect the precipitate.
- Wash it with distilled water on the filter paper to remove soluble impurities.
- Dry it in a warm oven or by leaving it on filter paper.

### Explained

To make lead(II) iodide: mix lead nitrate solution and potassium iodide solution. The lead and iodide ions form a yellow precipitate. The potassium and nitrate ions stay in solution and pass through the filter paper; washing removes any left on the solid.

To make barium sulfate: mix barium chloride solution with sodium sulfate (or dilute sulfuric acid).

### Common mistakes

- Crystallising the filtrate. The salt you want is the solid on the filter paper, not in the filtrate.
- Skipping the washing step, leaving soluble salts mixed with the product.

### Practical

- Hazards: lead and barium compounds are toxic; wear gloves and eye protection; dispose of residues as instructed, not down the sink.

## Check questions

1. Explain why copper sulfate can be made by adding excess copper oxide to acid and filtering, but sodium sulfate cannot be made by adding excess sodium hydroxide to acid. (3 marks)
2. Use the solubility rules to state whether each is soluble or insoluble: (a) magnesium carbonate, (b) ammonium chloride, (c) barium sulfate, (d) zinc nitrate. (4 marks)
3. Silver nitrate solution is mixed with sodium chloride solution. Name the precipitate and write the ionic equation. (2 marks)
4. Describe how to make a pure, dry sample of calcium carbonate from calcium chloride solution and sodium carbonate solution. (4 marks)

## Mark scheme

1. Copper oxide is insoluble so excess can be filtered off (1); sodium hydroxide is soluble so excess would stay in the solution/contaminate the salt (1); so a titration must be used to add exactly the right amount (1).
2. (a) insoluble (b) soluble (c) insoluble (d) soluble (1 each).
3. Silver chloride (1); Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s) (1).
4. Mix the two solutions (1); filter to collect the calcium carbonate precipitate (1); wash with distilled water (1); dry in a warm oven/on filter paper (1).

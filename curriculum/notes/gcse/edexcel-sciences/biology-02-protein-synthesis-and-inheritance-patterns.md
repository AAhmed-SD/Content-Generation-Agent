# Protein synthesis and inheritance patterns (Edexcel gap file)

DNA structure, genes, alleles and monohybrid crosses are taught in [AQA 8461 4.6](../../aqa/gcse/biology-8461/4.6-inheritance-variation-and-evolution.md). This file adds the detail Edexcel examines beyond that.

### Board differences

- Edexcel names the steps of transcription and translation (RNA polymerase, mRNA, tRNA, codons) and asks about variants in non-coding DNA. AQA only outlines protein synthesis.
- Codominance with multiple alleles (ABO blood groups) and sex-linked inheritance are Edexcel only.
- 3.7B to 3.10B, 3.17B and 3.18B are Biology only; 3.7B to 3.10B and 3.18B are higher tier.

## 3.7B Base sequence, amino acids and protein shape (Biology only) (HT only)

### What you need to know

- The order of bases in a gene decides the order of amino acids in a protein.
- Each group of three bases (a triplet or codon) codes for one amino acid.
- The chain of amino acids folds into a particular shape, and this shape lets the protein do its job (for example, the active site of an enzyme).

### Explained

DNA has four bases: A, T, C and G. Three bases together make a code word. For example, the triplet TAC on the template strand leads to the amino acid methionine being added. A gene of 900 bases codes for a protein of 300 amino acids.

Once joined, the amino acid chain folds. Amino acids at different points attract or repel each other, so the order of amino acids decides the final 3D shape. Change the order and the shape may change, so the protein may no longer work.

### Common mistakes

- Saying one base codes for one amino acid. It takes three.
- Saying the gene "is" the protein. The gene is the instructions; the protein is made from them.

## 3.8B Transcription and translation (Biology only) (HT only)

### What you need to know

- **Transcription** happens in the nucleus:
  - RNA polymerase binds to non-coding DNA just in front of a gene.
  - It makes a single strand of messenger RNA (mRNA) that is complementary to the gene's template strand.
  - The mRNA leaves the nucleus through a pore.
- **Translation** happens at a ribosome in the cytoplasm:
  - The mRNA attaches to a ribosome.
  - Transfer RNA (tRNA) molecules bring specific amino acids. Each tRNA has an anticodon that matches a codon on the mRNA.
  - The amino acids are joined in the order of the codons, making a polypeptide chain.

### Explained

DNA is too big to leave the nucleus and must be kept safe, so the cell makes a working copy of just one gene. RNA is like DNA but single-stranded and uses the base U (uracil) in place of T.

Base pairing in transcription: DNA template A → mRNA U; T → A; C → G; G → C.

At the ribosome, the mRNA is read three bases at a time. A tRNA whose anticodon pairs with the codon brings its amino acid. The ribosome joins this amino acid to the one before, then moves along to the next codon. This continues until a stop codon is reached and the finished chain is released to fold.

### Worked example

A section of DNA template strand reads: TAC GGA TTC.

- mRNA (complementary, U for A): AUG CCU AAG.
- Three codons, so three amino acids are joined.
- The tRNA anticodons are: UAC GGA UUC.

### Common mistakes

- Writing T in mRNA. RNA uses U.
- Saying translation happens in the nucleus. It happens at ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
- Mixing up codon (on mRNA) and anticodon (on tRNA).

## 3.9B Variants in non-coding DNA (Biology only) (HT only)

### What you need to know

- Not all DNA codes for proteins. Some non-coding DNA controls whether genes are switched on.
- A variant (change in the base sequence) in the non-coding region where RNA polymerase binds can change how easily RNA polymerase attaches.
- This changes how much mRNA, and so how much protein, is made, which can change the phenotype.

### Explained

Think of the non-coding region in front of a gene as a "start here" sign. If a variant makes the sign hard to read, RNA polymerase binds less well, less mRNA is made and less protein is produced. If the sign becomes easier to read, more protein is made. The protein itself is normal; only the amount changes.

### Common mistakes

- Saying non-coding variants always have no effect. They can change the amount of protein made.

## 3.10B Variants in coding DNA (Biology only) (HT only)

### What you need to know

- A variant inside a gene can change a codon, which may change one amino acid in the protein.
- This can change the protein's shape and so its activity. For an enzyme, the active site may no longer fit the substrate.
- Some variants have no effect because the new codon still codes for the same amino acid, or the change is in a part of the protein that does not affect its shape.

### Explained

Coding variants change *what* protein is made; non-coding variants change *how much*. Examples of coding changes:

- Substitution: one base swapped. May change one amino acid or none.
- Insertion or deletion: shifts every codon after it, so usually many amino acids change and the protein does not work.

### Common mistakes

- Saying every mutation causes a disease. Most have no effect on the phenotype.

## 3.17B ABO blood groups: codominance and multiple alleles (Biology only)

### What you need to know

- The ABO blood group gene has three alleles: Iᴬ, Iᴮ and Iᴼ. This is an example of **multiple alleles** (more than two forms of a gene in the population). Each person still has only two.
- Iᴬ and Iᴮ are **codominant**: if both are present, both are expressed and the person has blood group AB.
- Iᴼ is recessive to both Iᴬ and Iᴮ.

### Explained

| Genotype | Blood group |
| --- | --- |
| IᴬIᴬ or IᴬIᴼ | A |
| IᴮIᴮ or IᴮIᴼ | B |
| IᴬIᴮ | AB |
| IᴼIᴼ | O |

Codominance is different from simple dominance: neither allele hides the other, so the heterozygote has a third phenotype that shows both.

### Worked example

A father is group A (IᴬIᴼ) and a mother is group B (IᴮIᴼ). What blood groups can their children have?

| | Iᴮ | Iᴼ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Iᴬ** | IᴬIᴮ (AB) | IᴬIᴼ (A) |
| **Iᴼ** | IᴮIᴼ (B) | IᴼIᴼ (O) |

Each of AB, A, B and O has a probability of 1 in 4 (25%).

### Common mistakes

- Writing alleles as A, B and O without the I. Edexcel expects Iᴬ, Iᴮ and Iᴼ.
- Thinking two group A parents cannot have a group O child. If both are IᴬIᴼ, there is a 1 in 4 chance.

## 3.18B Sex-linked genetic disorders (Biology only) (HT only)

### What you need to know

- Sex-linked disorders are caused by alleles on the X chromosome. The Y chromosome is much smaller and does not carry a matching allele.
- Most are recessive, for example red-green colour blindness and haemophilia.
- Males (XY) have only one X, so a single recessive allele causes the disorder. Females (XX) need two copies; with one copy they are **carriers**.
- So these disorders are much more common in males.

### Explained

Write the allele as a superscript on the X: Xᴴ (normal clotting) and Xʰ (haemophilia). The Y has no allele.

| Genotype | Phenotype |
| --- | --- |
| XᴴXᴴ | Female, unaffected |
| XᴴXʰ | Female carrier (unaffected) |
| XʰXʰ | Female with haemophilia (rare) |
| XᴴY | Male, unaffected |
| XʰY | Male with haemophilia |

A son always gets his X from his mother, so an affected son inherits the allele from his mother, never his father.

### Worked example

A carrier mother (XᴴXʰ) and an unaffected father (XᴴY):

| | Xᴴ | Y |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Xᴴ** | XᴴXᴴ | XᴴY |
| **Xʰ** | XᴴXʰ | XʰY |

- Probability a child has haemophilia = 1/4 (25%).
- Probability a son has haemophilia = 1/2 (50%).
- No daughters are affected, but half are carriers.

### Common mistakes

- Putting the allele on the Y chromosome.
- Saying a father can pass an X-linked disorder to his son. He passes his Y to sons.
- Mixing up "probability a child is affected" and "probability a son is affected".

## 3.21 The Human Genome Project and medicine

### What you need to know

- The Human Genome Project (finished in 2003) worked out the order of all the bases in human DNA and helped locate about 20 000 genes.
- Possible uses in medicine:
  - finding genes linked to diseases, and testing people for alleles that raise their risk;
  - developing new treatments and drugs targeted at faulty genes;
  - personalised medicine: choosing the drug and dose that works best with a person's genotype.
- There are concerns: people may be anxious about their risk, insurers or employers might discriminate, and having a risk allele does not mean you will get the disease.

### Explained

Before the project, scientists hunting for a disease gene had to search blind. With the full sequence, they can compare the DNA of people with and without a disease to find the differences. This has led to tests such as screening for alleles that raise breast cancer risk.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the project "cured" genetic diseases. It gave information that helps research and testing.
- Saying a high-risk allele means you will definitely get the disease. It raises the probability.

## Check questions

1. Describe how mRNA is made from a gene. (3 marks)
2. A DNA template strand reads ATG CCA. Write the mRNA it codes for. (1 mark)
3. Explain how a change in one base in the coding part of an enzyme's gene could stop the enzyme working. (3 marks)
4. Explain how a variant in non-coding DNA could change a person's phenotype. (3 marks)
5. A man with blood group AB and a woman with blood group O have children. Use a genetic diagram to give the possible blood groups of their children and the probability of each. (3 marks)
6. Explain why colour blindness is more common in males than in females. (2 marks)
7. A colour-blind man (XᵇY) has children with a woman who is not a carrier (XᴮXᴮ). What is the probability that their daughter is a carrier? Explain your answer. (2 marks)
8. Give one benefit and one concern of using information from the Human Genome Project in medicine. (2 marks)

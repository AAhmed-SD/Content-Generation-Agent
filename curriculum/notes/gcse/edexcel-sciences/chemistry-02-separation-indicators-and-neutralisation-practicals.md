# Separation, indicators and two core practicals (Edexcel gap file)

Mixtures and separation are introduced in [AQA 8462 4.1.1.2](../../aqa/gcse/chemistry-8462/4.1-atomic-structure-and-the-periodic-table.md); chromatography is in [AQA 8462 4.8.1.3](../../aqa/gcse/chemistry-8462/4.8-chemical-analysis.md); pH and neutralisation are in [AQA 8462 4.4.2](../../aqa/gcse/chemistry-8462/4.4-chemical-changes.md). This file adds the Edexcel detail.

### Board differences

- Edexcel's core practical 2.11 combines simple distillation and paper chromatography on one sample of ink. AQA's chromatography practical does not include distillation.
- Edexcel's core practical 3.6 (adding calcium hydroxide or calcium oxide to acid and tracking pH) has no AQA equivalent.
- Edexcel names three indicators and their colours.

## 2.7 Which mixtures each technique separates

### What you need to know

| Technique | Separates | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Filtration | an insoluble solid from a liquid | sand from salt solution |
| Crystallisation | a dissolved solid from its solution (keeping the solid) | copper sulfate crystals from solution |
| Simple distillation | a liquid (solvent) from a solution (keeping the liquid) | pure water from sea water |
| Fractional distillation | two or more miscible liquids with different boiling points | ethanol from water; crude oil fractions |
| Paper chromatography | soluble substances (such as dyes) in a mixture | colours in an ink |

### Explained

The method depends on the physical property that differs: particle size and solubility (filtration), boiling point (distillation) or how strongly each substance sticks to the paper versus dissolves in the solvent (chromatography).

Crystallisation: heat the solution to evaporate some solvent until crystals start to form on a cold glass rod dipped in it, then leave it to cool so crystals form slowly; filter and dry the crystals. Heating to dryness can decompose some salts or drive off water of crystallisation.

Fractional distillation uses a fractionating column packed with glass beads. The column is hotter at the bottom. The liquid with the lower boiling point reaches the top first and passes into the condenser.

### Common mistakes

- Saying filtration separates salt from water. Dissolved salt passes through the filter paper.
- Mixing up simple and fractional distillation. Simple: solvent from solute. Fractional: liquids from each other.

## 2.8 Choosing a separation method

### What you need to know

- Given the properties of the substances (solubility, melting and boiling points), you should pick a technique and explain why it works.

### Worked example

A mixture contains sand, salt and water. Get dry salt.

1. Filter to remove the sand (insoluble), which stays on the filter paper.
2. Crystallise the filtrate (salt solution) to get salt crystals.

A mixture of two liquids boils at 65 °C (methanol) and 100 °C (water). Use fractional distillation; methanol distils first, at 65 °C.

### Common mistakes

- Choosing evaporation to get water. Evaporation loses the water; use distillation to collect it.

## 2.11 Core practical: composition of inks by simple distillation and paper chromatography

### What you need to know

- Simple distillation of ink collects the solvent (usually water) and leaves the dyes behind.
- Paper chromatography of the ink separates and identifies the dyes.
- The boiling point of the collected liquid (a thermometer at the side arm reads about 100 °C for water) helps identify the solvent.

### Explained

Distillation:

1. Put the ink in a round-bottomed flask with anti-bumping granules.
2. Fit a thermometer with its bulb level with the side arm leading to a Liebig condenser. Cold water enters the condenser at the bottom and leaves at the top.
3. Heat gently. The solvent boils, the vapour passes into the condenser, cools and condenses. Collect the distillate in a beaker or test tube.
4. The dyes stay in the flask. Record the steady temperature while the liquid is distilling.

Chromatography:

1. Draw a pencil start line about 1.5 cm from the bottom of the paper.
2. Put a small spot of ink on it, plus spots of known dyes for comparison.
3. Stand the paper in a beaker with solvent below the start line; cover the beaker.
4. Remove the paper when the solvent nearly reaches the top; mark the solvent front.
5. Measure distances and calculate Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent.

### Common mistakes

- Placing the thermometer bulb in the liquid. It must be at the side arm to read the vapour temperature.
- Water entering the top of the condenser. It enters at the bottom so the condenser fills completely.
- Using a pen for the start line. The ink would also separate.

### Practical

- Hazards: hot apparatus and steam; flammable solvents if not water (use a water bath or electric heater). Never heat a sealed system; keep the end of the condenser open.

## 3.3 Indicators: litmus, methyl orange and phenolphthalein

### What you need to know

| Indicator | Colour in acid | Colour in alkali |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Litmus | red | blue |
| Methyl orange | red | yellow |
| Phenolphthalein | colourless | pink |

- Universal indicator shows a range of colours, so it gives an approximate pH. The three single indicators above only show acid or alkali.

### Explained

Single indicators change colour sharply at one point, which makes them good for titrations: the end point is easy to see. Universal indicator changes gradually, so it is not used in titrations.

### Common mistakes

- Saying phenolphthalein is pink in acid. It is colourless in acid and pink in alkali.
- Saying methyl orange is orange in alkali. It is yellow.

## 3.6 Core practical: pH change when calcium hydroxide or calcium oxide is added to acid

### What you need to know

- Small, equal amounts of powdered calcium hydroxide (or calcium oxide) are added to a fixed volume of dilute hydrochloric acid, and the pH is measured after each addition.
- The pH starts low (about 1), rises slowly, then rises sharply near neutralisation, and levels off at a high value once the calcium hydroxide is in excess.

### Explained

Method:

1. Measure 50 cm³ of dilute hydrochloric acid into a beaker.
2. Measure the starting pH with a pH meter, or with universal indicator paper and a colour chart.
3. Add one spatula measure (or a weighed 0.3 g) of calcium hydroxide, stir, and record the pH.
4. Repeat until the pH stops changing.
5. Plot pH (y-axis) against mass or number of spatulas added (x-axis).

Ca(OH)₂(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l)

At first the acid is in excess, so the pH stays low. Near the end, a small addition removes the last H⁺ ions, so pH jumps. Once all the acid has reacted, extra calcium hydroxide (which is only slightly soluble) makes the solution alkaline, around pH 12.

A pH meter gives more precise readings than indicator paper, which relies on judging colour.

### Common mistakes

- Expecting a straight line. The curve is S-shaped.
- Not stirring, so the solid has not reacted when the reading is taken.

### Practical

- Hazards: calcium hydroxide and calcium oxide are irritants and calcium oxide reacts strongly with water; avoid breathing in powder; wear eye protection.
- Keep the amount added each time the same; use the same volume and concentration of acid.

## Check questions

1. Name the technique used to obtain pure water from salty water, and explain why it works. (3 marks)
2. A student wants to separate ethanol (boiling point 78 °C) from water. Name the technique and say which liquid is collected first. (2 marks)
3. In the inks core practical, explain where the thermometer bulb should be and why. (2 marks)
4. A dye moves 3.6 cm and the solvent front moves 8.0 cm. Calculate the Rf value. (2 marks)
5. Give the colour of phenolphthalein and of methyl orange in sodium hydroxide solution. (2 marks)
6. Describe how the pH changes as calcium hydroxide powder is added to dilute hydrochloric acid until it is in excess, and explain the change. (4 marks)

## Mark scheme

1. Simple distillation (1); water boils and becomes vapour while salt does not (1); vapour is cooled and condensed in a condenser and collected (1).
2. Fractional distillation (1); ethanol collected first (1).
3. Level with the side arm/entrance to the condenser (1); to measure the temperature of the vapour that is being collected (1).
4. 3.6 ÷ 8.0 (1) = 0.45 (1).
5. Phenolphthalein pink (1); methyl orange yellow (1).
6. Starts low/about pH 1 and rises slowly (1); acid in excess, H⁺ ions being neutralised (1); sharp rise near neutral point/pH 7 (1); levels off high/about 12 as calcium hydroxide in excess/OH⁻ present (1).

# Formulae, hazards and empirical formulae (Edexcel gap file)

Balancing equations, relative formula mass and moles are in [AQA 8462 4.3](../../aqa/gcse/chemistry-8462/4.3-quantitative-chemistry.md); ionic compounds are in [AQA 8462 4.2.1](../../aqa/gcse/chemistry-8462/4.2-bonding-structure-and-the-properties-of-matter.md). This file adds Edexcel's Topic 0 skills, naming rules, percentage by mass and empirical formulae.

### Board differences

- Edexcel has a "Topic 0" of formulae, equations and hazards examined across all papers. AQA does not list hazard symbols or a table of ion formulae as spec content.
- Empirical formulae and the magnesium oxide experiment are Edexcel only at GCSE.
- 0.4 (ionic equations) is higher tier.

## 0.1 Formulae of elements, simple compounds and ions

### What you need to know

- Most elements are written as their symbol (Na, Fe, C). Seven common elements exist as diatomic molecules: H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂.
- Learn these common formulae: water H₂O, carbon dioxide CO₂, ammonia NH₃, methane CH₄, hydrochloric acid HCl, sulfuric acid H₂SO₄, nitric acid HNO₃, sodium hydroxide NaOH, calcium carbonate CaCO₃.
- Learn these ions:

| Positive ions | Negative ions |
| --- | --- |
| H⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Li⁺, Ag⁺, NH₄⁺ (ammonium) | Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ (halides), OH⁻ (hydroxide), NO₃⁻ (nitrate) |
| Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺, Fe²⁺ (iron(II)), Ba²⁺ | O²⁻ (oxide), S²⁻, SO₄²⁻ (sulfate), CO₃²⁻ (carbonate) |
| Al³⁺, Fe³⁺ (iron(III)) | |

### Explained

To build a formula, the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge. Calcium nitrate: Ca²⁺ needs two NO₃⁻, so Ca(NO₃)₂. Put brackets round a group ion when you need more than one of it.

### Common mistakes

- Writing CaNO₃₂ instead of Ca(NO₃)₂.
- Writing Cl for chlorine gas. It is Cl₂.

## 0.4 Balanced ionic equations (HT only)

### What you need to know

- An ionic equation shows only the ions (and other particles) that actually react; spectator ions, which are unchanged, are left out.
- Both the atoms and the total charge must balance.

### Explained

Steps for a precipitation reaction, silver nitrate + sodium chloride:

1. Full equation: AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq).
2. Split aqueous ionic compounds into ions: Ag⁺ + NO₃⁻ + Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl(s) + Na⁺ + NO₃⁻.
3. Cross out ions that appear on both sides (Na⁺ and NO₃⁻).
4. Ionic equation: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s).

Other common ionic equations: neutralisation H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l); displacement Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s).

### Common mistakes

- Splitting the solid precipitate into ions. Solids, liquids and gases stay as written.
- Forgetting to check that charges balance (left and right totals must be equal).

## 0.5 Hazard symbols

### What you need to know

Hazard symbols (diamond-shaped pictograms with a red border) on containers warn of dangers and tell you what precautions to take.

| Symbol meaning | Danger | Example | Precaution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Flammable | catches fire easily | ethanol | keep away from flames |
| Oxidising | provides oxygen, makes other things burn more fiercely | hydrogen peroxide (concentrated), potassium manganate(VII) | keep away from flammable materials |
| Corrosive | destroys skin and eyes, attacks metals | concentrated acids and alkalis | wear goggles and gloves |
| Toxic (acute) | can cause death if swallowed, breathed in or absorbed | chlorine, mercury compounds | use a fume cupboard; avoid contact |
| Harmful / irritant (exclamation mark) | causes irritation or harm in larger doses | dilute acids, copper sulfate | eye protection; wash off spills |
| Health hazard | long-term harm (e.g. causes cancer) | some organic solvents | avoid exposure |
| Hazardous to the environment | harms aquatic life | copper and silver salts | do not pour down the sink |

### Common mistakes

- Confusing oxidising (a flame over a circle) with flammable (a flame).
- Saying "be careful" as a precaution. Name a specific action, such as "wear eye protection".

## 0.6 Evaluating risks in practical work

### What you need to know

- A risk assessment identifies each hazard, the risk (how it could cause harm), and a precaution to reduce the risk.
- Common precautions: eye protection; heating with a water bath instead of a flame for flammable liquids; using a fume cupboard for toxic gases; using dilute solutions; tying back hair; standing up while heating.

### Explained

| Activity | Hazard | Risk | Precaution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Heating copper sulfate solution to crystallise | hot liquid, spitting | burns, eye damage | eye protection; heat gently; let cool before touching |
| Electrolysis of sodium chloride solution | chlorine gas (toxic) | breathing it in | small scale, well-ventilated room, stop after short time |
| Burning alcohols | flammable liquid | fire | spirit burners with caps; keep stock bottles away |

### Common mistakes

- Listing a hazard without saying how the precaution reduces the risk.

## 1.25 Naming compounds: -ide and -ate

### What you need to know

- **-ide**: the compound contains only the two elements named (plus any metal). Sodium chloride (NaCl), magnesium oxide (MgO). Exception: hydroxide (OH⁻) contains oxygen and hydrogen.
- **-ate**: the negative ion contains oxygen as well as another element. Sulfate (SO₄²⁻), nitrate (NO₃⁻), carbonate (CO₃²⁻).

### Common mistakes

- Calling CuSO₄ copper sulfide. Sulfide is S²⁻ only; sulfate contains oxygen.

## 1.43 Percentage by mass of an element in a compound

### What you need to know

- Percentage by mass of an element = (Aᵣ × number of atoms of that element ÷ Mᵣ of compound) × 100.

### Worked example

Find the percentage by mass of nitrogen in ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃ (N = 14, H = 1, O = 16).

- Mᵣ = 14 + (4 × 1) + 14 + (3 × 16) = 80.
- Mass of nitrogen = 2 × 14 = 28.
- Percentage = (28 ÷ 80) × 100 = 35%.

### Common mistakes

- Counting only one nitrogen. NH₄NO₃ has two.

## 1.44 Formulae from reacting masses or percentage composition

### What you need to know

- The **empirical formula** is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.
- Method:
  1. Write the mass (or percentage) of each element.
  2. Divide each by its Aᵣ.
  3. Divide all the answers by the smallest one.
  4. If needed, multiply to get whole numbers.

### Worked example

A compound contains 5.6 g of iron and 2.4 g of oxygen (Fe = 56, O = 16). Find its empirical formula.

| | Fe | O |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mass | 5.6 | 2.4 |
| ÷ Aᵣ | 5.6 ÷ 56 = 0.10 | 2.4 ÷ 16 = 0.15 |
| ÷ smallest | 1 | 1.5 |
| × 2 | 2 | 3 |

Empirical formula: Fe₂O₃.

### Common mistakes

- Rounding 1.5 to 2. Ratios close to .5 must be doubled; only round numbers very close to whole numbers (such as 1.98 → 2).
- Dividing by the atomic number instead of Aᵣ.

## 1.45 Empirical and molecular formulae

### What you need to know

- The **molecular formula** shows the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule.
- (a) The empirical formula can be found from the molecular formula by cancelling to the simplest ratio: C₆H₁₂O₆ → CH₂O.
- (b) The molecular formula can be found from the empirical formula and the Mᵣ: divide Mᵣ by the empirical formula mass, then multiply every subscript by the answer.

### Worked example

A compound has empirical formula CH₂ and Mᵣ = 56. Find its molecular formula.

- Empirical formula mass = 12 + 2 = 14.
- 56 ÷ 14 = 4.
- Molecular formula = C₄H₈.

### Common mistakes

- Dividing Mᵣ by the number of atoms rather than by the empirical formula mass.

## 1.46 Experiment: the empirical formula of magnesium oxide

### What you need to know

- Magnesium is heated in a crucible so it reacts with oxygen from the air. Measuring the mass of magnesium and of the oxide formed lets you find the mass of oxygen that reacted, and so the empirical formula.

### Explained

1. Weigh an empty crucible and lid.
2. Add a coiled strip of cleaned magnesium ribbon and weigh again.
3. Heat strongly on a pipe-clay triangle. Lift the lid slightly from time to time to let air in, but not so far that white smoke (magnesium oxide) escapes.
4. When the magnesium no longer glows, heat a little longer, cool and reweigh.
5. Heat again, cool and reweigh until the mass stays the same (heating to constant mass), to make sure all the magnesium has reacted.

### Worked example

| Measurement | Mass (g) |
| --- | --- |
| crucible + lid | 26.10 |
| crucible + lid + magnesium | 26.34 |
| crucible + lid + magnesium oxide | 26.50 |

- Mass of Mg = 0.24 g; mass of O = 26.50 − 26.34 = 0.16 g.
- Mg: 0.24 ÷ 24 = 0.010. O: 0.16 ÷ 16 = 0.010.
- Ratio 1 : 1, so MgO.

### Common mistakes

- Taking the lid off fully, so magnesium oxide smoke escapes and the oxygen mass is too low.
- Not heating to constant mass, so some magnesium is unreacted.

### Practical

- Hazards: very bright light (do not look directly); hot crucible (use tongs; allow to cool on a heatproof mat). Wear eye protection.

## Check questions

1. Write the formula of (a) aluminium sulfate and (b) ammonium carbonate. (2 marks)
2. Write the ionic equation, with state symbols, for the reaction of barium chloride solution with sodium sulfate solution to make a barium sulfate precipitate. (2 marks)
3. A bottle has the corrosive hazard symbol. Give the danger and one precaution. (2 marks)
4. Calculate the percentage by mass of oxygen in calcium carbonate, CaCO₃ (Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16). (2 marks)
5. A compound contains 40% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen and 53.3% oxygen by mass. Its Mᵣ is 60. Find its empirical and molecular formulae. (4 marks)
6. In the magnesium oxide experiment, explain why the lid is lifted from time to time and why the crucible is heated to constant mass. (2 marks)

## Mark scheme

1. (a) Al₂(SO₄)₃ (1). (b) (NH₄)₂CO₃ (1).
2. Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s) (1 for correct species, 1 for state symbols).
3. Destroys/burns skin and eyes (1); wear eye protection/gloves (1).
4. Mᵣ = 100 (1); (48 ÷ 100) × 100 = 48% (1).
5. C 40 ÷ 12 = 3.33; H 6.7 ÷ 1 = 6.7; O 53.3 ÷ 16 = 3.33 (1); ratio 1 : 2 : 1 so CH₂O (1); empirical formula mass 30, 60 ÷ 30 = 2 (1); C₂H₄O₂ (1).
6. Lid lifted to let oxygen/air in for the magnesium to react (1); constant mass shows all the magnesium has reacted (1).

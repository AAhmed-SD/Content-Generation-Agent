# 4.2 Bonding, structure, and the properties of matter

How atoms are held together decides how a substance behaves. This topic covers ionic, covalent and metallic bonding, the structures they make, why those structures have the properties they do, the forms of carbon, and nanoparticles (Chemistry only).

## 4.2.1.1 Chemical bonds

### What you need to know

There are three types of strong chemical bonds: ionic, covalent and metallic.

- For ionic bonding the particles are oppositely charged ions.
- For covalent bonding the particles are atoms that share pairs of electrons.
- For metallic bonding the particles are atoms that share delocalised electrons.

Ionic bonding occurs in compounds formed from metals combined with non-metals. Covalent bonding occurs in most non-metallic elements and in compounds of non-metals. Metallic bonding occurs in metallic elements and alloys.

You should be able to explain chemical bonding in terms of electrostatic forces and the transfer or sharing of electrons.

### Explained

| Bond | Between | What happens to electrons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ionic | A metal and a non-metal | Transferred from the metal to the non-metal |
| Covalent | Non-metals | Shared in pairs |
| Metallic | Metal atoms | Outer electrons delocalised (free to move) through the whole structure |

## 4.2.1.2 Ionic bonding

### What you need to know

- When a metal atom reacts with a non-metal atom, electrons in the outer shell of the metal atom are transferred. Metal atoms lose electrons to become positively charged ions. Non-metal atoms gain electrons to become negatively charged ions.
- The ions produced by metals in Groups 1 and 2 and by non-metals in Groups 6 and 7 have the electronic structure of a noble gas (Group 0).
- The electron transfer during the formation of an ionic compound can be represented by a dot and cross diagram.
- The charge on the ions produced by metals in Groups 1 and 2 and by non-metals in Groups 6 and 7 relates to the group number of the element in the periodic table.
- You should be able to draw dot and cross diagrams for ionic compounds formed by metals in Groups 1 and 2 with non-metals in Groups 6 and 7, and work out the charge on the ions from the group number.

### Explained

| Group | Electrons lost or gained | Ion charge | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Lose 1 | +1 | Na⁺ |
| 2 | Lose 2 | +2 | Mg²⁺ |
| 6 | Gain 2 | −2 | O²⁻ |
| 7 | Gain 1 | −1 | Cl⁻ |

**Sodium chloride.** Sodium (2,8,1) loses its outer electron to chlorine (2,8,7). Sodium becomes Na⁺ (2,8) and chlorine becomes Cl⁻ (2,8,8). Both now have the electronic structure of a noble gas.

**Magnesium oxide.** Magnesium (2,8,2) gives two electrons to oxygen (2,6). Mg²⁺ (2,8) and O²⁻ (2,8).

In a dot and cross diagram, one atom's electrons are drawn as dots, the other's as crosses. Show the ions in square brackets with the charge outside, top right.

### Common mistakes

- Drawing a shared pair in an ionic compound. Electrons are transferred, not shared.
- Writing the charge inside the brackets, or forgetting the brackets.

## 4.2.1.3 Ionic compounds

### What you need to know

- An ionic compound is a giant structure of ions. Ionic compounds are held together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions. These forces act in all directions in the lattice, and this is called ionic bonding.
- The structure of sodium chloride can be represented in different forms.
- You should be able to deduce that a compound is ionic from a diagram of its structure, describe the limitations of using dot and cross, ball and stick, two- and three-dimensional diagrams to represent a giant ionic structure, and work out the empirical formula of an ionic compound from a given model or diagram that shows the ions in the structure.

### Explained

In sodium chloride, each Na⁺ is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions and each Cl⁻ by six Na⁺. The pattern repeats in all directions: a giant lattice.

| Diagram | Limitation |
| --- | --- |
| Dot and cross | Shows electron transfer but not the arrangement of ions in the lattice |
| Ball and stick | The sticks suggest bonds are physical links; the gaps between ions are much bigger than they really are; shows only a tiny part of the lattice |
| Two-dimensional | Does not show the 3D arrangement |
| Three-dimensional (space-filling) | Shows only the outer layer and a small part of the structure |

**Working out a formula.** The charges must balance. Calcium (Ca²⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻): two Cl⁻ balance one Ca²⁺, so CaCl₂. Aluminium (Al³⁺) and oxide (O²⁻): 2 × (+3) = +6 and 3 × (−2) = −6, so Al₂O₃.

## 4.2.1.4 Covalent bonding

### What you need to know

- When atoms share pairs of electrons, they form covalent bonds. These bonds between atoms are strong.
- Covalently bonded substances may consist of small molecules. Some covalently bonded substances have very large molecules, such as polymers. Some covalently bonded substances have giant covalent structures, such as diamond and silicon dioxide.
- The covalent bonds in molecules and giant structures can be represented in a number of ways.
- You should be able to recognise common substances that consist of small molecules from their chemical formula; draw dot and cross diagrams for the molecules of hydrogen, chlorine, oxygen, nitrogen, hydrogen chloride, water, ammonia and methane; represent the covalent bonds in small molecules, in the repeating units of polymers and in part of giant covalent structures using a line to represent a single bond; describe the limitations of using these representations; and deduce the molecular formula of a substance from a given model or diagram.

### Explained

| Molecule | Formula | Bonds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrogen | H₂ | One single bond (H–H) |
| Chlorine | Cl₂ | One single bond |
| Oxygen | O₂ | One double bond (O=O): two shared pairs |
| Nitrogen | N₂ | One triple bond (N≡N): three shared pairs |
| Hydrogen chloride | HCl | One single bond |
| Water | H₂O | Two single O–H bonds; oxygen also has two non-bonding pairs |
| Ammonia | NH₃ | Three single N–H bonds; nitrogen also has one non-bonding pair |
| Methane | CH₄ | Four single C–H bonds |

Each atom shares enough electrons to fill its outer shell: hydrogen needs 2; carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and chlorine need 8.

Limitations of representations: dot and cross diagrams do not show the shape of the molecule; displayed formulas (with lines) show which atoms are bonded but not the 3D shape or which electrons came from which atom; ball and stick models show shape but not the electrons.

### Common mistakes

- Drawing oxygen with a single bond. O₂ has a double bond.
- Forgetting the non-bonding pairs on oxygen in water.

## 4.2.1.5 Metallic bonding

### What you need to know

- Metals consist of giant structures of atoms arranged in a regular pattern.
- The electrons in the outer shell of metal atoms are delocalised and so are free to move through the whole structure. The sharing of delocalised electrons gives rise to strong metallic bonds.

### Explained

Picture a regular grid of positive metal ions in a "sea" of delocalised electrons. The strong electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons is the metallic bond.

## 4.2.2.1 The three states of matter

### What you need to know

- The three states of matter are solid, liquid and gas. Melting and freezing take place at the melting point; boiling and condensing take place at the boiling point.
- The three states of matter can be represented by a simple model, in which the particles are represented by small solid spheres. Particle theory can help to explain melting, boiling, freezing and condensing.
- The amount of energy needed to change state from solid to liquid and from liquid to gas depends on the strength of the forces between the particles of the substance. The nature of the particles involved depends on the type of bonding and the structure of the substance. The stronger the forces between the particles, the higher the melting point and boiling point of the substance.
- (HT only) Limitations of the simple model include that in the model there are no forces, that all particles are represented as spheres and that the spheres are solid.
- You should be able to predict the states of substances at different temperatures, given appropriate data, and explain the different temperatures at which changes of state occur in terms of energy transfers and types of bonding.

### Explained

| State | Arrangement | Movement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solid | Close together in a regular pattern | Vibrate in fixed positions |
| Liquid | Close together, random | Move around each other |
| Gas | Far apart, random | Move quickly in all directions |

Predicting a state: if room temperature (about 20 °C) is below the melting point, the substance is solid; between the melting and boiling points, liquid; above the boiling point, gas.

(HT only) The model is limited because real particles are not solid spheres, they have forces between them, and the model does not show how far apart the particles really are.

## 4.2.2.2 State symbols

### What you need to know

In chemical equations, the three states of matter are shown as (s), (l) and (g), with (aq) for aqueous solutions. You should be able to include appropriate state symbols in chemical equations for the reactions in this specification.

### Explained

Aqueous means dissolved in water. Example: Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g).

## 4.2.2.3 Properties of ionic compounds

### What you need to know

- Ionic compounds have regular structures (giant ionic lattices) in which there are strong electrostatic forces of attraction in all directions between oppositely charged ions.
- These compounds have high melting points and high boiling points because of the large amounts of energy needed to break the many strong bonds.
- When melted or dissolved in water, ionic compounds conduct electricity because the ions are free to move and so charge can flow.

### Common mistakes

- Saying solid ionic compounds conduct. The ions are fixed in the solid.
- Saying electrons carry the current in an ionic liquid. The ions move.

## 4.2.2.4 Properties of small molecules

### What you need to know

- Substances that consist of small molecules are usually gases or liquids that have relatively low melting points and boiling points.
- These substances have only weak forces between the molecules (intermolecular forces). It is these intermolecular forces that are overcome, not the covalent bonds, when the substance melts or boils.
- The intermolecular forces increase with the size of the molecules, so larger molecules have higher melting and boiling points.
- These substances do not conduct electricity because the molecules do not have an overall electric charge.

### Common mistakes

- Saying covalent bonds break when water boils. The weak intermolecular forces are overcome; the O–H bonds stay intact.

## 4.2.2.5 Polymers

### What you need to know

- Polymers have very large molecules. The atoms in the polymer molecules are linked to other atoms by strong covalent bonds.
- The intermolecular forces between polymer molecules are relatively strong, and so these substances are solids at room temperature.
- You should be able to recognise polymers from diagrams showing their bonding.

### Explained

A polymer is shown by its repeating unit in brackets with an "n" outside, meaning the unit repeats many times. Poly(ethene) is –(CH₂–CH₂)ₙ–.

## 4.2.2.6 Giant covalent structures

### What you need to know

- Substances that consist of giant covalent structures are solids with very high melting points. All of the atoms in these structures are linked to other atoms by strong covalent bonds. These bonds must be overcome to melt or boil these substances.
- Diamond and graphite (forms of carbon) and silicon dioxide (silica) are examples of giant covalent structures.
- You should be able to recognise giant covalent structures from diagrams showing their bonding and structure.

## 4.2.2.7 Properties of metals and alloys

### What you need to know

- Metals have giant structures of atoms with strong metallic bonding. This means that most metals have high melting and boiling points.
- In pure metals, atoms are arranged in layers, which allows metals to be bent and shaped.
- Pure metals are too soft for many uses and so are mixed with other metals to make alloys, which are harder.
- You should be able to explain why alloys are harder than pure metals in terms of the layers of atoms.

### Explained

In a pure metal, all the atoms are the same size, so the layers slide over each other easily. In an alloy, atoms of a different size distort the layers, so they cannot slide as easily. The alloy is harder.

## 4.2.2.8 Metals as conductors

### What you need to know

- Metals are good conductors of electricity because the delocalised electrons in the metal carry electrical charge through the metal.
- Metals are good conductors of thermal energy because energy is transferred by the delocalised electrons.

## 4.2.3.1 Diamond

### What you need to know

- In diamond, each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds with other carbon atoms in a giant covalent structure, so diamond is very hard, has a very high melting point and does not conduct electricity.
- You should be able to explain the properties of diamond in terms of its structure and bonding.

### Explained

All four outer electrons of each carbon are used in bonding, so there are no free electrons to carry charge. Breaking the many strong covalent bonds takes a lot of energy, giving the very high melting point. Uses: cutting tools and drill tips.

## 4.2.3.2 Graphite

### What you need to know

- In graphite, each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds with three other carbon atoms, forming layers of hexagonal rings which have no covalent bonds between the layers.
- In graphite, one electron from each carbon atom is delocalised.
- You should be able to explain the properties of graphite in terms of its structure and bonding, and know that graphite is similar to metals in that it has delocalised electrons.

### Explained

| Property | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Soft and slippery | Only weak forces between the layers, so the layers slide over each other |
| High melting point | Many strong covalent bonds must be broken |
| Conducts electricity and thermal energy | One delocalised electron per carbon atom can move along the layers |

Uses: pencil leads, lubricants, electrodes.

## 4.2.3.3 Graphene and fullerenes

### What you need to know

- Graphene is a single layer of graphite and has properties that make it useful in electronics and composites.
- Fullerenes are molecules of carbon atoms with hollow shapes. The structure of fullerenes is based on hexagonal rings of carbon atoms, but they may also contain rings with five or seven carbon atoms. The first fullerene to be discovered was Buckminsterfullerene (C₆₀), which has a spherical shape.
- Carbon nanotubes are cylindrical fullerenes with very high length to diameter ratios. Their properties make them useful for nanotechnology, electronics and materials.
- You should be able to explain the properties of graphene in terms of its structure and bonding, recognise graphene and fullerenes from diagrams, and give examples of the uses of fullerenes, including carbon nanotubes.

### Explained

Graphene is one atom thick. It is very strong (many strong covalent bonds), very light, and conducts electricity (delocalised electrons).

Uses of fullerenes:

- delivering drugs into the body (molecules can be carried inside the hollow cage)
- lubricants (spherical molecules roll)
- catalysts (large surface area)
- reinforcing materials: nanotubes add strength without much weight, for example in tennis rackets.

## 4.2.4.1 Sizes of particles and their properties (Chemistry only)

### What you need to know

- Nanoscience refers to structures that are 1–100 nm in size, of the order of a few hundred atoms.
- Nanoparticles are smaller than fine particles (PM2.5), which have diameters between 100 and 2500 nm (1 × 10⁻⁷ m and 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m).
- Coarse particles (PM10) have diameters between 1 × 10⁻⁵ m and 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m. Coarse particles are often referred to as dust.
- As the side of a cube decreases by a factor of 10, the surface area to volume ratio increases by a factor of 10.
- Nanoparticles may have properties different from those for the same materials in bulk because of their high surface area to volume ratio. It may also mean that smaller quantities are needed to be effective than for materials with normal particle sizes.
- You should be able to compare "nano" dimensions to typical dimensions of atoms and molecules.

### Explained

| Particle | Diameter |
| --- | --- |
| Atom | About 0.1 nm |
| Nanoparticle | 1 to 100 nm |
| Fine particle (PM2.5) | 100 to 2500 nm |
| Coarse particle (PM10, dust) | 2500 to 10 000 nm |

### Worked example

A cube has sides of 10 nm. Another has sides of 1 nm. Compare their surface area to volume ratios.

- 10 nm cube: surface area = 6 × 10² = 600 nm²; volume = 10³ = 1000 nm³; ratio = 0.6.
- 1 nm cube: surface area = 6 nm²; volume = 1 nm³; ratio = 6.

The side fell by a factor of 10 and the ratio rose by a factor of 10.

## 4.2.4.2 Uses of nanoparticles (Chemistry only)

### What you need to know

- Nanoparticles have many applications in medicine, in electronics, in cosmetics and sun creams, as deodorants, and as catalysts.
- New applications for nanoparticulate materials are an important area of research.
- You should consider advantages and disadvantages of the applications of these nanoparticulate materials, but do not need to know specific examples or properties other than those specified.

### Explained

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Small amounts are effective, because of the large surface area | Long-term effects on health are not fully known |
| Sun creams with nanoparticles give better protection and do not leave a white film | Nanoparticles may be breathed in or pass into cells |
| Catalysts can be more efficient | Could harm the environment when washed away |

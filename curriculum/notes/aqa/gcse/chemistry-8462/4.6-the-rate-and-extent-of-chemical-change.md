# 4.6 The rate and extent of chemical change

Some reactions are fast and some slow; some go both ways. This topic covers measuring rate, what changes it, collision theory, catalysts, reversible reactions and equilibrium.

## 4.6.1.1 Calculating rates of reactions

### What you need to know

- The rate of a chemical reaction can be found by measuring the quantity of a reactant used or the quantity of product formed over time.
- The quantity of reactant or product can be measured by the mass in grams or by a volume in cm³. The units of rate of reaction may be given as g/s or cm³/s.
- (HT only) For the Higher Tier, you are also required to use quantity of reactants in terms of moles and units for rate of reaction in mol/s.
- You should be able to calculate the mean rate of a reaction from given information about the quantity of a reactant used or the quantity of a product formed and the time taken; draw and interpret graphs showing the quantity of product formed or quantity of reactant used up against time; and use the slope of the tangent as a measure of the rate of reaction.
- (HT only) You should be able to calculate the gradient of a tangent to the curve on these graphs as a measure of rate of reaction at a specific time.

```latex
\text{mean rate of reaction} = \frac{\text{quantity of reactant used or product formed}}{\text{time taken}}
```

### Explained

On a graph of product made against time, the curve is steepest at the start (fastest rate), then gets less steep as reactants are used up, and goes flat when the reaction has finished.

(HT only) To find the rate at one moment: draw a tangent (a straight line touching the curve at that point), then calculate its gradient = change in y ÷ change in x, using a large triangle for accuracy.

### Worked example

A reaction makes 60 cm³ of gas in 30 seconds. Mean rate = 60 ÷ 30 = 2 cm³/s.

(HT only) A tangent at 20 s passes through (0 s, 10 cm³) and (40 s, 50 cm³). Gradient = (50 − 10) ÷ (40 − 0) = 1 cm³/s.

## 4.6.1.2 Factors which affect the rates of chemical reactions

### What you need to know

Factors which affect the rates of chemical reactions include:

- the concentrations of reactants in solution
- the pressure of reacting gases
- the surface area of solid reactants
- the temperature
- the presence of catalysts.

You should be able to recall how changing these factors affects the rate of chemical reactions.

### Practical

**Required practical: investigate how changes in concentration affect the rates of reactions by a method involving measuring the volume of a gas produced and a method involving a change in colour or turbidity.**

Method 1, gas volume: magnesium ribbon and hydrochloric acid.

1. Put a set volume of hydrochloric acid of one concentration in a conical flask.
2. Connect a gas syringe (or an upturned measuring cylinder in water).
3. Add a measured length of magnesium ribbon, seal the flask and start a stopwatch.
4. Record the volume of hydrogen every 10 seconds until it stops increasing.
5. Repeat with different concentrations of acid.

Method 2, turbidity: sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid.

1. Draw a black cross on paper and put a conical flask on it.
2. Add a set volume of sodium thiosulfate solution of one concentration.
3. Add hydrochloric acid, start a stopwatch, and swirl.
4. The reaction makes sulfur, which turns the solution cloudy. Stop timing when you can no longer see the cross from above.
5. Repeat with different concentrations of sodium thiosulfate. Rate = 1 ÷ time.

Variables: the independent variable is concentration; the dependent variable is the volume of gas or the time for the cross to disappear; control variables include temperature, volumes, and the mass or length of magnesium. A limitation of method 2 is that judging when the cross disappears varies between people.

## 4.6.1.3 Collision theory and activation energy

### What you need to know

- Collision theory explains how various factors affect rates of reactions. According to this theory, chemical reactions can occur only when reacting particles collide with each other and with sufficient energy. The minimum amount of energy that particles must have to react is called the activation energy.
- Increasing the concentration of reactants in solution, the pressure of reacting gases, and the surface area of solid reactants increases the frequency of collisions and so increases the rate of reaction.
- Increasing the temperature increases the frequency of collisions and makes the collisions more energetic, and so increases the rate of reaction.
- You should be able to predict and explain using collision theory the effects of changing conditions of concentration, pressure and temperature on the rate of a reaction; predict and explain the effects of changes in the size of pieces of a reacting solid in terms of surface area to volume ratio; and use simple ideas about proportionality when using collision theory to explain the effect of a factor on the rate of a reaction.

### Explained

| Change | Effect on particles | Effect on rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Higher concentration | More particles in the same volume, so more frequent collisions | Faster |
| Higher pressure (gases) | Particles closer together, so more frequent collisions | Faster |
| Larger surface area (smaller pieces, powder) | More particles exposed, so more frequent collisions | Faster |
| Higher temperature | Particles move faster: more frequent collisions, and more collisions have at least the activation energy | Faster |
| Catalyst | Lowers the activation energy, so more collisions are successful | Faster |

Proportionality: doubling the concentration roughly doubles the frequency of collisions, so it roughly doubles the rate.

### Common mistakes

- Saying "more collisions". Say "more frequent collisions" (more collisions per second).
- For temperature, giving only the frequency. The key point is more collisions have energy equal to or above the activation energy.

## 4.6.1.4 Catalysts

### What you need to know

- Catalysts change the rate of chemical reactions but are not used up during the reaction. Different reactions need different catalysts. Enzymes act as catalysts in biological systems.
- Catalysts increase the rate of reaction by providing a different pathway for the reaction that has a lower activation energy.
- A reaction profile for a catalysed reaction can be drawn in the form of an energy level diagram.
- You should be able to identify catalysts in reactions from their effect on the rate of reaction and because they are not included in the chemical equation for the reaction, and explain catalytic action in terms of activation energy.

### Explained

On a reaction profile, a catalysed reaction has a lower peak than the uncatalysed reaction. The reactants and products stay at the same energy, so the overall energy change does not change.

## 4.6.2.1 Reversible reactions

### What you need to know

- In some chemical reactions, the products of the reaction can react to produce the original reactants. Such reactions are called reversible reactions and are represented: A + B ⇌ C + D.
- The direction of reversible reactions can be changed by changing the conditions. For example: ammonium chloride ⇌ ammonia + hydrogen chloride. Heating drives the forward reaction; cooling drives the reverse.

## 4.6.2.2 Energy changes and reversible reactions

### What you need to know

- If a reversible reaction is exothermic in one direction, it is endothermic in the opposite direction. The same amount of energy is transferred in each case.
- For example: hydrated copper sulfate (blue) ⇌ anhydrous copper sulfate (white) + water. The forward reaction is endothermic; the reverse is exothermic.

### Explained

Heat blue hydrated copper sulfate and it turns white as water is driven off. Add water to the white powder and it turns blue again and gets hot. This is used as a test for water.

## 4.6.2.3 Equilibrium

### What you need to know

- When a reversible reaction occurs in apparatus which prevents the escape of reactants and products, equilibrium is reached when the forward and reverse reactions occur at exactly the same rate.

### Explained

At equilibrium, the reactions have not stopped. Both are still happening, at the same rate, so the amounts of reactants and products stay constant. This is called dynamic equilibrium. It can only happen in a closed system.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the reaction stops at equilibrium.
- Saying the amounts of reactants and products are equal at equilibrium. They are constant, not necessarily equal.

## 4.6.2.4 The effect of changing conditions on equilibrium (HT only)

### What you need to know

- The relative amounts of all the reactants and products at equilibrium depend on the conditions of the reaction.
- If a system is at equilibrium and a change is made to any of the conditions, then the system responds to counteract the change.
- The effects of changing conditions on a system at equilibrium can be predicted using Le Chatelier's Principle.
- You should be able to make qualitative predictions about the effect of changes on systems at equilibrium when given appropriate information.

## 4.6.2.5 The effect of changing concentration (HT only)

### What you need to know

- If the concentration of one of the reactants or products is changed, the system is no longer at equilibrium and the concentrations of all the substances will change until equilibrium is reached again.
- If the concentration of a reactant is increased, more products will be formed until equilibrium is reached again.
- If the concentration of a product is decreased, more reactants will react until equilibrium is reached again.
- You should be able to interpret appropriate given data to predict the effect of a change in concentration of a reactant or product on given reactions at equilibrium.

## 4.6.2.6 The effect of temperature changes on equilibrium (HT only)

### What you need to know

- If the temperature of a system at equilibrium is increased, the relative amount of products at equilibrium increases for an endothermic reaction, and the relative amount of products at equilibrium decreases for an exothermic reaction.
- If the temperature of a system at equilibrium is decreased, the relative amount of products at equilibrium increases for an exothermic reaction, and the relative amount of products at equilibrium decreases for an endothermic reaction.
- You should be able to interpret appropriate given data to predict the effect of a change in temperature on given reactions at equilibrium.

## 4.6.2.7 The effect of pressure changes on equilibrium (HT only)

### What you need to know

- For a gaseous system at equilibrium, an increase in pressure causes the equilibrium position to shift towards the side with the smaller number of molecules, as shown by the symbol equation for that reaction.
- A decrease in pressure causes the equilibrium position to shift towards the side with the larger number of molecules, as shown by the symbol equation for that reaction.
- You should be able to interpret appropriate given data to predict the effect of pressure changes on given reactions at equilibrium.

### Explained (4.6.2.4 to 4.6.2.7)

The system always shifts to oppose the change.

Example: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g). The forward reaction is exothermic. There are 4 molecules on the left and 2 on the right.

| Change | Equilibrium shifts | Effect on the yield of ammonia |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Add more nitrogen | Right, to use it up | More ammonia |
| Remove ammonia | Right, to replace it | More ammonia |
| Increase temperature | Left (the endothermic direction, to take in the extra energy) | Less ammonia |
| Decrease temperature | Right (the exothermic direction) | More ammonia |
| Increase pressure | Right (fewer molecules) | More ammonia |

A catalyst does not change the position of equilibrium. It only makes equilibrium reached faster.

### Common mistakes

- Saying a catalyst increases the yield.
- Counting atoms instead of molecules when deciding the effect of pressure.

## Check questions

1. A reaction produces 0.90 g of product in 45 seconds. Calculate the mean rate. [1 mark]
2. Explain, using collision theory, why powdered calcium carbonate reacts faster with acid than lumps. [3 marks]
3. Explain why increasing temperature increases the rate of reaction. [3 marks]
4. Describe how to investigate the effect of concentration on the rate of reaction between sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid. [4 marks]
5. Explain how a catalyst increases the rate of reaction. [2 marks]
6. Describe what you would see when water is added to anhydrous copper sulfate, and state the energy change. [2 marks]
7. Explain what is meant by dynamic equilibrium. [2 marks]
8. (HT only) For 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g), where the forward reaction is exothermic, predict and explain the effect on the yield of SO₃ of increasing the pressure. [2 marks]
9. (HT only) For the same reaction, predict and explain the effect of increasing the temperature. [2 marks]
10. (HT only) A tangent to a rate curve passes through (10 s, 20 cm³) and (50 s, 60 cm³). Calculate the rate at that point. [2 marks]

## Mark scheme

1. 0.90 ÷ 45 = 0.020 g/s (1).
2. Powder has a larger surface area (1); more particles are exposed to the acid (1); more frequent collisions, so a faster rate (1).
3. Particles move faster (1); collisions are more frequent (1); more collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy (1).
4. Mix set volumes of sodium thiosulfate and acid in a flask over a black cross (1); time how long until the cross disappears (1); repeat with different concentrations of thiosulfate (1); keep temperature and volumes the same (1).
5. Provides an alternative pathway (1) with a lower activation energy (1).
6. Turns from white to blue (1); exothermic / gets hot (1).
7. Forward and reverse reactions happen at the same rate (1); the amounts of reactants and products stay constant (1).
8. Yield increases (1); equilibrium shifts towards the side with fewer molecules (2 compared with 3) (1).
9. Yield decreases (1); equilibrium shifts in the endothermic (reverse) direction to oppose the temperature rise (1).
10. (60 − 20) ÷ (50 − 10) (1) = 1 cm³/s (1).

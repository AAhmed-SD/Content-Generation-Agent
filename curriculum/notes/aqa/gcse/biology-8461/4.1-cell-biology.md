# 4.1 Cell biology

Cells are the basic unit of all living things. This topic covers what cells contain, how they divide, and how substances move in and out of them.

## 4.1.1.1 Eukaryotes and prokaryotes

### What you need to know

- Plant and animal cells are eukaryotic. They have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, and genetic material inside a nucleus.
- Bacterial cells are prokaryotic. They are much smaller than eukaryotic cells. They have a cell membrane, cytoplasm and a cell wall, but their genetic material is not inside a nucleus.
- A prokaryote's DNA is a single loop in the cytoplasm. Many also have plasmids: small rings of DNA.
- You should be able to compare cell sizes using orders of magnitude.

### Explained

The key difference is the nucleus. A eukaryotic cell keeps its DNA inside a nucleus. A prokaryotic cell has its DNA loose in the cytoplasm.

Sizes: most animal and plant cells are roughly 10 to 100 micrometres (µm) across. Most bacteria are roughly 1 to 10 µm. So a typical eukaryotic cell is about 10 times bigger, or one order of magnitude, than a typical bacterium.

An order of magnitude is a power of 10. If one thing is 10 times bigger than another, it is one order of magnitude bigger. If it is 1000 times bigger, it is three orders of magnitude bigger (10³).

### Worked example

A plant cell is 50 µm long. A bacterium is 0.5 µm long. How many orders of magnitude bigger is the plant cell?

1. 50 ÷ 0.5 = 100.
2. 100 = 10².

Answer: two orders of magnitude.

### Common mistakes

- Saying bacteria have no DNA. They do. It is just not inside a nucleus.
- Saying bacteria have no cell wall. They do, but it is not made of cellulose.
- Forgetting plasmids when asked to name structures found only in prokaryotes.

## 4.1.1.2 Animal and plant cells

### What you need to know

Animal cells and plant cells both contain:

| Structure | Job |
| --- | --- |
| Nucleus | Contains the genetic material that controls the cell's activities |
| Cytoplasm | Gel where most chemical reactions happen, controlled by enzymes |
| Cell membrane | Controls which substances pass in and out |
| Mitochondria | Where aerobic respiration happens, releasing energy |
| Ribosomes | Where proteins are made (protein synthesis) |

Plant cells often also have:

| Structure | Job |
| --- | --- |
| Chloroplasts | Absorb light for photosynthesis; contain chlorophyll |
| Permanent vacuole | Filled with cell sap; keeps the cell firm |
| Cell wall made of cellulose | Strengthens and supports the cell |

Algal cells also have a cellulose cell wall.

- You should be able to explain how the structures in a cell relate to its job, and estimate the sizes of cells and structures.

### Explained

Not every plant cell has chloroplasts. Root cells are underground, get no light and have none. So "plant cells have chloroplasts" is true for leaf cells, not all plant cells.

Ribosomes and mitochondria are too small to see clearly with a light microscope. You see them in electron micrographs.

### Common mistakes

- Saying plant cells have chloroplasts instead of mitochondria. Plant cells have both: they respire all the time.
- Saying the cell wall controls what enters the cell. That is the membrane. The wall only supports.
- Saying the nucleus "makes proteins". Proteins are made at the ribosomes.

## 4.1.1.3 Cell specialisation

### What you need to know

Cells specialise to carry out a particular job. You should be able to explain how the structure of these cells relates to their job:

- Animal: sperm cells, nerve cells, muscle cells.
- Plant: root hair cells, xylem cells, phloem cells.

### Explained

| Cell | Job | Adaptations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sperm cell | Reach and fertilise an egg | Tail to swim; many mitochondria to release energy for swimming; enzymes in the head to digest a way into the egg; nucleus with half the normal genetic material |
| Nerve cell | Carry electrical impulses | Long axon to carry impulses over a distance; branched endings (dendrites) to connect to many other cells |
| Muscle cell | Contract to cause movement | Protein fibres that slide to shorten the cell; many mitochondria to release energy |
| Root hair cell | Absorb water and mineral ions from soil | Long thin projection gives a large surface area; many mitochondria for active transport of mineral ions; no chloroplasts |
| Xylem cell | Carry water and mineral ions up the plant | Dead cells joined end to end into hollow tubes; walls strengthened with lignin; no end walls, so water flows freely |
| Phloem cell | Carry dissolved sugars around the plant | Sieve tube cells with few internal structures and pores in the end walls (sieve plates) so sap flows through; companion cells next to them supply energy |

An exam answer should link each feature to what it does. "Sperm has mitochondria" scores less than "sperm has many mitochondria to release energy for the tail to swim."

### Common mistakes

- Listing a feature without saying how it helps the cell's job.
- Saying xylem carries sugar. Xylem carries water and mineral ions; phloem carries sugars.

## 4.1.1.4 Cell differentiation

### What you need to know

- As an organism develops, cells differentiate to form different types of cells.
- Most types of animal cell differentiate at an early stage.
- Many types of plant cells keep the ability to differentiate throughout life.
- In mature animals, cell division is mainly for repair and replacement.
- As a cell differentiates, it gains new structures that suit its job.

### Explained

Differentiation means a cell becomes specialised. A cell that has differentiated has switched on the genes it needs for its job and switched others off. In animals this mostly happens in the embryo. In plants, the growing tips (meristems) keep making new cells that can become any type, which is why a cutting can grow into a whole new plant.

### Common mistakes

- Mixing up differentiation (becoming specialised) with division (making more cells).

## 4.1.1.5 Microscopy

### What you need to know

- Light microscopes and electron microscopes are used to study cells.
- An electron microscope has much higher magnification and resolving power than a light microscope.
- So an electron microscope can be used to study cells in finer detail, including subcellular structures like ribosomes and the internal structure of mitochondria and chloroplasts.
- You should be able to carry out calculations with magnification, real size and image size, and use standard form and prefixes (milli, micro, nano).

```latex
\text{magnification} = \frac{\text{size of image}}{\text{size of real object}}
```

### Explained

Magnification is how many times bigger the image is than the real object. Resolution is the smallest distance between two points that can still be seen as separate. A higher resolution shows more detail. A light microscope can magnify an image a lot but it will just become blurry: its resolution is limited by the wavelength of light. Electrons have a much shorter wavelength, so an electron microscope resolves much finer detail.

Units:

| Unit | Symbol | In metres | Conversion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| millimetre | mm | 10⁻³ m | 1 mm = 1000 µm |
| micrometre | µm | 10⁻⁶ m | 1 µm = 1000 nm |
| nanometre | nm | 10⁻⁹ m | |

The development of the electron microscope let scientists see subcellular structures for the first time, which changed our understanding of cells.

### Worked example

A drawing of a cell is 24 mm long. The real cell is 80 µm long. Calculate the magnification.

1. Convert to the same unit: 24 mm = 24 000 µm.
2. Magnification = 24 000 ÷ 80 = 300.

Answer: ×300.

A mitochondrion is 0.002 mm long. Write this in standard form in metres.

1. 0.002 mm = 2 × 10⁻³ mm.
2. 1 mm = 10⁻³ m, so 2 × 10⁻³ × 10⁻³ = 2 × 10⁻⁶ m.

Answer: 2 × 10⁻⁶ m (which is 2 µm).

### Common mistakes

- Not converting both sizes to the same unit before dividing.
- Giving magnification with a unit. It has none; write "×300".
- Saying an electron microscope is "clearer". Use the word resolution.

### Practical

**Required practical: using a light microscope to observe, draw and label plant and animal cells.**

1. Peel a thin layer of onion epidermis and lay it flat on a slide in a drop of water.
2. Add a drop of iodine solution as a stain and lower a coverslip at an angle so no air bubbles are trapped.
3. Place the slide on the stage. Start with the lowest-power objective lens.
4. Use the coarse focus to bring the cells into view, then the fine focus to sharpen them.
5. Switch to a higher-power objective and refocus with the fine focus.
6. Draw what you see in pencil, with clear unshaded lines, labels with ruled lines, and the magnification.

Why each step: a thin layer lets light pass through; the stain makes the nucleus and other parts visible; lowering the coverslip at an angle avoids bubbles that look like cells; starting on low power makes the cells easier to find.

Total magnification = eyepiece lens magnification × objective lens magnification.

## 4.1.1.6 Culturing microorganisms (Biology only)

### What you need to know

- Bacteria multiply by simple cell division called binary fission. This can be as often as once every 20 minutes if there are enough nutrients and a suitable temperature.
- Bacteria can be grown in a nutrient broth solution or as colonies on an agar gel plate.
- Uncontaminated cultures are needed to investigate the effect of chemicals such as disinfectants and antibiotics.
- You should be able to describe how to prepare an uncontaminated culture using aseptic technique, and explain why each step is taken.
- You should be able to calculate the number of bacteria in a population after a certain time, and calculate cross-sectional areas of colonies or clear areas using πr².

### Explained

Aseptic technique and why:

| Step | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Sterilise Petri dishes and agar before use (for example in an autoclave) | Kills any bacteria already present |
| Sterilise the inoculating loop by passing it through a flame | Kills microorganisms on the loop |
| Lift the Petri dish lid only slightly, and work near a Bunsen flame | Stops microorganisms in the air getting in |
| Secure the lid with tape, but do not seal it all the way round | Stops microorganisms getting in; oxygen can still enter, so harmful anaerobic bacteria do not grow |
| Store the dish upside down | Stops condensation dripping onto the agar |
| Incubate at 25 °C at most in school | Lowers the chance of growing pathogens that grow at body temperature |

The number of bacteria doubles with each division. After n divisions, one bacterium becomes 2ⁿ bacteria.

```latex
\text{number of bacteria} = \text{starting number} \times 2^{n}
```

### Worked example

A bacterium divides every 30 minutes. Starting with 1 bacterium, how many are there after 4 hours? Give your answer in standard form.

1. 4 hours = 240 minutes. 240 ÷ 30 = 8 divisions.
2. 2⁸ = 256.
3. 256 = 2.56 × 10².

Answer: 2.56 × 10² bacteria.

A clear zone around an antibiotic disc has a diameter of 18 mm. Calculate its area.

1. Radius = 18 ÷ 2 = 9 mm.
2. Area = π × 9² = 254.5 mm² (to 1 d.p.).

### Common mistakes

- Using the diameter instead of the radius in πr².
- Counting the number of divisions wrong: count how many times the generation time fits into the total time.
- Saying the lid is sealed completely. It is taped, not sealed.

### Practical

**Required practical (Biology only): investigating the effect of antiseptics or antibiotics on bacterial growth using agar plates and measuring zones of inhibition.**

1. Spread a bacterial culture evenly over an agar plate using aseptic technique.
2. Soak paper discs in different antiseptics or antibiotics, plus one disc in sterile water as a control.
3. Place the discs on the agar, tape the lid and incubate at 25 °C for about 48 hours.
4. Measure the diameter of the clear zone around each disc and calculate its area.

The larger the clear zone, the more effective the substance at killing bacteria or stopping their growth. The water disc shows that any clear zone is caused by the substance, not the disc.

## 4.1.2.1 Chromosomes

### What you need to know

- The nucleus of a cell contains chromosomes made of DNA molecules.
- Each chromosome carries a large number of genes.
- In body cells, chromosomes are normally found in pairs.

### Explained

A gene is a short section of DNA that codes for a particular protein. One chromosome is one very long DNA molecule, coiled up, carrying hundreds or thousands of genes. Human body cells have 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs: one chromosome of each pair came from the mother, the other from the father.

## 4.1.2.2 Mitosis and the cell cycle

### What you need to know

Cells divide in a series of stages called the cell cycle:

1. **Growth and DNA replication.** The cell grows. It increases the number of subcellular structures such as ribosomes and mitochondria. The DNA replicates to form two copies of each chromosome.
2. **Mitosis.** One set of chromosomes is pulled to each end of the cell, and the nucleus divides.
3. **Cytokinesis.** The cytoplasm and cell membrane divide to form two identical daughter cells.

- Cell division by mitosis is important for growth and development of multicellular organisms.
- You should be able to recognise and describe situations where mitosis happens.

### Explained

The two daughter cells are genetically identical to each other and to the parent cell. Mitosis happens when an organism grows, when damaged tissue is repaired (such as a cut healing), and in asexual reproduction.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the DNA is copied during mitosis. It is copied before, in the growth stage.
- Saying mitosis makes four cells. Mitosis makes two identical cells. Meiosis makes four non-identical gametes.
- Saying the daughter cells have half the chromosomes. They have the full number.

## 4.1.2.3 Stem cells

### What you need to know

- A stem cell is an undifferentiated cell that can give rise to many more cells of the same type, and certain other cells can arise from stem cells by differentiation.
- Stem cells from human embryos can be cloned and made to differentiate into most different types of human cells.
- Stem cells from adult bone marrow can form many types of cells, including blood cells.
- Meristem tissue in plants can differentiate into any type of plant cell, throughout the life of the plant.
- Treatment with stem cells may be able to help conditions such as diabetes and paralysis.
- In therapeutic cloning, an embryo is produced with the same genes as the patient. Stem cells from the embryo are not rejected by the patient's body, so they may be used for medical treatment.
- The use of stem cells has potential risks, such as transfer of viral infection, and some people have ethical or religious objections.
- Stem cells from meristems can be used to produce clones of plants quickly and cheaply.

### Explained

| Source | What it can become | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Human embryo | Most types of human cell | Most useful for treatment; ethical objections because the embryo is destroyed |
| Adult bone marrow | A limited range, such as blood cells | Fewer ethical issues; already used in bone marrow transplants |
| Plant meristem | Any type of plant cell, all through life | Used to clone plants |

Uses of plant cloning from meristems: protecting rare species from extinction, and producing large numbers of identical crop plants with useful features such as disease resistance.

An "evaluate" question on stem cells needs points for and against, then a judgement.

- For: could replace damaged cells (for example insulin-producing cells in diabetes, nerve cells in paralysis); therapeutic cloning avoids rejection.
- Against: risk of transferring viral infection; ethical and religious objections to using embryos; treatments are still being developed and may not work; possible risk of the cells growing out of control.

### Common mistakes

- Saying adult stem cells can become any cell type.
- Giving only one side in an evaluate question.

## 4.1.3.1 Diffusion

### What you need to know

- Substances move into and out of cells across cell membranes by diffusion.
- Diffusion is the spreading out of the particles of any substance in a solution, or particles of a gas, resulting in a net movement from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration.
- Examples: oxygen and carbon dioxide in gas exchange, and the waste product urea diffusing from cells into the blood plasma to be taken to the kidney.
- Factors that affect the rate of diffusion: the difference in concentration (concentration gradient), the temperature, and the surface area of the membrane.
- A single-celled organism has a relatively large surface area to volume ratio, so diffusion across its membrane can meet its needs.
- In multicellular organisms, surfaces and organ systems are specialised for exchanging materials. Exchange surfaces are made effective by: a large surface area; a membrane that is thin, giving a short diffusion path; (in animals) an efficient blood supply; (in animals, for gas exchange) being ventilated.
- You should be able to calculate and compare surface area to volume ratios.

### Explained

Diffusion is passive: it needs no energy from the cell. It happens because particles move about randomly, so more move from where there are many to where there are few than the other way.

| Factor | Change | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concentration gradient | Steeper | Faster diffusion |
| Temperature | Higher | Faster, because particles have more kinetic energy |
| Surface area | Larger | Faster, because more particles can cross at once |

Examples of exchange surfaces and their adaptations:

| Surface | Adaptations |
| --- | --- |
| Lungs (alveoli) | Huge total surface area; walls one cell thick; rich blood supply; ventilated by breathing |
| Small intestine (villi) | Villi and microvilli give a large surface area; thin lining; rich blood supply |
| Fish gills | Gill filaments and lamellae give a large surface area; thin; rich blood supply; water flows over them |
| Leaves | Flat and thin; stomata let gases in and out; air spaces inside |
| Plant roots | Root hair cells give a large surface area |

### Worked example

Compare the surface area to volume ratio of a cube with 1 cm sides and a cube with 3 cm sides.

- 1 cm cube: surface area = 6 × 1 × 1 = 6 cm². Volume = 1 cm³. Ratio = 6 : 1.
- 3 cm cube: surface area = 6 × 3 × 3 = 54 cm². Volume = 27 cm³. Ratio = 54 : 27 = 2 : 1.

The smaller cube has the larger ratio. As organisms get bigger, their surface area to volume ratio falls, so they need exchange surfaces and transport systems.

### Common mistakes

- Leaving out "net" movement in the definition.
- Saying diffusion needs energy.
- Saying "large surface area" without saying it speeds up diffusion.

## 4.1.3.2 Osmosis

### What you need to know

- Water may move across cell membranes by osmosis.
- Osmosis is the diffusion of water from a dilute solution to a concentrated solution through a partially permeable membrane.

### Explained

A dilute solution has a lot of water and little solute. A concentrated solution has less water and more solute. Water moves by osmosis from the dilute side to the concentrated side.

What happens to cells:

| Solution outside the cell | Animal cell | Plant cell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| More dilute than the cell | Water moves in; the cell swells and may burst | Water moves in; the cell becomes firm (turgid); the wall stops it bursting |
| Same concentration | No net movement | No net movement |
| More concentrated than the cell | Water moves out; the cell shrinks | Water moves out; the cell becomes floppy, and the membrane can pull away from the wall |

### Practical

**Required practical: investigating the effect of a range of concentrations of salt or sugar solutions on the mass of plant tissue.**

1. Use a cork borer to cut potato cylinders of the same diameter, and trim them to the same length.
2. Blot each cylinder dry and measure its starting mass.
3. Put each cylinder in a different concentration of sugar solution (for example 0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 mol/dm³) for the same time, at the same temperature.
4. Remove, blot dry gently, and measure the final mass.
5. Calculate the percentage change in mass for each.
6. Plot percentage change in mass against concentration.

```latex
\text{percentage change in mass} = \frac{\text{final mass} - \text{starting mass}}{\text{starting mass}} \times 100
```

Where the line crosses the x-axis (0% change), the solution has the same concentration as the potato cells. Percentage change is used, not change in mass, because the cylinders start at slightly different masses.

Variables: the independent variable is concentration; the dependent variable is the percentage change in mass; control variables include the cylinder size, the potato, the time and the temperature.

### Worked example

A potato cylinder's mass goes from 4.00 g to 4.36 g. Calculate the percentage change.

1. Change = 4.36 − 4.00 = 0.36 g.
2. 0.36 ÷ 4.00 × 100 = 9%.

Answer: +9%. The solution was more dilute than the potato cells, so water moved into the potato by osmosis.

### Common mistakes

- Saying water moves from "high concentration to low concentration" without saying of what. Say from a dilute solution to a concentrated solution.
- Saying salt or sugar moves by osmosis. Osmosis is water only.
- Not blotting the cylinders before weighing, which adds surface water to the mass.

## 4.1.3.3 Active transport

### What you need to know

- Active transport moves substances from a more dilute solution to a more concentrated solution, against a concentration gradient.
- This requires energy from respiration.
- Active transport lets mineral ions be absorbed into plant root hairs from very dilute solutions in the soil. Plants need these ions for healthy growth.
- It also lets sugar molecules be absorbed from lower concentrations in the gut into the blood, which has a higher sugar concentration. Sugar is used for cell respiration.
- You should be able to describe how substances are transported into and out of cells by diffusion, osmosis and active transport, and explain the differences between them.

### Explained

| | Diffusion | Osmosis | Active transport |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What moves | Any dissolved substance or gas | Water only | Particular substances, such as mineral ions and glucose |
| Direction | High to low concentration | Dilute to concentrated solution | Low to high concentration, against the gradient |
| Energy from respiration? | No | No | Yes |
| Membrane needed? | Not always | Partially permeable membrane | Yes, with carrier proteins |

Cells that do a lot of active transport, such as root hair cells and cells lining the small intestine, have many mitochondria to release the energy needed.

### Common mistakes

- Saying active transport "uses energy" without saying the energy comes from respiration.
- Saying active transport moves substances "down" a gradient.

# 4.2 Organisation

Cells are organised into tissues, organs and organ systems. This topic covers digestion, the heart and blood, non-communicable disease, and how plants are organised to transport water and sugar.

## 4.2.1 Principles of organisation

### What you need to know

- Cells are the basic building blocks of all living organisms.
- A tissue is a group of cells with a similar structure and function.
- An organ is an aggregation of tissues performing a specific function.
- Organs are organised into organ systems, which work together to form organisms.

### Explained

The levels, smallest first: cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism.

Example: muscle cells form muscle tissue; the stomach is an organ made of muscle tissue, glandular tissue (makes digestive juices) and epithelial tissue (covers the inside and outside); the stomach is part of the digestive system; the digestive system is one of the organ systems in a human.

### Common mistakes

- Calling the stomach a tissue. It is an organ: it contains several tissues.

## 4.2.2.1 The human digestive system

### What you need to know

- The digestive system is an organ system in which several organs work together to digest and absorb food.
- Enzymes catalyse specific reactions in living organisms because of the shape of their active site.
- You should be able to use the "lock and key theory" as a simple model of enzyme action.
- You should be able to explain the effect of temperature and pH on enzymes.
- Digestive enzymes convert food into small soluble molecules that can be absorbed into the bloodstream.
  - Carbohydrases break down carbohydrates to simple sugars. Amylase is a carbohydrase that breaks down starch.
  - Proteases break down proteins to amino acids.
  - Lipases break down lipids (fats) to glycerol and fatty acids.
- The products of digestion are used to build new carbohydrates, lipids and proteins. Some glucose is used in respiration.
- Bile is made in the liver and stored in the gall bladder. It is alkaline, to neutralise hydrochloric acid from the stomach. It also emulsifies fat to form small droplets, which increases the surface area. Both of these increase the rate of fat breakdown by lipase.
- You should be able to calculate the rate of chemical reactions.

### Explained

**How enzymes work.** An enzyme is a protein that acts as a biological catalyst: it speeds up a reaction without being used up. Each enzyme has an active site with a particular shape. Only a substrate with the matching shape fits into it, like a key in a lock. The substrate binds, the reaction happens, and the products leave. The enzyme is unchanged and can be used again.

**Temperature.** As temperature rises, molecules move faster and collide more often, so the rate rises. Above the optimum temperature (about 37 °C for human enzymes), the bonds holding the enzyme's shape break. The active site changes shape so the substrate no longer fits. The enzyme is denatured, and the rate falls quickly. Denaturing is permanent.

**pH.** Each enzyme has an optimum pH. Too far either side changes the shape of the active site and denatures the enzyme. Protease in the stomach (pepsin) works best in acid, around pH 2. Enzymes in the small intestine work best in slightly alkaline conditions.

**Where the enzymes are made:**

| Enzyme | Made in | Breaks down | Into |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Amylase (a carbohydrase) | Salivary glands, pancreas, small intestine | Starch | Sugars (maltose, then glucose) |
| Protease | Stomach, pancreas, small intestine | Proteins | Amino acids |
| Lipase | Pancreas, small intestine | Lipids | Glycerol and fatty acids |

**The organs in order:** mouth (chewing; amylase in saliva) → oesophagus → stomach (protease; hydrochloric acid kills bacteria and gives the right pH for stomach protease; muscles churn food) → small intestine (enzymes from the pancreas and the intestine itself finish digestion; villi absorb the products into the blood) → large intestine (water absorbed) → rectum and anus. The liver makes bile and the gall bladder stores it.

```latex
\text{rate of reaction} = \frac{1}{\text{time taken}}
```

### Worked example

Amylase took 40 seconds to break down all the starch at pH 6 and 120 seconds at pH 4. Calculate the rate at each pH.

- pH 6: 1 ÷ 40 = 0.025 s⁻¹.
- pH 4: 1 ÷ 120 = 0.0083 s⁻¹ (2 s.f.).

The reaction was three times faster at pH 6.

### Common mistakes

- Saying enzymes are "killed". Enzymes are not alive; they are denatured.
- Saying the substrate is denatured. The enzyme is.
- Saying bile is an enzyme, or that it digests fat. Bile emulsifies fat, which speeds up digestion by lipase.
- Saying lipids break down into "fatty acids" only. Include glycerol.

### Practical

**Required practical: use qualitative reagents to test for carbohydrates, lipids and proteins.**

| Test for | Reagent | Method | Positive result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Starch | Iodine solution | Add a few drops to the food sample | Orange-brown turns blue-black |
| Sugars (reducing sugars such as glucose) | Benedict's solution | Add to the food solution and heat in a water bath at about 75 °C for 5 minutes | Blue turns green, yellow or brick red, depending on how much sugar |
| Protein | Biuret reagent | Add to the food solution | Blue turns purple (lilac) |
| Lipids | Sudan III stain | Add a few drops to the food solution and shake | A red-stained oily layer forms on top |

Safety: wear eye protection; take care with the hot water bath; Biuret reagent contains an irritant.

**Required practical: investigate the effect of pH on the rate of reaction of amylase.**

1. Put a drop of iodine solution into each well of a spotting tile.
2. Put amylase solution, a pH buffer and starch solution in separate tubes in a water bath at a set temperature (for example 35 °C) so they reach the same temperature.
3. Mix the amylase and buffer with the starch and start a stopwatch.
4. Every 10 seconds, put a drop of the mixture onto a fresh iodine well.
5. When the iodine stays orange-brown, all the starch has been broken down. Record the time.
6. Repeat with buffers of different pH.

The independent variable is pH; the dependent variable is the time taken for starch to disappear; control variables include temperature, volumes and concentrations of amylase and starch.

## 4.2.2.2 The heart and blood vessels

### What you need to know

- You should know the structure and functioning of the human heart and lungs, including how lungs are adapted for gaseous exchange.
- The heart is an organ that pumps blood around the body in a double circulatory system. The right ventricle pumps blood to the lungs, where gas exchange takes place. The left ventricle pumps blood around the rest of the body.
- The natural resting heart rate is controlled by a group of cells located in the right atrium that act as a pacemaker. Artificial pacemakers are electrical devices used to correct irregularities in the heart rate.
- The body contains three different types of blood vessel: arteries, veins and capillaries. You should be able to explain how their structure relates to their functions.
- You should be able to use simple compound measures such as rate, and carry out rate calculations for blood flow.

### Explained

**The heart.** Blood flows: body → vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body. Valves stop blood flowing backwards. The left ventricle wall is thicker than the right because it pumps blood all round the body at higher pressure, not just to the lungs next door. The coronary arteries branch off the aorta and supply the heart muscle itself with oxygenated blood.

Double circulation means blood passes through the heart twice in each full circuit: once to the lungs and once to the body. This keeps blood pressure high for delivering oxygen to the body.

**The lungs.** Air passes down the trachea, into two bronchi, then smaller bronchioles, ending in tiny air sacs called alveoli. Oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli into the blood; carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveoli. Alveoli are adapted for gas exchange: a very large total surface area, walls only one cell thick for a short diffusion path, a rich network of capillaries to keep the concentration gradient steep, and ventilation by breathing.

**Blood vessels.**

| Vessel | Job | Structure, and why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Artery | Carries blood away from the heart at high pressure | Thick walls of muscle and elastic fibres to withstand and even out the high pressure; narrow lumen |
| Vein | Carries blood back to the heart at low pressure | Thin walls; large lumen to reduce resistance to flow; valves to stop backflow |
| Capillary | Exchanges substances with body cells | Walls one cell thick, so diffusion is fast; very narrow; huge network so every cell is near one |

```latex
\text{rate of blood flow} = \frac{\text{volume of blood}}{\text{time}}
```

### Worked example

1500 cm³ of blood flows through an artery in 5 minutes. Calculate the rate of blood flow in cm³ per second.

1. 5 minutes = 300 seconds.
2. 1500 ÷ 300 = 5 cm³/s.

### Common mistakes

- Saying arteries carry oxygenated blood and veins carry deoxygenated blood. That is usually true, but the definition is about direction: arteries carry blood away from the heart. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood.
- Saying veins have thick walls.
- Saying the left side of the heart pumps to the lungs. The right side does.

## 4.2.2.3 Blood

### What you need to know

- Blood is a tissue consisting of plasma, in which the red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets are suspended.
- You should know the functions of each of these blood components.
- You should be able to recognise different types of blood cells in a photograph or diagram, and explain how they are adapted to their functions.

### Explained

| Part | Job | Adaptations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plasma | Pale yellow liquid that carries blood cells, dissolved food (glucose, amino acids), carbon dioxide, urea, hormones and antibodies | Mostly water, so substances dissolve in it |
| Red blood cells | Carry oxygen from the lungs to the body | Contain haemoglobin, which binds oxygen; no nucleus, leaving more room for haemoglobin; biconcave disc shape gives a large surface area for oxygen to diffuse in and out |
| White blood cells | Part of the immune system; defend against pathogens | Have a nucleus; some engulf pathogens, some make antibodies or antitoxins |
| Platelets | Help blood clot at a wound | Small fragments of cells with no nucleus |

In a diagram: red blood cells are many, round, with a pale centre and no nucleus; white blood cells are larger and have a nucleus, often lobed; platelets are tiny fragments.

### Common mistakes

- Calling platelets cells. They are cell fragments.
- Saying plasma carries oxygen. Red blood cells carry almost all the oxygen.

## 4.2.2.4 Coronary heart disease: a non-communicable disease

### What you need to know

- In coronary heart disease, layers of fatty material build up inside the coronary arteries, narrowing them. This reduces the flow of blood through the coronary arteries, resulting in a lack of oxygen for the heart muscle.
- Stents are used to keep the coronary arteries open.
- Statins are widely used to reduce blood cholesterol levels, which slows down the rate of fatty material deposit.
- In some people heart valves may become faulty, preventing the valve from opening fully, or the heart valve might develop a leak. Faulty heart valves can be replaced using biological or mechanical valves.
- In the case of heart failure, a donor heart, or heart and lungs, can be transplanted. Artificial hearts are occasionally used to keep patients alive while waiting for a transplant, or to allow the heart to rest as an aid to recovery.
- You should be able to evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of treating cardiovascular diseases by drugs, mechanical devices or transplant.

### Explained

| Treatment | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stent (a mesh tube inserted into the artery to hold it open) | Quick effect; minor surgery with a fast recovery; lasts a long time | Risk of complications from surgery, infection or blood clots; the artery can narrow again |
| Statins | No surgery; reduce the risk of heart attack and stroke; can raise "good" cholesterol | Must be taken long-term; side effects such as muscle pain or liver problems; take time to work |
| Replacement valve: biological (from a pig or cow, or a human donor) | No need for lifelong anti-clotting drugs | Wears out after some years; may need replacing |
| Replacement valve: mechanical | Lasts a long time | Patient needs drugs to stop blood clots for life; can make a clicking sound |
| Heart transplant | Can save a patient with heart failure | Shortage of donors; risk of rejection, so immunosuppressant drugs for life; major surgery |
| Artificial heart | Keeps a patient alive until a donor is found; less risk of rejection, as it is not living tissue | Temporary; risk of blood clots and infection; patient needs to carry a power source |

### Common mistakes

- Saying fatty deposits build up in the heart. They build up in the coronary arteries.
- Saying coronary heart disease is communicable.

## 4.2.2.5 Health issues

### What you need to know

- Health is the state of physical and mental well-being.
- Diseases, both communicable and non-communicable, are major causes of ill health. Other factors including diet, stress and life situations may have a profound effect on both physical and mental health.
- Different types of disease may interact:
  - defects in the immune system mean that an individual is more likely to suffer from infectious diseases
  - viruses living in cells can be the trigger for cancers
  - immune reactions initially caused by a pathogen can trigger allergies such as skin rashes and asthma
  - severe physical ill health can lead to depression and other mental illness.
- You should be able to read and interpret information from graphs, tables and charts, and use a scatter diagram to identify a correlation between two variables. You should understand the principles of sampling as applied to scientific data, including epidemiological data.

### Explained

A communicable disease can spread from person to person or between animals and people, because it is caused by a pathogen. A non-communicable disease cannot spread, for example coronary heart disease, most cancers and type 2 diabetes.

Epidemiology is the study of patterns of disease in populations. A good study uses a large, random sample so the results represent the whole population.

### Common mistakes

- Defining health as "not being ill". Health includes mental well-being.

## 4.2.2.6 The effect of lifestyle on some non-communicable diseases

### What you need to know

- Risk factors are linked to an increased rate of a disease. They can be aspects of a person's lifestyle, or substances in the person's body or environment.
- A causal mechanism has been proven for some risk factors, but not in others.
  - The effects of diet, smoking and exercise on cardiovascular disease.
  - Obesity as a risk factor for type 2 diabetes.
  - The effect of alcohol on the liver and brain function.
  - The effect of smoking on lung disease and lung cancer.
  - The effects of smoking and alcohol on unborn babies.
  - Carcinogens, including ionising radiation, as risk factors in cancer.
- Many diseases are caused by the interaction of a number of factors.
- You should be able to discuss the human and financial cost of these diseases to individuals, local communities, nations and globally, and explain the effect of lifestyle factors on the incidence of non-communicable diseases at local, national and global levels.

### Explained

| Risk factor | Disease |
| --- | --- |
| Diet high in saturated fat; lack of exercise; smoking | Cardiovascular disease |
| Obesity | Type 2 diabetes |
| Too much alcohol | Liver disease (cirrhosis); damage to brain function |
| Smoking | Lung disease (such as bronchitis and emphysema); lung cancer |
| Smoking and alcohol during pregnancy | Harm to the unborn baby, such as low birth weight |
| Carcinogens, including ionising radiation (such as UV light and X-rays) | Cancer |

A correlation between a risk factor and a disease does not prove the factor causes the disease. A causal mechanism is the explanation of how one leads to the other. For example, scientists showed how chemicals in tobacco smoke damage DNA in lung cells, which proved that smoking causes lung cancer.

Costs: to individuals (pain, shorter life, loss of income); to communities and nations (care costs for the health service, lost working days); globally (a heavy burden in poorer countries where treatment is less available).

### Common mistakes

- Saying a correlation proves a cause.
- Saying a risk factor means you will get the disease. It only increases the chance.

## 4.2.2.7 Cancer

### What you need to know

- Cancer is the result of changes in cells that lead to uncontrolled growth and division.
- Benign tumours are growths of abnormal cells that are contained in one area, usually within a membrane. They do not invade other parts of the body.
- Malignant tumour cells are cancers. They invade neighbouring tissues and spread to different parts of the body in the blood, where they form secondary tumours.
- Scientists have identified lifestyle risk factors for various types of cancer. There are also genetic risk factors for some cancers.

### Explained

| | Benign tumour | Malignant tumour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spread | Stays in one place, usually inside a membrane | Invades nearby tissue; spreads through the blood |
| Secondary tumours | No | Yes |
| Is it cancer? | No | Yes |
| Can it be harmful? | Yes, if it presses on an organ such as the brain | Yes |

Lifestyle risk factors include smoking, obesity, UV exposure and some viral infections (such as HPV and cervical cancer). Genetic risk factors include inherited faulty genes, such as BRCA genes linked to breast cancer.

### Common mistakes

- Saying benign tumours are always harmless.
- Saying cancer is caused by cells dividing too fast. The key is uncontrolled division.

## 4.2.3.1 Plant tissues

### What you need to know

Plant tissues include:

- epidermal tissues
- palisade mesophyll
- spongy mesophyll
- xylem and phloem
- meristem tissue found at the growing tips of shoots and roots.

The leaf is a plant organ. You should know the structure of the tissues in a leaf, and explain how their structure relates to their function.

### Explained

| Tissue | Job | Adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Upper epidermis | Covers the leaf | Transparent, so light passes through; covered in a waxy cuticle that reduces water loss |
| Palisade mesophyll | Main site of photosynthesis | Tall cells packed with chloroplasts, near the top of the leaf where light is strongest |
| Spongy mesophyll | Gas exchange | Loosely packed with air spaces, so gases diffuse quickly to and from cells |
| Lower epidermis | Covers the underside | Contains stomata, surrounded by guard cells, for gas exchange |
| Xylem | Carries water and mineral ions | Hollow tubes strengthened by lignin |
| Phloem | Carries dissolved sugars | Sieve tubes with pores in the end walls |
| Meristem | Makes new cells at shoot and root tips | Cells divide by mitosis and can differentiate into any plant cell |

Guard cells open the stomata to let carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis and close them to reduce water loss.

## 4.2.3.2 Plant organ system

### What you need to know

- The roots, stem and leaves form a plant organ system for transport of substances around the plant.
- Root hair cells, xylem and phloem are adapted to their functions.
- Xylem tissue transports water and mineral ions from the roots to the stems and leaves. It is composed of hollow tubes strengthened by lignin, adapted for the transport of water in the transpiration stream.
- The role of stomata and guard cells is to control gas exchange and water loss.
- Phloem tissue transports dissolved sugars from the leaves to the rest of the plant for immediate use or storage. The movement of food molecules through phloem tissue is called translocation.
- Phloem is composed of tubes of elongated cells. Cell sap can move from one phloem cell to the next through pores in the end walls.
- The rate of transpiration is affected by temperature, humidity, air movement and light intensity.
- You should be able to explain the effect of changing these factors on the rate of transpiration, and understand and use simple compound measures such as the rate of transpiration. You should be able to calculate surface area and mean values, and use them in estimating rates.

### Explained

**Transpiration** is the loss of water vapour from the leaves, mostly through the stomata. Water evaporates from cells inside the leaf and diffuses out. This pulls more water up the xylem from the roots, in a continuous flow called the transpiration stream. It also carries mineral ions up the plant.

| Factor | Change | Effect on transpiration | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | Higher | Faster | Water molecules have more energy, so evaporation and diffusion are faster |
| Humidity | Higher | Slower | The air already holds more water vapour, so the concentration gradient is smaller |
| Air movement (wind) | More | Faster | Water vapour is blown away from the leaf, keeping the gradient steep |
| Light intensity | Higher | Faster | More stomata open to let in carbon dioxide for photosynthesis, so more water escapes |

**Measuring transpiration.** A potometer measures water uptake by a cut shoot. As the shoot takes up water, an air bubble moves along a capillary tube. The distance moved in a set time gives the rate of water uptake, which is close to the rate of transpiration (a little water is used in photosynthesis).

**Translocation** moves sugars, made by photosynthesis in the leaves, through the phloem to where they are used or stored, such as growing tips and roots. It can move both up and down the plant.

### Worked example

In a potometer, the bubble moves 30 mm in 5 minutes. Calculate the rate of water uptake in mm per minute.

30 ÷ 5 = 6 mm per minute.

### Common mistakes

- Saying transpiration happens in the roots. It is loss of water from the leaves.
- Saying phloem carries water up the plant.
- Saying higher humidity speeds up transpiration.

# 4.7 Ecology

Living things depend on each other and on their surroundings. This topic covers communities, adaptations, food chains, sampling, the carbon and water cycles, decay, human impact on biodiversity, and (Biology only) trophic levels, biomass and food production.

## 4.7.1.1 Communities

### What you need to know

- An ecosystem is the interaction of a community of living organisms (biotic) with the non-living (abiotic) parts of their environment.
- To survive and reproduce, organisms require a supply of materials from their surroundings and from the other living organisms there.
- Plants in a community or habitat often compete with each other for light and space, and for water and mineral ions from the soil.
- Animals often compete with each other for food, mates and territory.
- Within a community each species depends on other species for food, shelter, pollination, seed dispersal and so on. If one species is removed it can affect the whole community. This is called interdependence.
- A stable community is one where all the species and environmental factors are in balance, so that population sizes remain fairly constant.
- You should be able to extract and interpret information from charts, graphs and tables relating to the interaction of organisms within a community.

### Explained

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Habitat | The place where an organism lives |
| Population | All the organisms of one species in a habitat |
| Community | All the populations of different species in a habitat |
| Ecosystem | A community plus its non-living environment |

Example of interdependence: if bees die out, plants they pollinate produce fewer seeds; animals that eat those seeds or fruits have less food; their predators have less food too.

Tropical rainforests and ancient oak woodland are examples of stable communities.

## 4.7.1.2 Abiotic factors

### What you need to know

Abiotic (non-living) factors which can affect a community are:

- light intensity
- temperature
- moisture levels
- soil pH and mineral content
- wind intensity and direction
- carbon dioxide levels for plants
- oxygen levels for aquatic animals.

You should be able to explain how a change in an abiotic factor would affect a given community.

### Explained

Example: if light intensity falls (for example, under a thick tree canopy), plants photosynthesise less and grow less; animals that eat them have less food; so populations through the food chain may fall.

## 4.7.1.3 Biotic factors

### What you need to know

Biotic (living) factors which can affect a community are:

- availability of food
- new predators arriving
- new pathogens
- one species outcompeting another so the numbers are no longer sufficient to breed.

You should be able to explain how a change in a biotic factor might affect a given community.

### Explained

Example: grey squirrels were introduced to the UK. They outcompete red squirrels for food and carry a virus that kills red squirrels. Red squirrel numbers have fallen sharply.

## 4.7.1.4 Adaptations

### What you need to know

- Organisms have features (adaptations) that enable them to survive in the conditions in which they normally live. These adaptations may be structural, behavioural or functional.
- Some organisms live in environments that are very extreme, such as at high temperature, pressure or salt concentration. These organisms are called extremophiles. Bacteria living in deep sea vents are extremophiles.
- You should be able to explain how organisms are adapted to live in their natural environment, given appropriate information.

### Explained

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structural | Features of the body | Thick fur and small ears in Arctic animals (less energy lost); large ears in desert animals (more energy lost); camouflage; cacti with spines instead of leaves (less water lost) |
| Behavioural | The way an organism acts | Migrating to warmer places; hunting at night in hot deserts; huddling together for warmth |
| Functional | Processes inside the body | Desert animals producing very concentrated urine; hibernating animals lowering their metabolism |

An exam answer should name the feature and explain how it helps survival in that environment.

## 4.7.2.1 Levels of organisation

### What you need to know

- Feeding relationships within a community can be represented by food chains. All food chains begin with a producer which synthesises molecules. This is usually a green plant or alga which makes glucose by photosynthesis.
- A range of experimental methods using transects and quadrats are used by ecologists to determine the distribution and abundance of species in an ecosystem.
- In relation to abundance of organisms, you should be able to understand the terms mean, mode and median, calculate arithmetic means, and plot and draw appropriate graphs selecting appropriate scales for the axes.
- Producers are eaten by primary consumers, which in turn may be eaten by secondary consumers and then tertiary consumers.
- Consumers that kill and eat other animals are predators, and those eaten are prey. In a stable community the numbers of predators and prey rise and fall in cycles.
- You should be able to interpret graphs used to model these cycles.

### Explained

Example food chain: grass → rabbit → fox. Grass is the producer; the rabbit is the primary consumer (and prey); the fox is the secondary consumer (and predator). The arrows show the direction the biomass moves: from what is eaten to what eats it.

**Predator–prey cycles.** When prey numbers rise, predators have more food, so predator numbers rise. More predators eat more prey, so prey numbers fall. With less food, predator numbers then fall. The predator peak comes a little after the prey peak.

### Worked example

A student counts daisies in 5 quadrats, each 1 m²: 4, 7, 3, 7, 9. The field is 2000 m². Estimate the daisy population.

1. Mean per quadrat = (4 + 7 + 3 + 7 + 9) ÷ 5 = 30 ÷ 5 = 6 per m².
2. Estimated population = 6 × 2000 = 12 000 daisies.

For the same data: the mode is 7 (most common) and the median is 7 (the middle value of 3, 4, 7, 7, 9).

### Common mistakes

- Drawing food chain arrows the wrong way. The arrow points to the eater.
- Saying the predator peak happens at the same time as the prey peak.

### Practical

**Required practical: measure the population size of a common species in a habitat, and use sampling techniques to investigate the effect of a factor on the distribution of this species.**

Estimating population size (random sampling with quadrats):

1. Lay out two tape measures at right angles to form a grid over the area.
2. Use a random number generator to pick coordinates, and place a quadrat there.
3. Count the number of the chosen species in the quadrat.
4. Repeat for at least 10 quadrats and calculate a mean per quadrat.
5. Scale up: population = mean per m² × total area in m².

Random placement avoids bias in choosing where to sample.

Investigating distribution (a transect):

1. Lay a tape measure from one area to another across a change, for example from the shade of a tree into open grass.
2. Place a quadrat at regular intervals along the line, such as every 2 m.
3. Count the species, or estimate percentage cover, in each quadrat.
4. Measure the factor at each point, such as light intensity with a light meter.

Plot the number of organisms against distance or against the factor.

## 4.7.2.2 How materials are cycled

### What you need to know

- All materials in the living world are recycled to provide the building blocks for future organisms.
- The carbon cycle returns carbon from organisms to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide, to be used by plants in photosynthesis.
- The water cycle provides fresh water for plants and animals on land before draining into the seas. Water is continuously evaporated and precipitated.
- You should be able to explain the importance of the carbon and water cycles to living organisms.
- Decay by microorganisms returns carbon to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide, and mineral ions to the soil.

### Explained

**The carbon cycle.**

| Process | What happens to carbon |
| --- | --- |
| Photosynthesis | Plants and algae take carbon dioxide out of the air and make glucose |
| Feeding | Carbon in plants passes to animals as they eat |
| Respiration | Plants, animals and microorganisms release carbon dioxide into the air |
| Death and decay | Microorganisms feed on dead organisms and waste, and release carbon dioxide by respiration |
| Combustion | Burning fossil fuels and wood releases carbon dioxide |
| Fossilisation | Some dead organisms do not decay and form fossil fuels over millions of years |

**The water cycle.** The Sun heats water in seas and lakes, which evaporates. Plants lose water vapour by transpiration. Water vapour cools and condenses into clouds, then falls as precipitation (rain, snow). It runs off into rivers, soaks into the ground, and drains back to the sea. Living things take in water to drink or through their roots.

## 4.7.2.3 Decomposition (Biology only)

### What you need to know

- You should be able to explain how temperature, water and availability of oxygen affect the rate of decay of biological material.
- Gardeners and farmers try to provide optimum conditions for rapid decay of waste biological material. The compost produced is used as a natural fertiliser for growing garden plants or crops.
- Anaerobic decay produces methane gas. Biogas generators can be used to produce methane gas as a fuel.
- You should be able to calculate rate changes in the decay of biological material, and translate information between numerical and graphical forms.

### Explained

| Factor | Effect | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | Warm speeds up decay; too hot slows it | Microorganisms' enzymes work faster in warmth, but are denatured if it is too hot |
| Water | Moist conditions speed up decay | Microorganisms need water to grow and for their reactions |
| Oxygen | More oxygen speeds up decay | Most decomposers respire aerobically |

A compost heap is turned to let oxygen in, kept moist, and heats up as the microorganisms respire.

A biogas generator holds plant waste or animal dung without oxygen. Anaerobic bacteria break it down and give off methane, which can be burned for heating or cooking.

### Practical

**Required practical (Biology only): investigate the effect of temperature on the rate of decay of fresh milk by measuring pH change.**

1. Put milk and sodium carbonate solution (which makes it alkaline) in a test tube with a few drops of phenolphthalein indicator, which is pink in alkaline conditions.
2. Put the tube and a separate tube of lipase solution in a water bath at a set temperature.
3. Add the lipase, start a stopwatch, and stir.
4. Lipase breaks down the fat in milk into fatty acids, so the pH falls. Stop the clock when the pink colour disappears.
5. Repeat at different temperatures.

Rate of decay = 1 ÷ time. A pH probe can be used instead of the indicator for more precise readings.

## 4.7.2.4 Impact of environmental change (Biology only) (HT only)

### What you need to know

- Environmental changes affect the distribution of species in an ecosystem. These changes include:
  - temperature
  - availability of water
  - composition of atmospheric gases.
- The changes may be seasonal, geographic or caused by human interaction.
- You should be able to evaluate the impact of environmental changes on the distribution of species in an ecosystem, given appropriate information.

### Explained

| Change | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Seasonal | Birds such as swallows migrate south in winter as insects become scarce |
| Geographic | Fewer plant species grow on high mountains, where it is colder and windier |
| Human | Rising average temperatures let some species spread further north; air pollution with sulfur dioxide reduces lichen growth near cities |

## 4.7.3.1 Biodiversity

### What you need to know

- Biodiversity is the variety of all the different species of organisms on Earth, or within an ecosystem.
- A great biodiversity ensures the stability of ecosystems by reducing the dependence of one species on another for food, shelter and the maintenance of the physical environment.
- The future of the human species on Earth relies on us maintaining a good level of biodiversity. Many human activities are reducing biodiversity, and only recently have measures been taken to try to stop this reduction.

### Explained

In a diverse ecosystem, if one food source is lost, consumers have others, so the community stays stable. Humans depend on biodiversity for food, medicines, clean water and air, and materials.

## 4.7.3.2 Waste management

### What you need to know

- Rapid growth in the human population and an increase in the standard of living mean that increasingly more resources are used and more waste is produced. Unless waste and chemical materials are properly handled, more pollution will be caused.
- Pollution can occur:
  - in water, from sewage, fertiliser or toxic chemicals
  - in air, from smoke and acidic gases
  - on land, from landfill and from toxic chemicals.
- Pollution kills plants and animals, which can reduce biodiversity.

### Explained

Fertiliser and sewage in water cause algae to grow fast and block light. Plants below die, and bacteria decaying them use up the oxygen, so fish die. Acidic gases such as sulfur dioxide form acid rain, which damages trees and makes lakes too acidic for some species.

## 4.7.3.3 Land use

### What you need to know

- Humans reduce the amount of land available for other animals and plants by building, quarrying, farming and dumping waste.
- The destruction of peat bogs, and other areas of peat, to produce garden compost reduces the area of this habitat and thus the variety of different plant, animal and microorganism species that live there (biodiversity).
- The decay or burning of the peat releases carbon dioxide into the atmosphere.

### Explained

Peat forms over thousands of years from plant material that does not fully decay in waterlogged, acidic bogs. It stores a lot of carbon. Using it for compost destroys a rare habitat and releases that carbon as carbon dioxide. Peat-free compost is an alternative.

## 4.7.3.4 Deforestation

### What you need to know

- Large-scale deforestation in tropical areas has occurred to:
  - provide land for cattle and rice fields
  - grow crops for biofuels.

### Explained

Effects of deforestation:

- Less carbon dioxide is taken out of the air by photosynthesis.
- Burning trees to clear land releases carbon dioxide; decaying wood releases it too, through microorganisms' respiration.
- Habitats are destroyed, so biodiversity falls.
- Cattle and rice fields release methane.

## 4.7.3.5 Global warming

### What you need to know

- Levels of carbon dioxide and methane in the atmosphere are increasing, and contribute to "global warming".
- You should be able to describe some of the biological consequences of global warming.
- You should understand that the scientific consensus about global warming and climate change is based on systematic reviews of thousands of peer-reviewed publications.

### Explained

Biological consequences of global warming:

- loss of habitats, for example as sea levels rise and flood low-lying land
- changes in the distribution of species, as some move to cooler areas and others spread into new ones
- changes in migration patterns
- reduced biodiversity, as species that cannot move or adapt become extinct.

## 4.7.3.6 Maintaining biodiversity

### What you need to know

Scientists and concerned citizens have put in place programmes to reduce the negative effects of humans on ecosystems and biodiversity. These include:

- breeding programmes for endangered species
- protection and regeneration of rare habitats
- reintroduction of field margins and hedgerows in agricultural areas where farmers grow only one type of crop
- reduction of deforestation and carbon dioxide emissions by some governments
- recycling resources rather than dumping waste in landfill.

You should be able to evaluate given information about methods that can be used to tackle problems caused by human impacts on the environment, and explain and evaluate the conflicting pressures on maintaining biodiversity.

### Explained

Conflicting pressures: protecting biodiversity can cost money and jobs. A farmer may lose income by leaving field margins; a developing country may need to clear forest to grow food or earn money from timber; protecting a habitat may stop new houses being built. An evaluation weighs the benefits to biodiversity against these costs.

Hedgerows and field margins give habitats and food for wild flowers, insects and birds in land otherwise covered by one crop (a monoculture).

## 4.7.4.1 Trophic levels (Biology only)

### What you need to know

- Trophic levels can be represented by numbers, starting at level 1 with plants and algae. Further trophic levels are numbered after this, depending on the order in which the organism feeds.
  - Level 1: plants and algae make their own food and are called producers.
  - Level 2: herbivores eat plants and algae and are called primary consumers.
  - Level 3: carnivores that eat herbivores are called secondary consumers.
  - Level 4: carnivores that eat other carnivores are called tertiary consumers.
- Apex predators are carnivores with no predators.
- Decomposers break down dead plant and animal matter by secreting enzymes into the environment. Small soluble food molecules then diffuse into the microorganism.

### Explained

Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) digest food outside their bodies. That is why decay depends on conditions that suit enzymes: warmth, moisture and oxygen.

## 4.7.4.2 Pyramids of biomass (Biology only)

### What you need to know

- Pyramids of biomass can be constructed to represent the relative amount of biomass in each trophic level of a food chain. Trophic level 1 is at the bottom of the pyramid.
- You should be able to construct accurate pyramids of biomass from appropriate data.

### Explained

Each bar's width is drawn to scale for the dry mass of all the organisms at that level, in the same unit (such as g/m²). Pyramids of biomass are almost always pyramid-shaped, because biomass is lost at each level.

## 4.7.4.3 Transfer of biomass (Biology only)

### What you need to know

- Producers are mostly plants and algae, which transfer about 1% of the incident energy from light for photosynthesis.
- Only approximately 10% of the biomass from each trophic level is transferred to the level above it.
- Losses of biomass are due to:
  - not all the ingested material is absorbed; some is egested as faeces
  - some absorbed material is lost as waste, such as carbon dioxide and water in respiration, and water and urea in urine.
- Large amounts of the glucose is used in respiration.
- You should be able to describe the differences between the trophic levels of organisms within an ecosystem, and calculate the efficiency of biomass transfers between trophic levels by percentages or fractions of mass.

```latex
\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{biomass transferred to the next level}}{\text{biomass available at the previous level}} \times 100
```

### Worked example

Plants in a field have 5000 g/m² of biomass. The rabbits that eat them have 400 g/m². Calculate the efficiency of transfer.

400 ÷ 5000 × 100 = 8%.

Food chains are short (rarely more than 4 or 5 levels) because so little biomass reaches the top.

### Common mistakes

- Saying energy is "used up". Biomass is lost from the chain as waste and through respiration.

## 4.7.5.1 Factors affecting food security (Biology only)

### What you need to know

- Food security means having enough food to feed a population.
- Biological factors which are threatening food security include:
  - the increasing birth rate has threatened food security in some countries
  - changing diets in developed countries means scarce food resources are transported around the world
  - new pests and pathogens that affect farming
  - environmental changes that affect food production, such as widespread famine occurring in some countries if rains fail
  - the cost of agricultural inputs
  - conflicts that have arisen in some parts of the world, which affect the availability of water or food.
- Sustainable methods must be found to feed all people on Earth.

## 4.7.5.2 Farming techniques (Biology only)

### What you need to know

- The efficiency of food production can be improved by restricting energy transfer from food animals to the environment. This can be done by limiting their movement and by controlling the temperature of their surroundings.
- Some animals are fed high protein foods to increase growth.
- You should be able to understand and discuss the ethical objections to some modern intensive animal farming methods.

### Explained

Animals kept in small, warm pens move less and lose less energy keeping warm, so more of their food becomes biomass (meat). This is intensive farming.

Ethical objections: the animals have little space and a poor quality of life; disease spreads easily in crowded conditions, so antibiotics are often used, which can increase antibiotic resistance; and it uses a lot of energy for heating.

## 4.7.5.3 Sustainable fisheries (Biology only)

### What you need to know

- Fish stocks in the oceans are declining. It is important to maintain fish stocks at a level where breeding continues, or certain species may disappear altogether in some areas.
- Control of net size and the introduction of fishing quotas play important roles in conservation of fish stocks at a sustainable level.

### Explained

| Method | How it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Larger mesh size in nets | Young, small fish escape and can grow and breed |
| Fishing quotas | Limits how many fish of each species can be caught, so enough remain to breed |

## 4.7.5.4 Role of biotechnology (Biology only)

### What you need to know

- Modern biotechnology techniques enable large quantities of microorganisms to be cultured for food.
- The fungus Fusarium is useful for producing mycoprotein, a protein-rich food suitable for vegetarians. The fungus is grown on glucose syrup in aerobic conditions, and the biomass is harvested and purified.
- A genetically modified bacterium produces human insulin. When harvested and purified, this is used to treat people with diabetes.
- GM crops could provide more food, or food with an improved nutritional value, such as golden rice.

### Explained

Mycoprotein is made in large vessels called fermenters, where the temperature, pH and oxygen are controlled so the fungus grows quickly. Golden rice is genetically modified to contain beta-carotene, which the body uses to make vitamin A; it could reduce vitamin A deficiency in countries where rice is the main food.

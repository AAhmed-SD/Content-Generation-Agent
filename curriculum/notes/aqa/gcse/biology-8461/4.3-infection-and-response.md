# 4.3 Infection and response

Pathogens cause communicable disease. This topic covers the main pathogens and diseases, how the body defends itself, vaccines, drugs and how new drugs are tested, plus monoclonal antibodies and plant disease (Biology only).

## 4.3.1.1 Communicable (infectious) diseases

### What you need to know

- Pathogens are microorganisms that cause infectious disease. They may be viruses, bacteria, protists or fungi. They may infect plants or animals.
- They can be spread by direct contact, by water or by air.
- Bacteria and viruses may reproduce rapidly inside the body.
- Bacteria may produce poisons (toxins) that damage tissues and make us feel ill.
- Viruses live and reproduce inside cells, causing cell damage.
- You should be able to explain how the spread of diseases can be reduced or prevented.

### Explained

| Pathogen | Size and nature | How it makes you ill |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Virus | Much smaller than bacteria; not a cell | Gets inside cells and makes copies of itself; the cell bursts, releasing new viruses, which damages tissue |
| Bacterium | Single prokaryotic cell | Reproduces quickly; releases toxins that damage tissues |
| Protist | Single eukaryotic cell | Often carried by a vector (an animal that spreads the disease) |
| Fungus | Some single cells; some grow as threads called hyphae | Hyphae grow into tissue; spores spread the disease |

Ways to reduce or prevent spread:

| Method | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Hygiene | Washing hands; cleaning kitchen surfaces; coughing into a tissue |
| Destroying or controlling vectors | Killing mosquitoes; using nets |
| Isolating infected individuals | Keeping someone with measles at home |
| Vaccination | Measles vaccine for children |

### Common mistakes

- Calling a virus a cell.
- Saying all microorganisms are pathogens. Only those that cause disease are.

## 4.3.1.2 Viral diseases

### What you need to know

- **Measles** is a viral disease showing symptoms of fever and a red skin rash. It is a serious illness that can be fatal if complications arise. Most young children are vaccinated against it. The measles virus is spread by inhalation of droplets from sneezes and coughs.
- **HIV** initially causes a flu-like illness. Unless successfully controlled with antiretroviral drugs, the virus attacks the body's immune cells. Late-stage HIV infection, or AIDS, occurs when the body's immune system becomes so badly damaged it can no longer deal with other infections or cancers. HIV is spread by sexual contact or exchange of body fluids, such as blood, which occurs when drug users share needles.
- **Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV)** is a widespread plant pathogen affecting many species of plants, including tomatoes. It gives a distinctive "mosaic" pattern of discolouration on the leaves, which affects the growth of the plant due to lack of photosynthesis.

### Explained

| Disease | Host | Symptoms | Spread | Control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Measles | Humans | Fever; red skin rash | Droplets from coughs and sneezes | Vaccination; isolating patients |
| HIV | Humans | Flu-like illness at first; later the immune system is damaged (AIDS) | Sexual contact; exchange of body fluids such as blood (shared needles) | Antiretroviral drugs; condoms; not sharing needles |
| TMV | Plants, such as tomatoes | Mosaic pattern of discoloured leaves; less photosynthesis, so poor growth | Contact between plants; on hands and tools | Removing infected plants; good hygiene; resistant varieties |

TMV slows growth because the discoloured parts of the leaf have less chlorophyll, so the plant makes less glucose by photosynthesis.

### Common mistakes

- Saying antibiotics treat HIV. HIV is a virus; antiretroviral drugs are used.
- Saying HIV and AIDS are the same. AIDS is the late stage of HIV infection.

## 4.3.1.3 Bacterial diseases

### What you need to know

- **Salmonella** food poisoning is spread by bacteria ingested in food, or on food prepared in unhygienic conditions. In the UK, poultry are vaccinated against Salmonella to control the spread. Fever, abdominal cramps, vomiting and diarrhoea are caused by the bacteria and the toxins they secrete.
- **Gonorrhoea** is a sexually transmitted disease (STD) with symptoms of a thick yellow or green discharge from the vagina or penis and pain on urinating. It is caused by a bacterium and was easily treated with the antibiotic penicillin until many resistant strains appeared. Gonorrhoea is spread by sexual contact. Its spread can be controlled by treatment with antibiotics or the use of a barrier method of contraception, such as a condom.

### Explained

| Disease | Symptoms | Spread | Control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Salmonella food poisoning | Fever, abdominal cramps, vomiting, diarrhoea | Eating contaminated food, such as undercooked chicken | Vaccinating poultry; cooking food thoroughly; keeping raw and cooked food apart; washing hands |
| Gonorrhoea | Thick yellow or green discharge; pain when urinating | Sexual contact | Antibiotics (some strains are now resistant to penicillin); condoms; tracing and testing sexual partners |

### Common mistakes

- Saying gonorrhoea is caused by a virus.

## 4.3.1.4 Fungal diseases

### What you need to know

- **Rose black spot** is a fungal disease where purple or black spots develop on leaves, which often turn yellow and drop early. It affects the growth of the plant as photosynthesis is reduced. It is spread in the environment by water or wind. Rose black spot can be treated by using fungicides and/or removing and destroying the affected leaves.

### Explained

Fewer leaves, and leaves with less green area, mean less photosynthesis, so less glucose for growth. Spores from the spots are carried to other plants by wind or splashed by rain.

## 4.3.1.5 Protist diseases

### What you need to know

- The pathogens that cause **malaria** are protists. The malarial protist has a life cycle that includes the mosquito. Malaria causes recurrent episodes of fever and can be fatal.
- The spread of malaria is controlled by preventing the vectors, mosquitoes, from breeding, and by using mosquito nets to avoid being bitten.

### Explained

The mosquito is a vector: it carries the protist from one person to another when it feeds on blood. Ways to control malaria:

- Remove standing water where mosquitoes lay eggs.
- Spray insecticide to kill mosquitoes.
- Sleep under nets treated with insecticide.
- Take antimalarial drugs when travelling to affected areas.

### Common mistakes

- Saying malaria is caused by the mosquito. It is caused by a protist; the mosquito is the vector.

## 4.3.1.6 Human defence systems

### What you need to know

Non-specific defence systems of the human body against pathogens:

- the skin
- the nose
- the trachea and bronchi
- the stomach.

The role of the immune system in defending against disease. If a pathogen enters the body, the immune system tries to destroy it. White blood cells help to defend against pathogens by:

- phagocytosis
- antibody production
- antitoxin production.

### Explained

**First line: stopping pathogens getting in.**

| Barrier | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Skin | A physical barrier; releases antimicrobial secretions; scabs seal cuts |
| Nose | Hairs and mucus trap particles that may contain pathogens |
| Trachea and bronchi | Cells make sticky mucus that traps pathogens; cells with cilia waft the mucus up to the throat to be swallowed |
| Stomach | Hydrochloric acid kills pathogens in food and drink |

**Second line: white blood cells.**

1. **Phagocytosis.** Some white blood cells (phagocytes) surround a pathogen, engulf it, and digest it.
2. **Antibodies.** Some white blood cells (lymphocytes) make antibodies. Each pathogen has antigens on its surface. An antibody has a shape that fits one particular antigen. Antibodies bind to the pathogen, clump pathogens together, and mark them to be destroyed.
3. **Antitoxins.** Some white blood cells make antitoxins, which bind to toxins released by bacteria and neutralise them.

### Common mistakes

- Saying white blood cells "eat" bacteria. Use "engulf" and "digest".
- Saying antibodies kill pathogens directly. They bind to them, so other white blood cells can destroy them.
- Saying antitoxins kill bacteria. They neutralise the toxins.

## 4.3.1.7 Vaccination

### What you need to know

- Vaccination involves introducing small quantities of dead or inactive forms of a pathogen into the body to stimulate the white blood cells to produce antibodies.
- If the same pathogen re-enters the body, the white blood cells respond quickly to produce the correct antibodies, preventing infection.
- You should be able to explain how vaccination will prevent illness in an individual, and how the spread of the pathogen can be reduced by immunising a large proportion of the population.

### Explained

1. The vaccine contains dead or inactive pathogens, carrying the pathogen's antigens.
2. White blood cells detect the antigens and make the matching antibodies.
3. Memory cells remain in the blood.
4. If the live pathogen enters the body later, the white blood cells make the right antibodies much faster and in greater numbers.
5. The pathogen is destroyed before it can cause illness.

If most of a population is vaccinated, the pathogen cannot spread easily, because most people it meets are immune. This protects the few who cannot be vaccinated, such as very young babies. This is called herd immunity.

### Common mistakes

- Saying a vaccine contains antibodies. It contains antigens (on dead or inactive pathogens); the body makes the antibodies.
- Saying a vaccine cures a disease. It prevents it.

## 4.3.1.8 Antibiotics and painkillers

### What you need to know

- Antibiotics, such as penicillin, are medicines that help to cure bacterial disease by killing infective bacteria inside the body.
- It is important that specific bacteria should be treated by specific antibiotics.
- The use of antibiotics has greatly reduced deaths from infectious bacterial diseases. However, the emergence of strains resistant to antibiotics is of great concern.
- Antibiotics cannot kill viral pathogens.
- Painkillers and other medicines are used to treat the symptoms of disease but do not kill pathogens.
- It is difficult to develop drugs that kill viruses without also damaging the body's tissues.

### Explained

Viruses live inside the body's own cells, so a drug that destroys them would also destroy the cells. That is why antibiotics do not work on colds or flu, and why there are few antiviral drugs.

Doctors should not prescribe antibiotics for viral infections, and patients should finish the course. Both reduce the chance of resistant bacteria developing (see 4.6.3.7).

### Common mistakes

- Saying antibiotics kill viruses.
- Saying painkillers cure a disease.

## 4.3.1.9 Discovery and development of drugs

### What you need to know

- Traditionally drugs were extracted from plants and microorganisms.
  - The heart drug digitalis originates from foxgloves.
  - The painkiller aspirin originates from willow.
  - Penicillin was discovered by Alexander Fleming from the Penicillium mould.
- Most new drugs are synthesised by chemists in the pharmaceutical industry. However, the starting point may still be a chemical extracted from a plant.
- New medical drugs have to be tested and trialled before being used to check that they are safe and effective.
- New drugs are extensively tested for toxicity, efficacy and dose.
- Preclinical testing is done in a laboratory using cells, tissues and live animals.
- Clinical trials use healthy volunteers and patients.
  - Very low doses of the drug are given at the start of the clinical trial.
  - If the drug is found to be safe, further clinical trials are carried out to find the optimum dose for the drug.
  - In double blind trials, some patients are given a placebo.

### Explained

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Toxicity | Whether the drug is harmful |
| Efficacy | Whether the drug works |
| Dose | How much should be given, and how often |
| Placebo | A dummy treatment that looks like the drug but contains no drug |
| Double blind trial | Neither the patients nor the doctors know who has the drug and who has the placebo, so the results are not biased |

The stages, in order:

1. Discovery: a possible drug is found or designed.
2. Preclinical testing on cells and tissues in a lab, then on live animals.
3. Clinical trials, first on a small number of healthy volunteers at a very low dose, to check safety.
4. Then on patients with the disease, to find the optimum dose and check that it works, usually double blind with a placebo.
5. Results are checked by other scientists (peer review) before they are published, to make sure the work is valid and not biased.

### Common mistakes

- Saying healthy volunteers are used to find out whether the drug works. They are used to check safety; patients show whether it works.
- Confusing a placebo with a vaccine.

## 4.3.2.1 Producing monoclonal antibodies (Biology only) (HT only)

### What you need to know

- Monoclonal antibodies are produced from a single clone of cells. The antibodies are specific to one binding site on one protein antigen, so they can target a specific chemical or specific cells in the body.
- They are produced by stimulating mouse lymphocytes to make a particular antibody. The lymphocytes are combined with a particular kind of tumour cell to make a cell called a hybridoma cell.
- The hybridoma cell can both divide and make the antibody. Single hybridoma cells are cloned to produce many identical cells that all produce the same antibody.
- A large amount of the antibody can be collected and purified.

### Explained

Why combine two cells: a lymphocyte makes the antibody but does not divide well; a tumour cell divides fast but makes no antibody. The hybridoma does both.

1. Inject a mouse with the chosen antigen.
2. The mouse's lymphocytes make antibodies against that antigen.
3. Collect the lymphocytes and fuse them with tumour cells to make hybridoma cells.
4. Select a hybridoma that makes the right antibody and clone it.
5. Collect and purify the identical antibodies.

## 4.3.2.2 Uses of monoclonal antibodies (Biology only) (HT only)

### What you need to know

Examples include:

- for diagnosis, such as in pregnancy tests
- in laboratories to measure the levels of hormones and other chemicals in blood, or to detect pathogens
- in research to locate or identify specific molecules in a cell or tissue, by binding to them with a fluorescent dye
- to treat some diseases. For cancer, the monoclonal antibody can be bound to a radioactive substance, a toxic drug or a chemical that stops cells growing and dividing. It delivers the substance to the cancer cells without harming other cells in the body.

Monoclonal antibodies create more side effects than expected. They are not yet as widely used as everyone hoped when they were first developed.

### Explained

**Pregnancy test.** A pregnant woman's urine contains the hormone hCG.

1. Urine is placed on the stick.
2. In the first zone, mobile antibodies attached to a blue bead bind to any hCG.
3. In the test zone, fixed antibodies bind to the hCG–bead complex, so a blue line appears: pregnant.
4. In the control zone, fixed antibodies bind to the free mobile antibodies, so a blue line always appears if the test has worked.

**Cancer treatment.** Cancer cells have particular antigens on their surface. Antibodies made against those antigens carry a drug or radioactive substance directly to the cancer cells, so fewer healthy cells are harmed than with normal chemotherapy.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Bind only to specific cells, so healthy cells are less affected | More side effects than expected, such as fever, vomiting and low blood pressure |
| Can be used to treat a range of conditions | Expensive to develop |
| | Producing them uses animals (mice) |

## 4.3.3.1 Detection and identification of plant diseases (Biology only)

### What you need to know

Plant diseases can be detected by:

- stunted growth
- spots on leaves
- areas of decay (rot)
- growths
- malformed stems or leaves
- discolouration
- the presence of pests.

(HT only) Identification can be made by:

- reference to a gardening manual or website
- taking infected plants to a laboratory to identify the pathogen
- using testing kits that contain monoclonal antibodies.

Plants can be infected by a range of viral, bacterial and fungal pathogens, as well as by insects.

Plants can be damaged by a range of ion deficiency conditions:

- stunted growth caused by nitrate deficiency
- chlorosis caused by magnesium deficiency.

You should know that plants need nitrate ions for protein synthesis and therefore growth, and magnesium ions to make chlorophyll.

### Explained

| Deficiency | Ion needed for | Symptom |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nitrate | Making amino acids and so proteins, for growth | Stunted growth |
| Magnesium | Making chlorophyll | Chlorosis: yellow leaves, because there is less chlorophyll |

Insect pests such as aphids feed on phloem sap, taking sugars the plant needs, and can spread viruses between plants.

## 4.3.3.2 Plant defence responses (Biology only)

### What you need to know

Physical defence responses to resist invasion of microorganisms:

- cellulose cell walls
- a tough waxy cuticle on leaves
- layers of dead cells on the surface of stems (bark on trees), which fall off.

Chemical plant defence responses:

- antibacterial chemicals
- poisons to deter herbivores.

Mechanical adaptations:

- thorns and hairs deter animals
- leaves which droop or curl when touched
- mimicry to trick animals.

### Explained

| Type | Example | How it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical | Waxy cuticle | Barrier pathogens cannot easily pass |
| Physical | Bark | Dead outer cells fall off, taking pathogens with them |
| Chemical | Antibacterial chemicals (such as those in mint and pine) | Kill bacteria |
| Chemical | Poisons (such as in foxgloves) | Stop herbivores eating the plant |
| Mechanical | Thorns and hairs | Animals avoid touching or eating the plant |
| Mechanical | Leaves that droop or curl when touched | Startle insects or dislodge them |
| Mechanical | Mimicry | For example, a plant with spots that look like insect eggs puts off insects from laying eggs there |

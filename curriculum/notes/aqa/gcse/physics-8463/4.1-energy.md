# 4.1 Energy

Energy is a way of keeping track of what can happen in a system. It is never made or destroyed; it moves between stores. This topic covers energy stores, the equations for kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic and thermal energy, power, efficiency, and the ways we generate electricity.

## Equations for this topic

| Equation | Symbols and units | Recall or given |
| --- | --- | --- |
| kinetic energy = 0.5 × mass × speed² | Ek = ½mv² (J, kg, m/s) | Recall |
| gravitational potential energy = mass × gravitational field strength × height | Ep = mgh (J, kg, N/kg, m) | Recall |
| elastic potential energy = 0.5 × spring constant × extension² | Ee = ½ke² (J, N/m, m) | Equation sheet |
| change in thermal energy = mass × specific heat capacity × temperature change | ΔE = mcΔθ (J, kg, J/kg °C, °C) | Equation sheet |

| power = work done ÷ time | P = W/t (W, J, s) | Recall |
| efficiency = useful output energy transfer ÷ total input energy transfer | no units (decimal or %) | Recall |
| efficiency = useful power output ÷ total power input | no units (decimal or %) | Recall |

## 4.1.1.1 Energy stores and systems

### What you need to know

- A system is an object or a group of objects that you choose to study.
- When a system changes, the energy in its stores changes. The main stores are kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic potential, thermal (internal), chemical, magnetic, electrostatic and nuclear.
- Energy can be moved between stores by heating, by a force doing work (mechanical work), by an electric current doing work, and by waves (such as light and sound).
- You should be able to describe the changes in the way energy is stored when a system changes, for example:
  - a ball thrown upwards
  - a moving object hitting an obstacle
  - an object sped up by a constant force
  - a vehicle slowing down
  - water being brought to the boil in an electric kettle.
- You should be able to calculate the changes in energy involved when a system changes, using the equations in this topic.

### Explained

Think of energy like money held in different accounts. The total never changes; it moves from one account to another. Physicists call the accounts stores and the ways money moves between them pathways.

| Change | Store that empties | Store that fills | Pathway |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ball thrown upwards (going up) | Kinetic store of the ball | Gravitational potential store of the ball | Work done by gravity (the weight acts against the motion) |
| Car hits a wall | Kinetic store of the car | Thermal stores of car, wall and air; some sound | Work done by contact forces; sound waves |
| Trolley pushed by a constant force | Chemical store of the person (or elastic store of a stretched band) | Kinetic store of the trolley | Work done by the force |
| Car braking | Kinetic store of the car | Thermal store of the brakes and surroundings | Work done by friction |
| Electric kettle | Chemical or nuclear store at the power station | Thermal store of the water | Electrical work, then heating |

A closed system is one where no energy enters or leaves. The total energy in a closed system stays the same, even though it may move between stores inside it.

### Common mistakes

- Calling heat, light or sound a store. They are ways energy is transferred, not places it is kept. Use "thermal store" not "heat energy".
- Forgetting the surroundings. In almost every real change some energy ends up in the thermal store of the surroundings.

## 4.1.1.2 Changes in energy

### What you need to know

- You should be able to calculate the energy in the kinetic store of a moving object:

```latex
E_k = \frac{1}{2} m v^2
```

- You should be able to calculate the energy in the gravitational potential store of an object raised above the ground:

```latex
E_p = m g h
```

- You should be able to calculate the energy in the elastic potential store of a stretched spring, as long as the limit of proportionality is not exceeded (the equation is on the equation sheet):

```latex
E_e = \frac{1}{2} k e^2
```

- Units: energy in joules (J), mass in kilograms (kg), speed in metres per second (m/s), gravitational field strength in newtons per kilogram (N/kg), height in metres (m), spring constant in newtons per metre (N/m), extension in metres (m).
- On Earth, g is about 9.8 N/kg. Questions will tell you which value to use.

### Explained

Kinetic energy depends on the square of speed. Double the speed and the kinetic energy goes up four times. That is why a small rise in speed makes a big difference in a crash.

Gravitational potential energy is the energy an object gains when it is lifted against gravity. It depends on how high it is lifted (the change in height), not how high it is above sea level.

For a falling object with no air resistance, every joule lost from the gravitational store goes into the kinetic store, so you can write mgh = ½mv². The mass cancels, which is why a heavy and a light ball dropped together land at the same time if air resistance is small.

### Worked example

A 1200 kg car moves at 15 m/s. Calculate the energy in its kinetic store.

1. Ek = ½ × 1200 × 15².
2. 15² = 225, so Ek = 0.5 × 1200 × 225 = 135 000 J (135 kJ).

A 0.40 kg ball is dropped from a height of 5.0 m. Use g = 9.8 N/kg. Ignore air resistance. Calculate its speed just before it hits the ground.

1. Energy lost from gravitational store: Ep = 0.40 × 9.8 × 5.0 = 19.6 J.
2. All of this goes to the kinetic store: 19.6 = ½ × 0.40 × v².
3. v² = 19.6 ÷ 0.20 = 98.
4. v = √98 = 9.9 m/s.

A spring with spring constant 40 N/m is stretched by 15 cm. Calculate the energy in its elastic store.

1. Convert: 15 cm = 0.15 m.
2. Ee = ½ × 40 × 0.15² = 0.5 × 40 × 0.0225 = 0.45 J.

### Common mistakes

- Squaring the whole of ½mv instead of only v.
- Leaving extension or height in centimetres. Convert to metres first.
- Using grams in Ek or Ep. Mass must be in kilograms.
- When rearranging Ek = ½mv² for speed, forgetting to take the square root at the end.

## 4.1.1.3 Energy changes in systems

### What you need to know

- The amount of energy needed to change the temperature of a substance, or released when it cools, depends on its mass, the material, and the temperature change.

```latex
\Delta E = m c \Delta\theta
```

- ΔE is the change in thermal energy in joules (J), m is mass in kg, c is specific heat capacity in joules per kilogram per degree Celsius (J/kg °C), and Δθ is the temperature change in °C. This equation is on the equation sheet.
- The specific heat capacity of a substance is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of it by 1 °C.

### Explained

Different materials warm up at different rates for the same energy input. Water has a very high specific heat capacity (about 4200 J/kg °C), so it takes a lot of energy to heat and gives out a lot as it cools. That is why it is used in radiators and storage heaters. Metals such as copper have low values (around 390 J/kg °C), so they heat up quickly.

### Worked example

How much energy is needed to heat 2.5 kg of water from 18 °C to 100 °C? (c of water = 4200 J/kg °C)

1. Δθ = 100 − 18 = 82 °C.
2. ΔE = 2.5 × 4200 × 82 = 861 000 J (861 kJ).

A 0.50 kg block of metal is heated with 9000 J of energy and its temperature rises by 20 °C. Calculate its specific heat capacity.

1. Rearrange: c = ΔE ÷ (m × Δθ).
2. c = 9000 ÷ (0.50 × 20) = 9000 ÷ 10 = 900 J/kg °C.

### Common mistakes

- Using the final temperature instead of the change in temperature.
- Using grams rather than kilograms. 500 g = 0.50 kg.

### Practical

Required practical 1: finding the specific heat capacity of a material.

1. Measure the mass of a metal block (for example 1 kg) with a balance. Wrap it in insulation.
2. Put an electric heater in one hole and a thermometer in the other. A drop of water in the thermometer hole improves contact.
3. Connect the heater to a power supply with an ammeter in series and a voltmeter in parallel (or use a joulemeter).
4. Record the starting temperature, switch on and start a stopwatch.
5. Record the current and potential difference, and the temperature every minute for about 10 minutes.
6. Energy transferred = potential difference × current × time (E = VIt), or read the joulemeter.
7. Plot temperature (y-axis) against energy transferred (x-axis). The gradient is 1 ÷ (m × c), so c = 1 ÷ (gradient × m).

Why the value comes out too high: some energy heats the surroundings, not the block, so more energy seems to be needed for each degree. Insulating the block reduces this. Thermal contact and the heater warming up before the block also add error, which is why you use the straight part of the graph.

Safety: the heater and block get hot; do not touch them and let them cool before packing away.

## 4.1.1.4 Power

### What you need to know

- Power is the rate at which energy is transferred or the rate at which work is done.

```latex
P = \frac{E}{t} \qquad P = \frac{W}{t}
```

- Power in watts (W), energy transferred in joules (J), work done in joules (J), time in seconds (s).
- An energy transfer of 1 joule per second is 1 watt.
- You should be able to give examples that show what power means, for example two motors lifting the same load the same height in different times.

### Explained

Two electric motors each lift a 10 kg box up 2 m. Both do the same work. The one that does it in 4 s has twice the power of the one that takes 8 s. Power tells you how fast, not how much.

### Worked example

A motor does 4800 J of work in 1 minute. Calculate its power.

1. t = 60 s.
2. P = 4800 ÷ 60 = 80 W.

A 2.0 kW kettle is switched on for 3.5 minutes. How much energy does it transfer?

1. P = 2000 W, t = 3.5 × 60 = 210 s.
2. E = P × t = 2000 × 210 = 420 000 J.

### Common mistakes

- Leaving time in minutes or hours. Convert to seconds.
- Leaving kW as kW. 2.0 kW = 2000 W.

## 4.1.2.1 Energy transfers in a system

### What you need to know

- Energy can be transferred usefully, stored or dissipated, but it cannot be created or destroyed. This is the principle of conservation of energy.
- In a closed system there is no net change in the total energy when energy is transferred.
- In every system change some energy is dissipated, meaning it is stored in less useful ways. This energy is often described as wasted.
- You should be able to explain ways of reducing unwanted energy transfers, for example by lubrication and by thermal insulation.
- The higher the thermal conductivity of a material, the higher the rate of energy transfer through it by conduction.
- The rate of cooling of a building depends on the thickness and the thermal conductivity of its walls.
- You should be able to describe how the rate of cooling of a building is affected by the thickness and thermal conductivity of its walls.

### Explained

Dissipated energy is spread out, usually into the thermal store of the surroundings, so it is hard to use again. A motor gets warm and makes a noise; the energy in those warm parts and in the sound is wasted.

Ways to reduce unwanted transfers:

- Lubrication (oil or grease) reduces friction between moving parts, so less work is done against friction and less energy goes to the thermal store.
- Thermal insulation slows heating of the surroundings. Materials with low thermal conductivity (such as trapped air in foam, fibreglass or double glazing) let energy through slowly.
- Thicker walls slow the rate of energy transfer. A house loses energy more slowly through thick walls made of a material with low thermal conductivity.

### Common mistakes

- Saying energy is "lost" as if it disappears. It is still there, just spread out and not useful.
- Mixing up conductivity and conductor. A material with low thermal conductivity is a good insulator.

### Practical

Required practical 2 (Physics only): investigating thermal insulators.

1. Put a small beaker inside a larger one. Fill the small beaker with a measured volume of hot water (for example 80 cm³ from a kettle).
2. Pack the gap between the beakers with the insulating material being tested. Put a lid of the same material on top with a hole for a thermometer.
3. Record the starting temperature and then the temperature every 2 minutes for 20 minutes.
4. Repeat with different materials, keeping the volume of water, starting temperature, beaker sizes and time the same.
5. Repeat with different thicknesses of one material (for example 1, 2, 3 and 4 layers of bubble wrap).

The best insulator gives the smallest temperature drop in the 20 minutes. More layers usually give a smaller drop, because the energy has further to travel through a poor conductor.

Safety: take care with hot water; stand beakers on a heatproof mat.

## 4.1.2.2 Efficiency

### What you need to know

- The energy efficiency of any energy transfer can be calculated:

```latex
\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful output energy transfer}}{\text{total input energy transfer}}
```

```latex
\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful power output}}{\text{total power input}}
```

- Efficiency has no unit. It can be written as a decimal (0.35) or a percentage (35%). Multiply the decimal by 100 to get a percentage.
- No device can be more than 100% efficient.
- (HT only) You should be able to describe ways to increase the efficiency of an intended energy transfer.

### Explained

A filament bulb might take in 60 J each second and give out only 6 J of light. Its efficiency is 6 ÷ 60 = 0.10, or 10%. The other 54 J heats the surroundings.

(HT only) To make a transfer more efficient you reduce the wasted energy: lubricate moving parts to cut friction, use streamlined shapes to cut air resistance, insulate to cut unwanted heating, use thicker wires or lower-resistance parts to cut heating in circuits, and swap filament bulbs for LEDs.

### Worked example

An electric motor has an input power of 500 W and a useful output power of 350 W. Calculate its efficiency as a percentage.

1. Efficiency = 350 ÷ 500 = 0.70.
2. As a percentage: 0.70 × 100 = 70%.

A kettle is 85% efficient. It transfers 340 000 J usefully to the water. How much energy was put in?

1. Total input = useful output ÷ efficiency.
2. 340 000 ÷ 0.85 = 400 000 J.

### Common mistakes

- Dividing the bigger number by the smaller and getting more than 100%. Useful output always goes on top.
- Giving efficiency a unit. It has none.

## 4.1.3 National and global energy resources

### What you need to know

- The main energy resources on Earth are: fossil fuels (coal, oil and gas), nuclear fuel, bio-fuel, wind, hydro-electricity, geothermal, the tides, the Sun, and water waves.
- A renewable energy resource is one that is being (or can be) replenished as it is used.
- The uses of energy resources include transport, electricity generation and heating.
- You should be able to describe the main energy sources for each of these uses, and how they have changed over time.
- You should be able to describe the environmental impact of using each resource, and explain patterns and trends in the use of energy resources.
- You should be able to compare the ways that different resources are used to generate electricity, including reliability.
- You should be able to consider the environmental issues that may arise, and understand that science can identify these issues but cannot always solve them, because of political, social, ethical or economic reasons.

### Explained

| Resource | Renewable? | Reliable? | Main good points | Main problems |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Coal, oil, gas | No | Yes, output can be changed to meet demand | Cheap to extract, large amounts available at once | Release carbon dioxide (global warming); coal and oil can release sulfur dioxide (acid rain); oil spills |
| Nuclear | No | Yes | No carbon dioxide while running; a lot of energy from a small mass of fuel | Radioactive waste lasts for a long time; risk of accidents; expensive to build and close down |
| Bio-fuel | Yes | Fairly, can be stored | Plants take in carbon dioxide while growing, so close to carbon neutral | Land used for fuel crops cannot grow food; forests may be cleared |
| Wind | Yes | No, only when it is windy | No fuel cost, no pollution while running | Visual and noise impact; low output per turbine |
| Hydro-electric | Yes | Yes, water can be held back | Can respond quickly to demand | Flooding valleys destroys habitats; few suitable sites |
| Geothermal | Yes | Yes | Steady output | Only works where hot rocks are near the surface |
| Tidal | Yes | Yes, tides are predictable, but output changes during the day | No fuel cost | Barrages affect estuary habitats and shipping |
| Solar | Yes | No, not at night and less on cloudy days | No pollution while running; good for remote places | Low output per panel; large areas needed |
| Water waves | Yes | No, depends on the weather | No fuel cost | Small scale; can affect coastlines and marine life |

Trends: for transport, petrol and diesel still lead, but electric vehicles and bio-fuels are growing. For heating, gas is still common in UK homes, with electric heat pumps rising. For electricity, the UK has moved away from coal towards gas, wind, solar and nuclear, mainly to cut carbon dioxide emissions. World energy demand keeps rising as the population grows and countries develop.

Why change is slow: building new power stations and wind farms costs money, some people object to them being built near their homes, and renewable sources that are not always available still need backup.

### Common mistakes

- Calling nuclear renewable. Uranium is used up.
- Saying a resource is "reliable" when you mean renewable. Reliable means available whenever needed; renewable means it will not run out.

## Mark scheme

1. Energy is transferred from the gravitational potential store of the skydiver (1); by work done against air resistance (1); to the thermal store of the skydiver and surrounding air (1). The kinetic store does not change because the speed is steady.
2. Ek = 0.5 × 70 × 6.0² (1) = 1260 J (1).
3. Ep = 2.0 × 9.8 × 1.5 (1) = 29.4 J (1).
4. k = 2Ee ÷ e² (1); = (2 × 0.32) ÷ 0.08² = 0.64 ÷ 0.0064 (1) = 100 N/m (1).
5. Δθ = 40 °C (1); c = 19 000 ÷ (0.25 × 40) (1) = 1900 J/kg °C (1).
6. t = 90 s (1); P = 36 000 ÷ 90 = 400 W (1).
7. 9 ÷ 12 = 0.75 (1); 75% (1).
8. Some energy heats the surroundings rather than the block (1), so more energy is recorded for each degree rise, making c too large (1). Improvement: insulate the block / use a lid / use oil or water in the thermometer hole for better contact (1).
9. Any two: same volume or mass of water; same starting temperature; same time interval; same size beakers; same thickness of material (when comparing materials) (2).
10. Any two: lubricate moving parts; reduce resistance of the coil wires (thicker or lower-resistance wire); improve cooling design or streamlining to cut air resistance on moving parts (2).
11. Advantage: renewable / no carbon dioxide or pollution while generating / no fuel cost (1). Disadvantage: unreliable, only works when windy / cannot increase output to meet demand / low output per turbine / visual impact (1).
12. Ek = 0.5 × 0.20 × 12² = 14.4 J (1); at the top, Ep = 14.4 J, so h = 14.4 ÷ (0.20 × 9.8) (1) = 7.3 m (1).

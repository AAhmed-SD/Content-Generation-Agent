# 4.3 Particle model of matter

Everything is made of tiny particles. How closely they are packed and how fast they move explain density, changes of state, heating and gas pressure. This topic covers density, internal energy, specific heat capacity, specific latent heat and (Physics only) the pressure and volume of gases.

## Equations for this topic

| Equation | Symbols and units | Recall or given |
| --- | --- | --- |
| density = mass ÷ volume | ρ = m/V (kg/m³, kg, m³) | Recall |
| change in thermal energy = mass × specific heat capacity × temperature change | ΔE = mcΔθ (J, kg, J/kg °C, °C) | Equation sheet |
| energy for a change of state = mass × specific latent heat | E = mL (J, kg, J/kg) | Equation sheet |
| pressure × volume = constant (fixed mass of gas at constant temperature) | pV = constant (Pa, m³) | Equation sheet (Physics only) |

## 4.3.1.1 Density of materials

### What you need to know

- The density of a material is defined by:

```latex
\rho = \frac{m}{V}
```

- Density ρ in kilograms per metre cubed (kg/m³), mass m in kilograms (kg), volume V in metres cubed (m³). Grams per centimetre cubed (g/cm³) is also used; 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³.
- The particle model can be used to explain the different states of matter and the differences in density.
- You should be able to recognise and draw simple diagrams to model the difference between solids, liquids and gases.
- You should be able to explain the differences in density between the different states of matter in terms of the arrangement of atoms or molecules.

### Explained

| State | Arrangement | Movement | Density |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Solid | Particles touching, in a regular pattern | Vibrate about fixed positions | High |
| Liquid | Particles touching, but in a random arrangement | Move around each other | High (usually a little less than the solid) |
| Gas | Particles far apart, random | Move fast in all directions | Very low |

In a diagram, draw solid particles as neat rows of touching circles, liquid particles as touching circles in a jumble, and gas particles as a few widely spaced circles.

Gases have low density because the same mass of particles is spread through a much bigger volume.

### Worked example

A block of aluminium has a mass of 0.54 kg and a volume of 0.000 20 m³. Calculate its density.

1. ρ = 0.54 ÷ 0.000 20 = 2700 kg/m³.

A stone of density 2.5 g/cm³ has a volume of 40 cm³. Calculate its mass.

1. m = ρ × V = 2.5 × 40 = 100 g.

### Common mistakes

- Mixing units, for example mass in grams with volume in m³. Keep g with cm³ and kg with m³.
- Converting cm³ to m³ by dividing by 100. It is 1 000 000 (1 m³ = 100 × 100 × 100 cm³).

### Practical

Required practical 5: finding the density of solids and liquids.

Regular solid:

1. Measure its mass with a top-pan balance.
2. Measure its length, width and height with a ruler (or vernier callipers for small objects).
3. Volume = length × width × height. Density = mass ÷ volume.

Irregular solid:

1. Measure its mass with a balance.
2. Fill a displacement (eureka) can with water up to the spout. Wait for it to stop dripping and put a measuring cylinder under the spout.
3. Lower the object in gently on a thread. The volume of water collected equals the volume of the object.
4. Density = mass ÷ volume.

(Instead of a eureka can, you can read a measuring cylinder before and after the object is put in; the rise is the object's volume.)

Liquid:

1. Put an empty measuring cylinder on a balance and zero (tare) it.
2. Pour in a measured volume of liquid, for example 50 cm³, reading from the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
3. Record the mass. Density = mass ÷ volume.

Errors: air bubbles stuck to the object, water splashing out of the eureka can, and reading the meniscus from an angle.

## 4.3.1.2 Changes of state

### What you need to know

- When substances change state (melt, freeze, boil, evaporate, condense or sublimate), mass is conserved.
- Changes of state are physical changes, which differ from chemical changes because the material recovers its original properties if the change is reversed.
- You should be able to describe how, when substances change state, mass is conserved.

### Explained

| Change | From | To |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Melting | Solid | Liquid |
| Freezing | Liquid | Solid |
| Boiling or evaporating | Liquid | Gas |
| Condensing | Gas | Liquid |
| Sublimating | Solid | Gas (directly) |

When 100 g of ice melts, you get 100 g of water. The particles are the same; only their arrangement and energy have changed. If you freeze the water again, you get ice with the same properties as before.

### Common mistakes

- Calling melting a chemical change. No new substance is made.

## 4.3.2.1 Internal energy

### What you need to know

- Energy is stored inside a system by the particles (atoms and molecules) that make up the system. This is called internal energy.
- Internal energy is the total kinetic energy and potential energy of all the particles that make up a system.
- Heating changes the energy stored within the system by increasing the energy of the particles that make up the system. This either raises the temperature of the system or produces a change of state.

### Explained

The kinetic energy part comes from the particles moving or vibrating. The potential energy part comes from the forces between particles and how far apart they are. Heating a solid makes its particles vibrate faster (more kinetic energy, higher temperature). When it melts, the extra energy goes into breaking bonds and moving particles apart (more potential energy) instead.

### Common mistakes

- Saying internal energy is just kinetic energy. It is kinetic plus potential.

## 4.3.2.2 Temperature changes in a system and specific heat capacity

### What you need to know

- If the temperature of the system increases, the increase in temperature depends on the mass of the substance heated, the type of material, and the energy input to the system.

```latex
\Delta E = m c \Delta\theta
```

- The specific heat capacity of a substance is the amount of energy required to raise the temperature of one kilogram of the substance by one degree Celsius.

### Explained

This is the same equation as in 4.1.1.3, seen from the particle side: the energy supplied raises the kinetic energy of the particles, and temperature is a measure of their average kinetic energy.

### Worked example

0.80 kg of water at 15 °C is given 50 400 J of energy. What is its final temperature? (c = 4200 J/kg °C)

1. Δθ = ΔE ÷ (m × c) = 50 400 ÷ (0.80 × 4200) = 50 400 ÷ 3360 = 15 °C.
2. Final temperature = 15 + 15 = 30 °C.

### Common mistakes

- Giving the temperature change as the final answer when the question asks for the final temperature.

## 4.3.2.3 Changes of state and specific latent heat

### What you need to know

- If a change of state happens, the energy supplied changes the energy stored (internal energy) but not the temperature.
- The specific latent heat of a substance is the amount of energy required to change the state of one kilogram of the substance with no change in temperature.

```latex
E = m L
```

- Energy E in joules (J), mass m in kilograms (kg), specific latent heat L in joules per kilogram (J/kg).
- Specific latent heat of fusion: change of state from solid to liquid.
- Specific latent heat of vaporisation: change of state from liquid to vapour.
- You should be able to interpret heating and cooling graphs that include changes of state.
- You should be able to distinguish between specific heat capacity and specific latent heat.

### Explained

A heating graph (temperature against time, steady heating) for ice starting below 0 °C:

1. Sloping line: ice warms up to 0 °C.
2. Flat line at 0 °C: ice melts. Energy goes into breaking bonds, not raising temperature.
3. Sloping line: water warms to 100 °C.
4. Flat line at 100 °C (longer than the melting one): water boils. Vaporisation needs much more energy than melting, because the particles must be pulled fully apart.
5. Sloping line: steam warms up.

A cooling graph is the same shape in reverse; on the flat parts energy is released as bonds form.

| | Specific heat capacity | Specific latent heat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it measures | Energy to raise 1 kg by 1 °C | Energy to change the state of 1 kg |
| Temperature change? | Yes | No |
| Unit | J/kg °C | J/kg |

### Worked example

How much energy is needed to melt 0.50 kg of ice at 0 °C? (Specific latent heat of fusion of water = 334 000 J/kg)

1. E = 0.50 × 334 000 = 167 000 J.

A kettle supplies 1 130 000 J to boil away water at 100 °C. The specific latent heat of vaporisation of water is 2 260 000 J/kg. What mass of water is turned to steam?

1. m = E ÷ L = 1 130 000 ÷ 2 260 000 = 0.50 kg.

### Common mistakes

- Using mcΔθ during a change of state. When the temperature is not changing, use E = mL.
- Thinking temperature rises while a substance is boiling. It stays the same until all of it has changed state.

## 4.3.3.1 Particle motion in gases

### What you need to know

- The molecules of a gas are in constant random motion. The temperature of the gas is related to the average kinetic energy of the molecules.
- Changing the temperature of a gas, held at constant volume, changes the pressure exerted by the gas.
- You should be able to explain how the motion of the molecules in a gas is related to both its temperature and its pressure.
- You should be able to explain qualitatively the relation between the temperature of a gas and its pressure at constant volume.

### Explained

Gas molecules move fast in all directions and hit the walls of their container. Each collision pushes on the wall. Pressure is the total force of all these collisions on each square metre of wall.

Heat the gas in a sealed, rigid container: the molecules gain kinetic energy and move faster. They hit the walls more often and harder, so the pressure rises.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the molecules get bigger or expand when heated. The molecules stay the same size; they move faster.

## 4.3.3.2 Pressure in gases (Physics only)

### What you need to know

- A gas can be compressed or expanded by pressure changes. The pressure produces a net force at right angles to the wall of the gas container (or any surface).
- You should be able to use the particle model to explain how increasing the volume in which a gas is contained, at constant temperature, can lead to a decrease in pressure.
- For a fixed mass of gas held at a constant temperature:

```latex
p V = \text{constant}
```

- Pressure p in pascals (Pa), volume V in metres cubed (m³). This equation is on the equation sheet.
- You should be able to calculate the change in the pressure of a gas or the volume of a gas (a fixed mass held at constant temperature) when either the pressure or volume is increased or decreased.

### Explained

If the same molecules have a bigger space to move in at the same speed, they hit each part of the wall less often. Fewer collisions per second on each square metre means lower pressure. Halve the volume and the pressure doubles; double the volume and the pressure halves.

Because pV is constant, you can write p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ for the before and after states.

### Worked example

A gas has a volume of 0.060 m³ at a pressure of 100 000 Pa. It is compressed at constant temperature to 0.020 m³. Calculate the new pressure.

1. p₁V₁ = 100 000 × 0.060 = 6000.
2. p₂ = 6000 ÷ 0.020 = 300 000 Pa.

### Common mistakes

- Using pV = constant when the temperature changes. It only works at constant temperature.
- Mixing units on the two sides. Any volume unit works as long as both volumes use the same one.

## 4.3.3.3 Increasing the pressure of a gas (Physics only) (HT only)

### What you need to know

- Work is the transfer of energy by a force.
- Doing work on a gas increases the internal energy of the gas and can cause an increase in the temperature of the gas.
- You should be able to explain how, in a given situation (for example a bicycle pump), doing work on an enclosed gas leads to an increase in the temperature of the gas.

### Explained

When you push a bike pump handle in, you apply a force over a distance, so you do work on the gas. The piston moves towards the molecules, so molecules bounce off it faster than they hit it. Their kinetic energy goes up, so the internal energy and temperature of the gas rise. That is why the pump barrel feels warm after a few strokes.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the pump gets warm only because of friction. Friction adds a little, but the main reason is the work done on the gas.

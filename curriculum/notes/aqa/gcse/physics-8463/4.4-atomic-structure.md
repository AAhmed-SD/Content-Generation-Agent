# 4.4 Atomic structure

Atoms have a tiny, dense nucleus surrounded by electrons. Some nuclei are unstable and give out radiation as they decay. This topic covers the structure of the atom and how the model developed, isotopes, alpha, beta, gamma and neutron radiation, nuclear equations, half-life, contamination and irradiation, and (Physics only) background radiation, medical uses, fission and fusion.

## 4.4.1.1 The structure of an atom

### What you need to know

- Atoms are very small, with a radius of about 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.
- The basic structure of an atom is a positively charged nucleus made of protons and neutrons, surrounded by negatively charged electrons.
- The radius of a nucleus is less than 1/10 000 of the radius of an atom. Most of the mass of an atom is concentrated in the nucleus.
- The electrons are arranged at different distances from the nucleus (different energy levels). The electron arrangements may change with the absorption of electromagnetic radiation (moving further from the nucleus to a higher energy level) or by the emission of electromagnetic radiation (moving closer to the nucleus to a lower energy level).

### Explained

| Particle | Relative charge | Relative mass | Where |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Proton | +1 | 1 | Nucleus |
| Neutron | 0 | 1 | Nucleus |
| Electron | −1 | Very small (about 1/2000) | Energy levels around the nucleus |

Scale: if an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be about the size of a pea on the centre spot. Almost all of the atom is empty space, yet nearly all the mass is in that pea.

An electron that absorbs the right amount of electromagnetic radiation jumps to a higher energy level, further out. When it drops back, it gives out electromagnetic radiation.

### Common mistakes

- Writing the size of the nucleus as 1/10 000 of the atom's mass. It is about its radius.

## 4.4.1.2 Mass number, atomic number and isotopes

### What you need to know

- In an atom, the number of electrons is equal to the number of protons in the nucleus. Atoms have no overall electrical charge.
- All atoms of a particular element have the same number of protons. The number of protons in an atom of an element is called its atomic number.
- The total number of protons and neutrons in an atom is called its mass number.
- Atoms can be represented with the mass number written top left and the atomic number bottom left of the symbol, for example sodium-23 as ²³₁₁Na.
- Atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons; these atoms are called isotopes of that element.
- Atoms turn into positive ions if they lose one or more outer electrons.
- You should be able to relate differences between isotopes to differences in conventional representations of their identities, charges and masses.

### Worked example

How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in an atom of ³⁷₁₇Cl?

1. Protons = atomic number = 17.
2. Neutrons = mass number − atomic number = 37 − 17 = 20.
3. Electrons = protons = 17 (it is a neutral atom).

Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are isotopes: both have 6 protons, but carbon-12 has 6 neutrons and carbon-14 has 8.

### Common mistakes

- Thinking isotopes have different numbers of protons. Then they would be different elements.

## 4.4.1.3 The development of the model of the atom

### What you need to know

- New experimental evidence may lead to a scientific model being changed or replaced.
- Before the discovery of the electron, atoms were thought to be tiny spheres that could not be divided.
- The discovery of the electron led to the plum pudding model of the atom: a ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it.
- The results from the alpha particle scattering experiment led to the conclusion that the mass of an atom was concentrated at the centre (nucleus) and that the nucleus was charged. This nuclear model replaced the plum pudding model.
- Niels Bohr adapted the nuclear model by suggesting that electrons orbit the nucleus at specific distances. Theoretical calculations of Bohr agreed with experimental observations.
- Later experiments led to the idea that the positive charge of any nucleus could be subdivided into a whole number of smaller particles, each particle having the same amount of positive charge. The name proton was given to these particles.
- The experimental work of James Chadwick provided the evidence to show the existence of neutrons within the nucleus. This was about 20 years after the nucleus became an accepted scientific idea.
- You should be able to describe why the new evidence from the scattering experiment led to a change in the atomic model, and the difference between the plum pudding model and the nuclear model.

### Explained

The alpha scattering experiment: positive alpha particles were fired at a very thin sheet of gold foil.

| Observation | What it showed |
| --- | --- |
| Most alpha particles went straight through | Most of the atom is empty space |
| Some were deflected by small angles | The centre of the atom is positively charged (it repels the positive alpha particles) |
| A very few bounced almost straight back | The mass and charge are concentrated in a very small nucleus |

The plum pudding model could not explain the bouncing back, because its positive charge and mass were spread out thinly, so alpha particles would all pass through with only tiny deflections.

Timeline: solid sphere → plum pudding (electron discovered) → nuclear model (alpha scattering) → Bohr model (electrons in fixed shells) → protons → neutrons (Chadwick).

### Common mistakes

- Saying the scattering experiment discovered neutrons. It showed a small, dense, charged nucleus; neutrons came later.

## 4.4.2.1 Radioactive decay and nuclear radiation

### What you need to know

- Some atomic nuclei are unstable. The nucleus gives out radiation as it changes to become more stable. This is a random process called radioactive decay.
- Activity is the rate at which a source of unstable nuclei decays. Activity is measured in becquerel (Bq).
- Count-rate is the number of decays recorded each second by a detector (for example a Geiger-Muller tube).
- The nuclear radiation emitted may be:
  - an alpha particle (α): two neutrons and two protons (the same as a helium nucleus)
  - a beta particle (β): a high speed electron ejected from the nucleus as a neutron turns into a proton
  - a gamma ray (γ): electromagnetic radiation from the nucleus
  - a neutron (n).
- You should be able to apply your knowledge to the uses of radiation and evaluate the best sources of radiation to use in a given situation.

### Explained

| Radiation | What it is | Ionising power | Range in air | Stopped by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alpha | 2 protons + 2 neutrons | Strongly ionising | A few centimetres | A sheet of paper, or skin |
| Beta | Fast electron from the nucleus | Moderately ionising | About a metre | A few millimetres of aluminium |
| Gamma | Electromagnetic wave | Weakly ionising | Very far | Several centimetres of lead or metres of concrete (reduced, not fully stopped) |

Ionising means knocking electrons off atoms, turning them into ions.

Choosing a source:

- Smoke alarm: alpha, because it ionises air well but is stopped by smoke particles.
- Paper or foil thickness control: beta, because a small change in thickness changes how much gets through.
- Tracer in the body or sterilising equipment: gamma, because it passes out of the body or through packaging.

### Common mistakes

- Saying a beta particle is an electron from the shells. It comes from the nucleus.
- Saying 1 Bq is one decay per minute. It is one decay per second.

## 4.4.2.2 Nuclear equations

### What you need to know

- Nuclear equations are used to represent radioactive decay.
- In a nuclear equation an alpha particle may be written as ⁴₂He and a beta particle as ⁰₋₁e.
- The emission of the different types of nuclear radiation may cause a change in the mass and/or the charge of the nucleus:
  - alpha decay causes both the mass and charge of the nucleus to decrease (mass number down 4, atomic number down 2)
  - beta decay does not cause the mass of the nucleus to change, but causes the charge of the nucleus to increase (atomic number up 1)
  - the emission of a gamma ray does not cause the mass or the charge of the nucleus to change.
- You should be able to use the names and symbols of common nuclei and particles to write balanced equations that show single alpha and beta decay.

### Worked example

Radium-226 (atomic number 88) decays by alpha emission to radon (Rn). Write the equation.

²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He

Check: top numbers 226 = 222 + 4; bottom numbers 88 = 86 + 2.

Carbon-14 (atomic number 6) decays by beta emission to nitrogen (N).

¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e

Check: 14 = 14 + 0; 6 = 7 + (−1).

### Common mistakes

- Making the atomic number go down in beta decay. A neutron becomes a proton, so it goes up by 1.
- Forgetting the element symbol changes whenever the atomic number changes.

## 4.4.2.3 Half-lives and the random nature of radioactive decay

### What you need to know

- Radioactive decay is random. You cannot tell which nucleus will decay next or when.
- The half-life of a radioactive isotope is the time it takes for the number of nuclei of the isotope in a sample to halve, or the time it takes for the count rate (or activity) from a sample containing the isotope to fall to half its initial level.
- You should be able to explain the concept of half-life and how it is related to the random nature of radioactive decay.
- You should be able to determine the half-life of a radioactive isotope from given information.
- (HT only) You should be able to calculate the net decline, expressed as a ratio, in a radioactive emission after a given number of half-lives.

### Explained

Each nucleus has the same chance of decaying in any second, like flipping coins. You cannot predict one coin, but with millions you can predict that about half land heads. That is why half-life is constant for a large sample, even though each decay is random.

To find half-life from a graph of activity against time: read the starting activity, halve it, go across to the curve and down to the time axis. Do it again from a different starting point to check.

### Worked example

A sample has an activity of 800 Bq. Its half-life is 3 hours. What is its activity after 12 hours?

1. Number of half-lives = 12 ÷ 3 = 4.
2. 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 → 50 Bq.

(HT only) What is the net decline as a ratio after 4 half-lives?

1. After n half-lives the fraction remaining is (½)ⁿ.
2. (½)⁴ = 1/16. The ratio of final to initial activity is 1 : 16.
3. The net decline is 15/16 of the original activity.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the sample is gone after two half-lives. After two half-lives a quarter is still left.

## 4.4.2.4 Radioactive contamination

### What you need to know

- Radioactive contamination is the unwanted presence of materials containing radioactive atoms on other materials. The hazard from contamination is due to the decay of the contaminating atoms. The type of radiation emitted affects the level of hazard.
- Irradiation is the process of exposing an object to nuclear radiation. The irradiated object does not become radioactive.
- Suitable precautions must be taken to protect against any hazard that the radioactive source used in the process of irradiation may present.
- You should be able to compare the hazards associated with contamination and irradiation.
- It is important for the findings of studies into the effects of radiation on humans to be published and shared with other scientists so that the findings can be checked by peer review.

### Explained

| | Contamination | Irradiation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What happens | Radioactive material gets onto or into something | Something is exposed to radiation from a source outside it |
| Does the object become radioactive? | Yes, it carries the radioactive atoms | No |
| Does it stop when you move away? | No, it goes with you | Yes, it stops when the source is removed or shielded |
| Most dangerous type | Alpha inside the body (strongly ionising, nothing to stop it) | Gamma and beta from outside (they reach the body; alpha is stopped by skin) |

Precautions: use tongs, keep sources in lead-lined boxes, keep as far away as possible, keep exposure time short, wear gloves and film badges.

Peer review: sharing results lets other scientists check methods and repeat studies, so safety limits are based on reliable evidence.

### Common mistakes

- Saying irradiated food is radioactive. It is not.

## 4.4.3.1 Background radiation (Physics only)

### What you need to know

- Background radiation is around us all of the time. It comes from:
  - natural sources such as rocks and cosmic rays from space
  - man-made sources such as the fallout from nuclear weapons testing and nuclear accidents.
- The level of background radiation and radiation dose may be affected by occupation and/or location.
- Radiation dose is measured in sieverts (Sv). 1000 millisieverts (mSv) = 1 sievert.

### Explained

Radon gas from granite rock is the largest natural source in the UK, so people in areas such as Cornwall get a higher dose. Pilots and aircrew get more cosmic radiation because they spend time at high altitude. Medical staff who work with X-rays or radioactive tracers are monitored because of their work.

When measuring a source's count-rate, measure background first and subtract it.

### Common mistakes

- Thinking most background radiation is man-made. Most is natural.

## 4.4.3.2 Different half-lives of radioactive isotopes (Physics only)

### What you need to know

- Radioactive isotopes have a very wide range of half-life values.
- You should be able to explain why the hazards associated with radioactive material differ according to the half-life involved.

### Explained

A short half-life source decays quickly, so it is very active at first but soon falls to a safe level. A long half-life source is less active at any moment but stays radioactive for a very long time, so it stays a hazard for years (for example nuclear waste that must be stored for thousands of years).

### Common mistakes

- Saying a long half-life is always more dangerous. It depends on time scale: short half-life gives a high dose quickly; long half-life lasts.

## 4.4.3.3 Uses of nuclear radiation (Physics only)

### What you need to know

- Nuclear radiations are used in medicine for:
  - exploration of internal organs
  - control or destruction of unwanted tissue.
- You should be able to describe and evaluate the uses of nuclear radiations for exploration of internal organs, and for control or destruction of unwanted tissue.
- You should be able to evaluate the perceived risks of using nuclear radiations in relation to given data and consequences.

### Explained

Tracers: a gamma emitter with a short half-life (hours) is injected or swallowed. Gamma passes out of the body to a camera outside, showing how an organ is working. A short half-life means it does not stay active in the body for long. Alpha would not get out and would damage cells.

Radiotherapy: a beam of gamma rays from outside the body is aimed at a tumour from several directions so the tumour gets a high dose and healthy tissue gets less. Sometimes a source is placed inside or next to the tumour.

Evaluating risk: weigh the benefit (finding or treating a disease) against the risk (cell damage or cancer from ionising radiation). Data such as dose in mSv compared with yearly background dose help decide.

### Common mistakes

- Choosing an alpha source for a medical tracer. It cannot be detected outside the body and is very damaging inside.

## 4.4.4.1 Nuclear fission (Physics only)

### What you need to know

- Nuclear fission is the splitting of a large and unstable nucleus (for example uranium or plutonium).
- Spontaneous fission is rare. Usually, for fission to occur the unstable nucleus must first absorb a neutron.
- The nucleus undergoing fission splits into two smaller nuclei, roughly equal in size, and emits two or three neutrons plus gamma rays. Energy is released by the fission reaction.
- All of the fission products have kinetic energy.
- The neutrons may go on to start a chain reaction.
- The chain reaction is controlled in a nuclear reactor to control the energy released. The explosion caused by a nuclear weapon is caused by an uncontrolled chain reaction.
- You should be able to draw or interpret diagrams representing nuclear fission and how a chain reaction may occur.

### Explained

A slow neutron is absorbed by a uranium-235 nucleus. The nucleus becomes unstable and splits into two daughter nuclei, releasing 2 or 3 neutrons and gamma rays. Each new neutron can be absorbed by another uranium nucleus, which splits and releases more neutrons, and so on.

In a reactor, control rods absorb some of the neutrons so that, on average, only one neutron from each fission causes another fission. A moderator slows neutrons down so they can be absorbed. The energy heats water to make steam for turbines.

A chain reaction diagram: one neutron → one nucleus splits → three neutrons → three nuclei split → nine neutrons, and so on.

### Common mistakes

- Saying fission happens when a nucleus is hit and "smashed". The neutron is absorbed first.

## 4.4.4.2 Nuclear fusion (Physics only)

### What you need to know

- Nuclear fusion is the joining of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus. In this process some of the mass may be converted into the energy of radiation.

### Explained

In the Sun, hydrogen nuclei fuse to make helium. The helium nucleus has slightly less mass than the nuclei that made it; the missing mass is released as energy. Fusion needs very high temperatures and pressures because the positive nuclei repel each other and must be moving very fast to get close enough to join. This is why fusion power stations are so hard to build.

| | Fission | Fusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What happens | Large nucleus splits | Light nuclei join |
| Fuel | Uranium or plutonium | Hydrogen isotopes |
| Where | Nuclear power stations today | Stars; experimental reactors |

### Common mistakes

- Confusing the two. Fission is splitting, fusion is joining (think "fuse together").

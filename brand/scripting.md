# Scripting

How a film script sounds. What the films are about, and in what mix, is `pillars.md`. Read with `product-facts.md` (what may be claimed), `safety.md` (what may never be said), and the niche file (who it is for). This file decides the voice; those files decide the content.

## Pacing

Viewers must follow it on the first listen.

- **Line one says what the film is about.** "The receipt for your Sunday revision." Not a riddle.
- **One idea per line, under about ten words.** Split anything longer into two lines.
- **Leave air.** Give each line an `after` pause of 0.4 to 0.6 seconds, 0.7 before the product line, and 0.9 to 1.2 at the end. The picture changes in the pause.
- **The product line is its own short line,** with "Offload" in it, after a pause.
- **When the words say chaos, the picture shows chaos:** a flood, a pile, shake, overflow, a red count going up. "Everything" is never three tidy cards. Formats with a chaos layer: `kanban` (pile), `inbox` and `lockscreen` (flood), `dialog` (cascade), `weather` (a storm day).

## The shape

1. **The first line is the whole hook.** Under two seconds. Specific, a little uncomfortable, true. "When did you last open anatomy?" beats "Revision can be hard."
2. **The on-screen words add to the voice.** They do not repeat it. Voice: "Be honest." Screen: "When did you last open anatomy?"
3. **One idea per film.** If it needs "and also", it is two films.
4. **The useful part comes before the product.** The tip, the reveal, the joke. A viewer who leaves before the product still got something.
5. **The product is one short line, said like a person.** "The app brings each card back right before it fades." Not: "Offload builds flashcards from your own lecture notes, and brings each one back right before you'd forget it."
6. **Something changes every two to three seconds:** a new line on screen, a cut, a reveal.
7. **Leave a reason to stay:** a question answered at the end, "the third one is the one nobody does", a loop back to the first line.

## Words

- Be specific: "pharmacology lecture six, still unopened, Thursday, 11pm" beats "your lectures".
- Use contractions. Short sentences. One clause where you can.
- A little humour, never at the viewer's expense.
- British English and UK terms: revise, placement, finals, mocks, sixth form.
- Write for the ear. Read it aloud. If you run out of breath, cut it.

**Retired themes (2026-11-05).** These were used in too many films. Do not use them again, not as a hook, a joke, an example or a background detail:

- Colour-coding, colouring in, or making a timetable look nice.
- Rereading, rereading notes, or rereading worked solutions.
- Delay and starting jokes: "delayed", "I'll start later", "getting ready to start", "the afternoon disappeared". Overused (2026-11-05); paused while pillar 1 in `pillars.md` leads.

Pick a different everyday habit instead. Some that are still fresh: watching a lecture back at double speed, saving TikToks "for later", the fridge, the group chat, "I'll do a big session on Sunday", ten tabs open, starting with the easiest topic, the night bus home from a shift, the problem sheet started at 11pm. When one of these has been used in three films, retire it here too.

Never use: "game-changer", "unlock", "level up", "hack", "journey", "seamless", "AI-powered", "study smarter not harder", "in today's video", "let's dive in", "it's that simple".

## Where a script comes from (the script lead, 2026-11-07)

A film is never improvised from a topic. It is built from four parts, picked before a word is written:

1. **One truth** from the account's truth bank (`marketing.truths`, built from `brand/niches/<account>-dossier.md`): a specific moment the viewer will recognise as theirs. The film opens on it and stays on it. Prefer fresh truths in season (the right month), and never one used in the last 60 days.
2. **One shape** from `brand/shapes.md`: the structure, with beats by the second. Follow its beats; do not invent a structure.
3. **One hook pattern** from the account's trend patterns (`marketing.hook_patterns`, learnt each week from what is over-performing): the shape of the first line, filled from the truth. The hook style rules still apply.
4. **One takeaway**, written before the script: the single sentence a viewer should be able to repeat after one watch. If you cannot write it, the film is not ready.

## Explaining Offload

Most viewers have never heard of Offload, and a claim they cannot picture slides straight past them. The explanation must be shown, tied to the film's own truth, and short.

- **Answer the truth, not the brochure.** The product beat answers the exact moment the film opened on. A film about the October question you got wrong explains that the wrong answer comes back. It does not list the planner too.
- **Show the app doing it, on screen,** while the voice says it plainly: the card coming back, the block moving to Thursday, the link in the calendar. The voice never describes what the screen already shows.
- **Three short beats, in this order, each its own line,** worded to fit the film. Use only what this film needs, and at least beat one:
  1. *It plans for you.* "Offload builds your revision plan from your notes and exam dates, and moves it when a shift runs late."
  2. *It teaches and tests you.* "It tests you as you go, and brings back what you got wrong before you forget it."
  3. *You just show up.* "You click the link in your calendar and start."
- **Concrete verbs, the viewer as the subject of the outcome:** "your wrong answers come back", not "Offload leverages spaced repetition". No feature names unless the film is about that feature.
- **Name Offload once,** at the start of beat one, then "it". The tagline sits on the end card, never spoken.
- **The before and after must be visible.** Show the problem state and the Offload state in the same picture language (the same week, the same deck, the same paper).
- **Only claims in `product-facts.md`.** If the truth's problem is one Offload does not solve, the film is a useful film with a light product beat, or it is not made.

> Weak: "Offload uses spaced repetition and tracks your attempts so you can see your progress."
>
> Strong (on a film about the anatomy question missed in October): "Offload keeps every question you've got wrong. It brings that one back next week. Before you've forgotten it again."

## Delivery: say it so it lands

A film is heard once, on a phone, with half the viewer's attention. Most failed scripts so far were not wrong, they were unclear. Before a script is voiced, it must pass these:

- **Say what it is about, out loud.** Within the first two lines, the film says it is about studying: revision, homework, coursework, an exam, a lecture. Never assume the viewer knows the context, the course or the app. A first-time viewer who misses the picture should still know, from the words alone, that this is about studying and what the problem is.
- **Name the thing, then explain why.** Say the specific struggle in plain words, then the reason it happens (on the time account, the executive function behind it: working memory, starting, time, planning), then what helps. A viewer should never have to guess the mechanism.
- **The cold-viewer test.** Someone who has never heard of Offload, watching once, can say what the film was about, what to do differently, and what Offload does, in one sentence each. If any is fuzzy, rewrite.
- **Every line is a complete thought.** No line depends on a word the viewer missed. No "it", "this" or "that" without its noun in the same or previous line.
- **Concrete nouns over concepts.** "Your pharmacology flashcards" beats "your content". "Thursday after your shift" beats "later in the week".
- **One move per line:** set up, turn, proof or payoff. A line that does two is two lines.
- **Build, then land.** Short lines land the turn: "Paper can't move." The landing line is the shortest in its beat.
- **No riddles in the hook,** no metaphor the viewer has to decode, no in-joke before the context.
- **Say numbers and acronyms the way people say them.** "Nine a.m." Write exam names the way students say them.
- **The screen text carries the key word,** not the sentence: the one or two words the viewer must catch.
- **One attitude per film:** warm, dry or urgent, chosen from the shape and the truth, and kept.
- **Every claim is true of the picture.** If a line says "only", "every", "none", "empty" or "busy", or names a count, a day, a time or a subject, the screen shows exactly that. "Your only study session" cannot play over a week with five study blocks; say what is specifically lost ("the only slot for pharmacology").
- **The picture says the same thing as the words.** If the voice says "packed week", "only free day" or "everything at once", the screen shows a packed week, one gap, everything at once. A line about chaos never plays over a calm screen, and a line about one thing never plays over ten.
- **Read it aloud at speed.** If you stumble, the voice will too.

## Endings

Rotate. Do not use the same ending twice in a row on one account.

- A loop back to the first line, as a question.
- A punchline.
- "Part two?" when there is one to make.
- "Save this for the night before."
- "Comment your letter" only on a choice film, and at most twice a week per account.
- The spoken tagline: rare.

## Before and after

> Before: "Offload holds the plan, and moves the work when you miss a day. Get a card wrong, and it comes back sooner."
>
> After: "If you said A, the problem isn't you. Paper can't move. This one moves itself."

> Before: "You're eight weeks in, and you haven't touched anatomy since week two."
>
> After: "Quick check. When did you last open anatomy? If the answer's the week you learnt it, same for most of us."

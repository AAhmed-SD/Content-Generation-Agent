# Content pillars

What the films are about, and in what mix. Read with `scripting.md` (how a film sounds) and `hook-styles.md` (how it opens). Decided 2026-11-05.

## The idea under everything

Studying is an executive-function problem, and the hardest part is the one nobody sees: **knowing what you actually know.** People misjudge it all the time. Familiar feels like known. Confidence feels like evidence. The exam-style question you got wrong three months ago is still wrong, sitting in a deck you never opened again, and nothing reminds you.

The fix is honest data about your own learning. Offload keeps it: every attempt, every miss, what is fading. That depth of tracking is what makes Offload different, so most films should be about it.

## The four pillars

| Pillar | Share of films | What it is about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Know what you actually know** | About half | Metacognition and honest data. The illusion of knowing; confidence is not evidence; the forgotten wrong answer; revising what feels comfortable, not what is weak; why you need a record, not a feeling. |
| **2. The science, shown** | About a quarter | How learning works, explained with graphs and live method demos: the forgetting curve, spacing, the testing effect, blurting then checking the gaps, the Feynman technique, mixing topics, applied exam-style questions. Uses the `science` format (dark, graphs, studies named). |
| **3. Life around study** | About a fifth | Placement rotas, part-time jobs, January exams, mocks: a plan that moves when life does. |
| **4. Starting** | A tenth at most | Getting going, the next small block. Mostly the time account. **Paused for now:** the "delayed", "I'll start later" and "getting ready to start" jokes are overused (see `scripting.md`, Retired themes). |

In any batch of eight proposals: at least three from pillar 1, at least two science explainers (pillar 2, `science` format), and at most one from pillar 4. Mix the looks: science films with graphs, object films (receipt, evidence board, lock screen and so on), and the launch-film look in the `scene` format (a flashcard grid where the wrong card comes back, a marked paper with the marks you lost, a week that moves, the real app screen).

Pillars 1 and 2 overlap well: a science film about why familiar feels like known is both.

## Pillar 1, in practice

The truths to dramatise:
- You can't tell what you know by how it feels. Recognising the page is not recalling it.
- Your wrong answers don't remind you. The question you missed in October is still missed.
- Without a record, you revise what you're already good at, because it feels productive.
- Confidence and accuracy are different numbers. Most people never see the second one.
- Depth beats coverage: knowing the five topics you're weak at is worth more than skimming fifty.

What Offload may say about it (only these, from `product-facts.md`):
- Practice is scored, and every attempt is kept (Results: attempt history).
- Weak answers come back sooner: spaced repetition shortens the gap, and a weak answer can create remediation cards and book re-learn time.
- A weekly report and a dashboard show what's due and how it's going.

Do not claim percentages, mastery scores, accuracy figures or anything else not in `product-facts.md`. No study statistics ("research shows 70%") unless the week's brief carries them.

Hook angles that fit:
- "When did you last see the question you got wrong in October?"
- "You think you know cardiology. What does your data say?"
- "Confidence isn't evidence."
- "The deck you failed three months ago is still failing. Quietly."
- "Name the three topics you're weakest at. Without guessing."
- "Your revision is mostly things you already know."

Formats that suit it: evidence board (the case against your confidence), exam paper (marked honestly), receipt (what you actually revised), tier list (what you think you know against what you do), wrapped (your week in real numbers, kept honest), jail (the app that never told you what you got wrong).

## Pillar 2, in practice

Each science film explains one method or one finding, shows it working, then lands on Offload's three beats.

| Topic | Show it with | Name the source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The forgetting curve | `curve` (forget) | Ebbinghaus, 1885 |
| Spacing beats cramming | `curve` (cram-vs-spaced or spaced) | Cepeda et al., 2006 |
| Testing beats going over notes | `bars` (relative heights only) | Roediger & Karpicke, 2006 |
| Blurting: recall, then check the gaps | `blurt` | (a method; no study needed) |
| The Feynman technique | `feynman` loop | (a method; no study needed) |
| Mixing topics (interleaving) | `bars` or `steps` | Rohrer & Taylor, 2007 |
| Applied, scenario-style questions | `scenario` | (exam technique) |

Rules for the science:
- **Graphs are shapes, not data.** No numbers on axes or bars unless the week's brief carries them with a source. Relative heights and curve shapes only.
- **Name the real study** when a graph rests on one, and only studies listed here or in the brief.
- **No overclaiming:** "beats", "lasts longer" and "fades slower" are fine; no "3x better" and no "guaranteed".
- **Clinical scenario questions** test applying knowledge (a mechanism, a drug class, which investigation first) and are never a patient case or a diagnosis (see `safety.md`). GCSE scenarios are exam-style application questions, never a real paper's wording.

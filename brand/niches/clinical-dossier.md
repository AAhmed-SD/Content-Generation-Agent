**Two corrections to the account file:**
1. **Radiography, not "radiology".** Radiology is a doctor's specialty. These students study **radiography** and become radiographers. Calling them "radiology students" is the quickest way to lose them. Use "radiography" everywhere.
2. **When the MLA content map changes.** The new map was published in October 2025. It applies to **all MLA exams and assessments taken from September 2026**. That covers final-year and penultimate-year UK students sitting MLA assessments in 2026–27 **(checked: regulator's content map and "you said, we did" document)**. The file's "written before January 2026" is close but not exact. The true hook is: "anything written for the old map may not match the exam you sit this year."

## 1. Who they are

Four regulated courses. All four mix university teaching with clinical placement, and all four end in professional registration rather than just a degree. What ties them together: **revision has to fit around a rota they don't control, and passing is a gate to a job, not just a grade.**

### Medicine (regulator: the GMC)

- **Degree:** MBBS / MBChB / BMBS, depending on the school. **Five years** is standard. It is **six** with an intercalated degree or a foundation/gateway year. **Four years** on graduate-entry courses.
- **Age:** school-leavers start at 18 (17 for some in Scotland), so most are 18–24. Graduate-entry and mature students are typically 21–35.
- **Where:** about 40+ medical schools across England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. Some run additional campuses, and a few have campuses abroad. Clinical years are spread across district general hospitals and GP practices, often hours from the main campus. Students move house or commute between "placement sites" several times a year.
- **How many (background, not re-checked):** UK intake is roughly in the 9,000–11,000 range a year, so 40,000–55,000 medical students at any one time.
- **The years:**
  - **Years 1–2 ("preclinical"):** lectures, problem- or case-based learning groups, anatomy (dissection or prosection), physiology, biochemistry, communication skills, and early GP or hospital visits. Exams are written papers (single best answer, sometimes short answer), early OSCEs, anatomy spotters, and progress tests at some schools.
  - **Optional intercalation:** a one-year extra degree, usually after year 2, 3 or 4.
  - **Years 3–4 ("clinical"):** rotations of a few weeks each through medicine, surgery, GP, paediatrics, obstetrics and gynaecology, psychiatry, and specialties. Days are on wards, in clinics and in theatre. There are workplace-based assessments to get signed, an e-portfolio, end-of-year written exams and OSCEs, and an elective, usually in year 4 or 5.
  - **Final year:** the **UKMLA**, made up of the **AKT** (written) and the **CPSA** (clinical, run by each school as its finals OSCE) **(checked)**. Also the **foundation programme application** (September–October), the **Prescribing Safety Assessment** (not re-checked), and the **assistantship**, a "be the F1" placement. Then shadowing and starting as an F1 doctor in early August.
- **What makes them different:** the longest course, the most standardised national exam, and the most crowded market of paid question banks and revision tools. Their "first job" goes through a national allocation they don't control. The pressure is competitive and comparative: there's always someone in the year group with a higher question-bank percentage.

### Dentistry (regulator: the GDC)

- **Degree:** BDS, **five years**. Graduate-entry and accelerated routes for medical graduates exist (four years or less). There are also a few international-route programmes.
- **Age:** mostly 18–24, with a graduate-entry minority.
- **Where:** a small number of dental schools, around 16 offering the BDS (not re-checked). Teaching hospitals plus outreach clinics in the community.
- **How many (background, not re-checked):** about 1,100–1,300 UK places a year, so roughly 6,000 dental students in total.
- **The years:**
  - **Years 1–2:** biomedical science, dental anatomy, and **simulation ("sim lab", "phantom heads")** where they practise drilling and restoring plastic teeth. Some schools see patients from year 1 **(checked: one school says so on its course page)**.
  - **Years 3–5:** treating real patients under supervision on clinic. They work across restorative dentistry, periodontics, endodontics, prosthodontics, oral surgery, orthodontics and paediatric dentistry, plus outreach placements.
  - **Throughout:** clinical activity is logged on a **digital clinical assessment and feedback system**. Students must reach **minimum clinical requirements** to be allowed to sit finals. Some schools require more than half of procedures to reach a set level of independence. Schools also require **simulation assessments to be passed before students can do those treatments on patients** **(checked: regulator inspection reports)**.
  - **Final year:** BDS finals (written papers, clinical exams, case presentations, OSCE-type stations; the format varies by school). Also **Dental Foundation Training (DFT) national recruitment**: application in the summer before final year, a **situational judgement test (SJT) in November**, preferencing in spring, offers in June **(checked)**.
- **What makes them different:** they need patients to pass. A patient who doesn't turn up is a missing requirement, not a free hour. They carry practical, hand-skills pressure and academic pressure at once, in a small year group where everyone knows everyone's numbers. Small schools, small cohorts, intense. **The DFT SJT window is open right now (4–12 November 2026).**

### Nursing (regulator: the NMC)

- **Degree:** BSc (Hons) Nursing, **three years** (four for honours in Scotland). They choose one of four **fields** from day one: **adult, children's (child), mental health, learning disability**. Some take dual-field degrees, MSc pre-registration (two years for graduates), or **degree apprenticeships** and the nursing associate route.
- **Age:** the oldest and most varied group. Many are 18–21, but a large share are mature students (late 20s to 40s+), often parents or carers, former healthcare assistants, or people working bank shifts alongside the course.
- **Where:** a large number of universities, in cities and regional campuses. Some have September and January/February/March intakes, so not everyone's year starts in September.
- **How many (background, not re-checked):** tens of thousands start each year across the UK, probably more than 25,000. Applications fell from their 2021 peak.
- **The structure:**
  - Roughly half theory, half practice. The current total is **4,600 hours, 2,300 in practice**, all logged and signed **(file)**. The NMC's 2026 practice learning review proposes cutting the total to 3,600 while keeping 2,300 in practice. **It is a consultation, not a rule** **(file)**.
  - Placements are **supernumerary** (in theory, not counted as staff). They are done in blocks across wards, community, care homes and specialist services ("spoke" visits).
  - Assessment in practice runs through a **practice assessment document (PAD)**, often electronic, plus an **ongoing achievement record**. A **practice supervisor** and **practice assessor** sign it off on placement, with an **academic assessor** at the university (not re-checked).
  - At university: essays and reflective assignments, presentations, exams, **numeracy / drug-calculation assessments** (many universities require 100%; not re-checked), simulation, and a final-year dissertation or literature review.
  - **No national exam** for UK-trained nurses **(file)**.
- **Money (not re-checked):** in England, a non-repayable training grant (the NHS Learning Support Fund, about £5,000 a year, with extras for parents and travel). Scotland has its own bursary. Wales and Northern Ireland have their own arrangements.
- **What makes them different:** hours. Every missed shift is a debt that must be **made up**. They work real 12-hour shifts and nights unpaid, then write essays. More life outside the course (kids, jobs, caring) than any other group. Practice is assessed by a person signing a document, so whether a sign-off happens can depend on whether a busy ward has time for it. In recent years, final-years have also worried about whether a newly qualified job will be there (not re-checked in detail).

### Diagnostic radiography (regulator: the HCPC)

- **Degree:** BSc (Hons) Diagnostic Radiography, **three years** (four in Scotland). Also MSc pre-registration (two years) and apprenticeships. **Therapeutic radiography** (radiotherapy) is a separate, smaller course. Include it only if a brief asks.
- **Age:** mostly 18–25, with a meaningful mature minority.
- **Where:** a smaller number of universities. Placements are in hospital imaging departments: general X-ray, A&E, mobiles on wards, theatre, fluoroscopy, CT, MRI and others.
- **How many (background, not re-checked):** low thousands start each year across the UK.
- **The structure:** university blocks (anatomy, imaging physics, radiation protection and the law around exposures, image appraisal, patient care, pathology, research) alternate with placement blocks. First-year placements are often 9 to 5, about 30 hours a week. Later years add extended days, weekends, and some evenings or nights **(file)**. On placement they collect **competencies** signed off by radiographers. At university they sit exams (often including anatomy and physics) and **image evaluation / image appraisal** assessments, both written and viva-style. Final year brings a dissertation or research project.
- **What makes them different:** constantly mistaken for something else ("so you're going to be a radiologist?" or "the X-ray person"). They have heavy physics and anatomy recall. Their competencies depend on the right examinations coming through the door while they're on shift. Final-years report the same pressures as qualified staff: time and workload **(file)**.

## 2. Their year, month by month

**Caveat for the whole section:** clinical years don't follow normal university terms. Medical clinical years often start in **mid-to-late August** and run 40–48 weeks. Nursing runs 45-week years with blocks, and some cohorts start in January to March. Exact dates are set **by each school**. Never state a national exam date for school-set exams.

### September
- **What's happening:**
  - Freshers and welcome weeks for year 1 of all four courses, usually mid-to-late September. Returning preclinical students start lectures; clinical medics are already a few weeks into rotations.
  - **Final-year medics:** nomination emails for the 2027 Foundation Programme arrived from **15 September 2026**. The **national application window ran 23 September to 7 October 2026 (12:00 midday)**, with no late applications under any circumstances **(checked)**.
  - New nursing cohorts start inductions, uniform fittings, occupational health checks and mandatory training (manual handling, basic life support, infection control).
  - Radiography year 1s start anatomy and physics. Some get a short observational placement early on.
- **Worried about:** "Is everyone already ahead?" Placement allocations: where am I, how far is the commute? Final-years: the foundation application and preferencing every foundation school in order. Money: the first loan instalment versus rent and the first travel costs.
- **Doing instead of studying:** societies (medics' and nurses' societies, sports clubs, MedSoc / DentSoc socials), moving into new houses, buying stethoscopes, scrubs and fob watches, making flashcards they'll never review, setting up a notes app "properly".

### October
- **What's happening:**
  - **7 October 2026: the foundation application closes.** Nothing changes after that except preferences.
  - Medical schools that use them run **progress tests** in this term.
  - First nursing assignments set. Some year 1 nurses start first placement in October or November.
  - Clinical medics move rotation. Dental students settle into clinic lists.
  - Clocks go back (25 October 2026), so the walk home from placement is now dark.
  - Reading weeks at some universities.
- **Worried about:** post-application anticlimax, then "now I actually have to revise for the AKT". Year 1 nurses dread the first placement. The first essay with academic referencing.
- **Instead:** Halloween socials, freshers' flu, part-time and bank shifts, rearranging their revision plan for the third time.

### November (now)
- **What's happening:**
  - **Final-year dental students sit the DFT situational judgement test, 4–12 November 2026** **(checked)**.
  - Final-year medics are mid-placement with AKT revision ramping up. At schools with January–February AKTs, it's "three months out" panic.
  - Nursing and radiography placement blocks.
  - Mid-semester deadlines.
  - Specialised foundation (academic) programme interviews start at some places (not re-checked).
  - Winter pressures start, so wards are busier and less teaching happens.
- **Worried about:** the question-bank backlog. "I've done 12% of the bank." Dark mornings, 07:00 starts, freshers'-flu-turned-placement-cough. Dentists: patients cancelling as it gets colder.
- **Instead:** Christmas socials and balls start, Black Friday, bank shifts for Christmas money, doomscrolling in the hospital car park.

### December
- **What's happening:**
  - Last placement weeks before the break.
  - Assignment deadlines just before Christmas or in early January.
  - **Christmas break** is usually about two weeks for clinical years. Preclinical students may get three.
  - Some nursing students have placement shifts running close to Christmas, depending on their block.
  - Christmas jumper day on wards.
- **Worried about:** "I'll revise over Christmas" against the reality of family, travel and part-time work. AKT-in-January students give up Christmas. Essays due on 5 January.
- **Instead:** going home, work shifts, Christmas parties, sleep, guilt about not revising, making a "Christmas revision timetable" that collapses by 27 December.

### January
- **What's happening:**
  - **AKT season begins at many medical schools** (January to February). One London school's published dates put its AKT on **2–3 February 2027** **(file)**.
  - January exams for preclinical years.
  - Semester 2 starts.
  - New nursing cohorts start at universities with January intakes.
  - Final-year nurses and radiographers start applying for newly qualified jobs (from January; not re-checked).
  - Dentists keep pushing to reach clinical numbers before finals.
  - Blue Monday content everywhere (they find it naff).
- **Worried about:** the AKT, finals, money (the loan gap after Christmas), and the dark.
- **Instead:** "new year new me" planning, buying a new planner, January gym, part-time shifts.

### February
- **What's happening:**
  - AKTs continue at many schools.
  - The **Prescribing Safety Assessment** typically has a first sitting window around February to March (not re-checked; check with each school).
  - **Ramadan 2027 is expected to begin around 8 February** (date depends on moon sighting), so many students will fast through AKT season. That affects energy, sleep and exam timing.
  - Reading weeks.
  - Elective planning and funding applications.
  - **Foundation programme allocation:** in 2026 it was delayed from 26 February to **12 March 2026** while the prioritisation law passed **(checked)**. The 2027 allocation date was not confirmed in what I found. Say "early spring" if needed, never a date.
- **Worried about:** results of January exams, the AKT pass, allocation anxiety ("what if I get sent to the other end of the country?").
- **Instead:** Valentine's socials, reading-week trips, more bank shifts.

### March
- **What's happening:**
  - Foundation allocation results (likely around now). Then programme preferencing.
  - **OSCE and CPSA preparation** for many years: practice stations in groups, mock OSCEs run by student societies.
  - Placement blocks.
  - **Easter 2027 falls on 28 March**, so Easter breaks span late March into April.
- **Worried about:** "I've never examined a real knee properly." Practising in front of peers. Final-years: what if I fail finals now, after all this?
- **Instead:** practising examinations on flatmates, Easter travel, comparing foundation allocations in the group chat.

### April
- **What's happening:**
  - The Easter break, which is mainly revision for anyone sitting summer exams.
  - **DFT applicant preferencing: 7 April to 6 May 2027** **(checked)**.
  - CPSA/finals OSCEs at some medical schools (March to May).
  - Final nursing placements running: the long final placement with final PAD sign-off (often around 10–12 weeks; not re-checked).
  - Radiography final placements.
- **Worried about:** the "summer exam" countdown, final sign-offs, dissertation deadlines.
- **Instead:** Easter at home, first sunny days, beer gardens, sports finals.

### May
- **What's happening:**
  - **Peak exam season for non-final years:** written papers and OSCEs for medicine and dentistry years 1–4, radiography and nursing module exams.
  - Finals OSCEs at several schools.
  - Dissertation hand-ins.
  - Second PSA window around May to June (not re-checked).
  - Foundation programme matching.
  - Mental Health Awareness Week (mid-May).
  - Bank holidays on 3 and 31 May 2027.
- **Worried about:** everything at once. Exam timetables, OSCE stations, hay fever during exams, sleep.
- **Instead:** library camping, lying in the park "doing flashcards", watching other people post revision setups.

### June
- **What's happening:**
  - Exams finish; results come out.
  - Finals results at many medical and dental schools.
  - **Regions informed of DFT offers: 9 June 2027** **(checked)**.
  - Nursing and radiography final assessment boards. Applications to join the register follow.
  - Graduation season begins.
  - Resit notifications.
- **Worried about:** results emails, resits ("summer's gone"), whether the newly qualified job is confirmed.
- **Instead:** balls, graduation plans, summer jobs, electives.

### July
- **What's happening:**
  - Graduations.
  - **Electives** for many medics (often between years 4 and 5 or after finals).
  - New F1s do **shadowing** and induction in late July.
  - Some clinical years have a short summer break.
  - Nurses and radiographers join the register and start preceptorship jobs.
  - Year 1 and 2 students are mostly off: summer jobs, healthcare assistant work, travel.
- **Worried about:** for F1s, the first rota and the first prescribing. For resitters, August.
- **Instead:** holidays, festivals, part-time work, family.

### August
- **What's happening:**
  - **Resits**, the "August resit" period.
  - **New F1 doctors start on the first Wednesday of August** (4 August 2027).
  - **Medical clinical years and some nursing years restart in mid-to-late August** at many schools.
  - A-level results day (mid-August) brings in next year's year 1s, and nursing recruits partly through Clearing.
  - **DFT starts on 1 September 2027** **(checked)**.
- **Worried about:** resits, a summer that was never really a summer, "placement already starts on the 23rd?"
- **Instead:** the last holiday, moving house for the next placement site.

## 4. A day in their life

### Day 1: Final-year medic, hospital placement day (November)
- **06:15:** Alarm. Second alarm. Checks the group chat: someone's done 80 questions already.
- **07:00:** Drives or catches two buses to a hospital 40 minutes away. Plans to do flashcards on the bus but feels sick reading, so listens to a podcast instead.
- **08:00–08:30:** Arrives, finds the ward, finds a computer that works.
- **08:30–11:30:** Ward round. Stands a lot. Writes in the notes if asked. The registrar is kind but busy.
- **11:30–13:00:** Jobs: bloods, a cannula if lucky. Tries to get a case discussion signed. The doctor says "remind me later".
- **13:00:** Lunch while doing 10 bank questions on the phone. Gets 4 right. Doesn't read the explanations.
- **14:00–16:30:** Clinic, or "self-directed learning" (aka the library or home).
- **17:00:** Home. Too tired. Dinner. Netflix "for 20 minutes".
- **20:00–22:30:** The real revision block: 40 bank questions on random mode, then the spaced-repetition app with 600 reviews due. Gets through 200.
- **23:30:** Doomscrolls. Sees a video of someone's perfect AKT timetable. Feels behind.
- **Where study time actually went:** about 2.5 hours, mostly in the evening, and half of it on reviews they'd fallen behind on rather than new material.

### Day 2: Year 1 medic or dentist, campus lecture day (October)
- **09:00–11:00:** Lectures. Half the year watches the recording later at 2x instead.
- **11:00–13:00:** Anatomy practical or sim lab.
- **13:00–14:00:** Lunch, society stall, group chat.
- **14:00–16:00:** Small-group learning session.
- **16:00–18:00:** "Library": 30 minutes making beautiful notes on a tablet, 30 minutes choosing a premade flashcard deck, 60 minutes on the phone.
- **19:00–late:** Society social or sports training.
- **Where study time went:** about an hour of real work, almost all of it passive (rewatching, making notes).

### Day 3: Nursing student, long day on placement (any month)
- **05:45:** Alarm. Uniform ironed last night. Packed lunch and snacks.
- **07:00–07:30:** Arrives, handover. Finds out who their practice supervisor is today. It's a new person.
- **07:30–12:00:** Observations, personal care, helping with the medicines round under supervision, answering call bells, admissions paperwork.
- **12:00–12:30:** Break (or not).
- **12:30–19:30:** More of the same. Discharges. One moment where they learn something properly. Asks about a mid-point interview: "Maybe Thursday?"
- **19:30–20:00:** Handover, hours sheet signed.
- **20:45:** Home. Shower, food, a child or partner to see.
- **22:00:** Opens the essay due Friday. Writes 150 words. Closes it.
- **Where study time went:** maybe 20 minutes. On long-day weeks, study happens on the days off, and the days off are also life admin.

### Day 4: Nursing student, night shift into a "day off" (spring)
- **19:15:** Handover. 20:00–07:30: obs, comfort, admissions, learning in the quiet hours (if there are any).
- **03:00:** Opens flashcards on the phone in a break. Reads the same card four times.
- **08:30:** Home, sleeps until 14:00.
- **15:00–17:00:** "Revision day", in a fog. Rereads lecture slides because active recall feels impossible.
- **Where study time went:** officially a study day; in practice about an hour of low-quality rereading.

### Day 5: Dental student, clinic day (winter)
- **08:00:** Checks the clinic list. The 9am patient cancelled by text at 07:52.
- **09:00–12:30:** Morning clinic: tries to pick up another student's patient, assists a friend, logs nothing for themselves.
- **12:30–13:30:** Lunch, rebooks patients, chases the lab about a denture.
- **13:30–17:00:** Afternoon clinic: a real procedure, graded on the system. A tutor gives a lower grade than hoped.
- **18:00–20:00:** Sim lab practice for the next competency test, or revising for a written exam.
- **21:00:** DFT SJT practice questions (November) or flashcards.
- **Where study time went:** about 2 hours. Much of the stress is clinical numbers, not knowledge.

### Day 6: Radiography student, placement in general imaging and A&E (first or second year)
- **08:30–09:00:** Arrives, gets a dosimeter badge, checks the list.
- **09:00–12:30:** General X-ray rooms: positioning, exposure, checks, under supervision. Asks for a competency on a chest X-ray. Goes well.
- **12:30–13:00:** Lunch. Physics notes.
- **13:00–17:00:** A&E imaging or mobiles on wards. Busy. The examination they need for a competency doesn't come in.
- **18:00–20:00:** Anatomy flashcards and an image appraisal practice set at home.
- **Where study time went:** about 2 hours, mostly in the evening, plus learning on the job that never feels like "revision".

### Day 7: Final-year medic, the weekend before the AKT (late January)
- **08:30:** Wakes, guilt about not waking at 07:00.
- **09:00–12:30:** A timed mock paper in the bank. Score lower than the bank's "pass" line. Panic.
- **12:30–13:30:** Lunch while reading the group chat: someone posted the bank's average.
- **13:30–17:30:** Reviews wrong answers, makes cards of them, then deletes half of them.
- **17:30–19:00:** Walk, food, a call home.
- **19:00–22:00:** Flashcard reviews, then a "high-yield" list from a friend.
- **23:30:** Can't sleep. Googles last year's pass mark.
- **Where study time went:** about 9 hours. The most effective part was the review of wrong answers; the least effective was rereading "high-yield" lists at 22:00.

### Day 8: Christmas Day (any course)
- **10:00:** "I'll do an hour this morning."
- **10:05:** Presents.
- **14:00–17:00:** Food. A relative asks: "So when are you a real doctor/nurse?"
- **20:00:** Opens the flashcard app: 900 due. Closes it.
- **Where study time went:** 0 hours. That's fine, and they need permission to feel that it's fine.

## 5. How they actually study

Describe tools **by type**; don't name them on cards unless the brief does.

### Spaced-repetition flashcard apps
- **What:** one dominant free app on desktop (paid on one phone platform), plus web-based alternatives. Medics in particular use **large shared premade decks** made by students. These get versioned and swapped, and are sometimes tagged to the content map.
- **Love:** the feeling of doing something evidence-based. Phone revision on buses and in breaks. Premade decks save hours.
- **Hate:** **the review pile**. Miss three days and there are 1,200 reviews. The settings rabbit hole. Cards that don't match their own school's teaching. Premade decks too big to ever finish. Decks that may not match **the new content map from September 2026**.
- **Time waste:** making cards instead of reviewing them, formatting cards, reinstalling add-ons, unsuspending entire decks and drowning.

### Question banks
- **What:** several paid subscription banks for UK medics, some for dentistry, fewer for nursing and radiography. Medics treat them as near-compulsory. Many banks show percentages compared with peers.
- **Love:** exam-like questions, explanations, the sense of progress, "% complete".
- **Hate:** cost, multiple subscriptions, explanations that are long or contradict each other, **peer-comparison statistics** that make them feel stupid, banks lagging behind content changes.
- **Time waste:** chasing completion percentage, random mode with no review of wrong answers, redoing questions they remember, switching banks.

### OSCE resources
- **What:** station checklists, websites, books, videos, mock OSCEs run by societies, practising on flatmates.
- **Love:** structure. The checklist feels safe.
- **Hate:** practising alone is impossible to judge. Finding partners. The awkwardness.
- **Time waste:** reading checklists silently instead of saying them out loud.

### Lecture recordings and the university learning platform
- **Love:** 2x speed, pausing.
- **Hate:** recordings missing or with no audio, 300 slides, lectures that duplicate each other.
- **Time waste:** "catching up" by rewatching every lecture at 1x in a passive state.

### Notes apps and tablets
- **What:** tablets with styluses, handwriting notes apps, all-in-one workspace apps, digital planners.
- **Love:** aesthetics, everything in one place.
- **Hate:** sync issues, fragmented notes.
- **Time waste:** building the perfect template and colour code, rewriting lecture slides into prettier lecture slides. This is classic "productive procrastination".

### Group chats
- **What:** messaging group chats for the year group, placement group, house, society.
- **Love:** shared notes, "is anyone else's assessor ghosting them", memes, instant answers.
- **Hate:** 400 unread messages, panic spreading at 23:00, people posting scores, hearing rumours about what's "on" the exam.
- **Time waste:** an hour lost reading other people's anxiety.

### Official references and guidelines
- **What:** national clinical guidelines, the national drug formulary, local hospital protocols.
- **Love:** they're the truth.
- **Hate:** long, change often.

### AI chat tools
- **What:** used to summarise lectures, generate questions, explain topics, help draft or "tidy" essays.
- **Love:** speed.
- **Hate and worry:** hallucinated facts in a field where facts matter. Universities' rules on AI use in assignments: academic misconduct fears.
- **Time waste:** conversation instead of recall.

### Nursing-specific
Essay planning and referencing tools, library databases, drug-calculation practice platforms, reflective-model templates, the e-PAD app or portal.

### Radiography-specific
Anatomy apps, online image banks and case collections, physics notes, positioning guides.

### Planners and timetables
- **What:** printable revision timetables, spreadsheets, wall planners, calendar apps.
- **Love:** the planning session (it feels like control).
- **Hate:** the plan breaks the first time a rota changes, a shift runs late, or they get ill. They then spend an evening rebuilding the plan instead of studying. **This is the gap Offload fills best** (section 11).

### Habits that waste their time (summary)
1. Rebuilding the plan after every disruption.
2. Making cards instead of reviewing.
3. Passive rereading and rewatching.
4. Question banks on random without reviewing errors.
5. Comparing percentages in group chats.
6. Over-formatting notes.
7. Doing everything they're good at and avoiding what they're not.
8. Studying in fragments too short to start anything (a 10-minute break on a long day) and then writing off the day.
9. All-or-nothing: "I didn't do my 3 hours, so the day's ruined."

## 7. Beliefs and myths

| # | Belief | Verdict | Brief evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Rereading notes is revising." | **False-ish** | Rereading creates familiarity, not recall. Practice testing and spaced practice beat it in large reviews of learning techniques. |
| 2 | "Testing yourself is just for checking; real learning is reading." | **False** | The testing effect: retrieval itself strengthens memory. |
| 3 | "Spacing is better than cramming." | **True** | One of the most replicated findings in learning research. |
| 4 | "Cramming works." | **Half true** | It can get you through tomorrow; it forgets fast. Clinical exams are cumulative, so cramming costs later. |
| 5 | "If it feels hard, it's not working." | **False** | Effortful recall ("desirable difficulty") tends to stick better than easy rereading. |
| 6 | "Highlighting helps." | **Mostly false** | Rated low-utility in the major reviews unless paired with recall. |
| 7 | "I'm a visual learner." | **False** | No good evidence that matching teaching to "learning styles" improves results. |
| 8 | "All-nighters before an exam help." | **False** | Sleep deprivation impairs recall and reasoning; sleep helps consolidation. |
| 9 | "Question banks alone are enough for the AKT." | **Half true** | They're good retrieval practice, but only if wrong answers are reviewed and topics are spaced. Percent-complete isn't learning. |
| 10 | "My bank score predicts whether I'll pass." | **Half true** | Rough correlation at best; banks differ in difficulty and cohorts differ. Unknown for any one person. |
| 11 | "The AKT doesn't matter because it's pass/fail." | **Half true** | It doesn't rank you for the foundation programme **(checked)**, but you must pass it to register. |
| 12 | "My grades decide my F1 job." | **Mostly false (now)** | Allocation uses preference-informed allocation with an unseen random rank **(checked)**. The specialised academic route still involves selection. |
| 13 | "You can't revise for an SJT." | **Half true** | Knowledge revision doesn't help much, but familiarity with format and professional guidance does. |
| 14 | "Make your own flashcards or they won't work." | **Half true** | Making cards helps some learning, but premade cards with real recall still work. Making cards is often procrastination. |
| 15 | "You have to clear every review every day." | **Half true** | Spacing works best with regularity, but missing days isn't a disaster; schedulers can recover. All-or-nothing makes people quit. |
| 16 | "Anything published for the old exam is still fine." | **Half true** | Most core knowledge holds, but **the content map changed from September 2026** **(checked)**. Check what's on it. |
| 17 | "OSCEs are about knowing things." | **Half true** | Knowledge matters, but structure, communication, safety and time are marked too. Saying it out loud is the skill. |
| 18 | "Practising OSCEs in my head is enough." | **False** | Performance under observation is a separate skill from knowing the steps. |
| 19 | "Nursing has no exams, so there's nothing to revise." | **False** | Exams, drug calculations (often 100% pass), and practice assessment all need preparation. |
| 20 | "Hours can just be made up at the end." | **Half true** | Usually possible, but it can delay finishing, registration and job start. |
| 21 | "Rest days are wasted days." | **False** | Recovery supports learning; burnout costs more time than rest does. |
| 22 | "I'll catch up over Christmas." | **Half true** | Some will. Most plans assume no family, no work, no tiredness. Realistic plans beat heroic ones. |
| 23 | "Handwriting notes is always better than typing." | **Half true** | The famous result has had mixed replication. What you do with notes (recall) matters more. |
| 24 | "Music helps me focus." | **Half true** | Varies by person. Lyrical music tends to interfere with reading-heavy tasks. |
| 25 | "Multitasking with the group chat open is fine." | **False** | Task-switching costs attention and time. |
| 26 | "Pomodoro is the answer." | **Half true** | Short blocks help start work, but no magic interval; placement days rarely allow neat blocks. |
| 27 | "Studying 8+ hours a day is what the top people do." | **Half true / misleading** | Social media shows edited days. Quality and spacing matter more than hours. |
| 28 | "Ten minutes isn't worth starting." | **False** | Short retrieval sessions on spaced material are effective; that's what spacing is. |
| 29 | "Dentistry is easier than medicine." | **False** | Different, not easier. Clinical requirements plus hand skills plus academics. |
| 30 | "Radiographers just press a button." | **False** | Positioning, physics, radiation protection, image appraisal, patient care. |
| 31 | "Caffeine makes up for sleep." | **Half true** | Helps alertness short-term; doesn't fix consolidation or reasoning. |
| 32 | "AI can make my revision for me." | **Half true** | Fine for generating practice; facts must be checked. University rules apply to assessed work. |

## 9. Feelings

- **What they fear:**
  - Failing the gate exam: AKT, finals, the BDS clinical requirements, the drug calc, the final PAD sign-off.
  - Being unsafe. That's the deepest fear, and it's why "no clinical advice" matters.
  - A fitness-to-practise referral over something small.
  - Being sent far from family by allocation. Not getting a job (unallocated medics, nurses without a newly qualified post).
  - Money running out mid-placement.
  - Being "found out" as not knowing enough on a ward round.
  - Being the one who failed when everyone else passed.
- **What they're proud of:**
  - The first time they did something real: a first cannula, first filling, first chest X-ray competency, first patient who remembered their name.
  - Surviving a run of long days or nights. Getting the PAD signed. A clean OSCE station.
  - Their professional identity, especially nurses and radiographers when someone gets it right.
  - Being trusted by a team.
- **What makes them feel behind:**
  - The review count. Peer percentages. Someone in the group chat "finishing".
  - Rotations that end before they've revised them. Missing teaching to help on the ward.
  - Social media revision setups. Their own plan sitting unrevised since Sunday.
- **What makes them feel on top of it:**
  - Knowing what to do *today* without deciding. A plan that survived a disrupted week.
  - Getting a question right that they'd got wrong last week. A short, clean session on a bus.
  - A day off that was actually off.
  - Reaching inbox zero on reviews, even once.
- **Emotional moments of the year:**
  - September: the first placement nerves.
  - 7 October: the foundation application deadline relief.
  - November: the DFT SJT, the dark, the backlog.
  - December: the Christmas guilt.
  - January to February: AKT dread, AKT done.
  - Early spring: the allocation email.
  - April to May: OSCE fear.
  - June: the results email ("passed" or "please contact the office").
  - July: graduation.
  - August: resits, or the first day as a qualified professional.

## 10. What they watch

### Formats that work (shapes, not creators)
- **POV text-on-screen, no voice, trending sound:** "POV: you open the app after three long days." The words carry it; the comments fill with "literally me". (The file notes up to 30x usual views for this shape on UK admissions accounts.)
- **"Rate/rank" formats:** revision methods tier lists, "things that actually helped in third year". Lots of comments and arguments.
- **"Things nobody tells you before…" lists:** first placement, first long day, final year. Heavily saved.
- **Before/after or "expectation vs reality":** the timetable vs the rota, the Christmas plan vs Christmas.

- **"Study with me" / timelapse / desk setup:** popular but saturated; works only with a twist.
- **Group-chat screenshot recreations:** the 23:14 message, the "has anyone finished…" panic. These get shared to real group chats.

- **Save:** plans, checklists, "do this tonight" steps, timetables, anything they'll use in January.

## 12. Care

**From safety.md (clinical):**
- **Both stamps before the pack opens.** It relates to revision. Not clinical advice.
- No diagnosis, no cure, no patient, no case. Never say what a finding, drug, dose, image or result means.
- The quiz shape uses only the student's own cards or non-clinical study content.
- The banned words filter applies (cure, guaranteed, diagnos-, suicide, kill myself, and the others). The writer never puts them back.
- No "Ad" unless it's an ambassador post. Never invent an ambassador.
- Name no creator, account, brand, school, university or person.

**From the research:**
1. **Confidentiality and professional codes.** These students are bound by their regulators' professionalism and social-media guidance. Content must never show or imply a real patient, a ward, a clinic room, a hospital name or logo, an ID badge, notes, screens, images (X-rays especially) or anything filmed on placement. A student copying a format could put their fitness to practise at risk. Our films must not model filming on placement.
2. **Patient dignity.** No jokes at patients' expense. No "difficult patient" humour. Dental "my patient DNA'd" is about the student's requirement, never the patient. Keep it about the schedule.
3. **Mental health.** These groups have high stress and burnout, and the healthcare workforce carries known risk around mental health. Keep to managing time. No "you have", no treatment claims. If a line could be distressing, the caption points to help and doesn't continue the story. Comments are not turned into cases; store themes only. "Crying in the car park" is a real in-joke. If used, keep it light and never as a punchline about despair.
4. **Money and hardship.** Many nursing students are parents and carers working extra shifts. Don't mock "spending" or "working instead of revising". Never imply they should give up paid work.
5. **Allocation, jobs and the prioritisation law.** Unallocated graduates and the 2026 prioritisation law are live, political and painful, especially for international students. Don't take sides. Don't joke about being unallocated. No dates beyond what's confirmed.
6. **Industrial action.** Doctors' strikes have disrupted teaching at times. Don't take a side.
7. **Professional identity.** Never call radiographers radiologists. Never rank the professions. Never call nurses angels or "just". Never say dentists are failed medics.
8. **Faith and fasting.** Ramadan 2027 is expected to fall around AKT season (from about 8 February). Do show plans that flex; don't make fasting a joke.
9. **Harassment and discrimination on placement** are real and reported. Not a content subject for an ad account.
10. **Under-18s.** A few first-years start at 17, especially in Scotland. Keep everything age-appropriate. No romantic or sexual bait.
11. **Numbers.** No figure on a card or film unless the brief carries it: no pass marks, no percentages, no review counts as "facts", no fake grades. Don't state any national exam date for school-set exams. The only confirmed national dates here are recruitment ones (foundation and DFT), and even those only if the brief carries them.
12. **The content-map hook.** "Is your question bank on the new map?" is true and useful. Never say a named resource is out of date.
13. **AI claims.** Never call Offload an AI tutor. The schedule is rule-based on their own materials.

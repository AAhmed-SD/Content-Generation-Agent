# offload.analytical: dossier

Written 7 November 2026 from research. For the team and the writer. Numbers here are background: never put one on a card or in a film unless the week's brief carries it. Name no creator, account, brand, school, university or person.

> **How sure we are.** These facts were checked against 2026 sources: the 2027 A-level and Scottish exam windows and results days, the UCAS deadlines for 2027 entry, the TMUA and STEP dates and formats, the end of the old Oxford maths test (MAT), the 2026 national survey figures on student experience and generative AI, and 2026 A-level maths and further maths entries. Those are marked *(check)* where it matters. Universities set their own dates. **Never give a university date on a card.**

## 1. Who they are

The account covers five groups. Each is in a different place, at a different age, under different pressure. What they share is a week built around **written problems that you either can or can't do from a blank page**. Rereading, colouring in and "getting the gist" don't help much here. Everything comes down to whether you can do the question.

### A. A-level Maths students (Year 12 and Year 13, England, Wales and Northern Ireland)

- **Age:** 16–18. Year 12 is lower sixth (16–17). Year 13 is upper sixth (17–18). Most are under 18 for most of the year, so **under-18 rules apply to everything for this group** (see section 12).
- **Where:** school sixth forms, sixth-form colleges, FE colleges. They're taught in classes, usually 4–5 hours of maths a week, with homework set by a teacher.
- **How many (background):** A-level Maths is the biggest A-level subject. In 2026 it was about **12.7% of all UK A-level entries**, and entries rose for the fourth year in a row. That's on the order of 100,000+ entrants *(exact figure: check JCQ)*.
- **What makes them different:** they sit **three linear exam papers at the end of Year 13** and there is **no coursework**. Everything rides on a few hours in June. Year 12 has internal end-of-year exams that feed into **predicted grades**, and predicted grades feed the university application. They're also often taking physics, chemistry, economics or computer science, so maths competes with other subjects for time. Many are applying to university in Year 13 while revising.

### B. A-level Further Maths students

- **Age:** same as above, 16–18.
- **How many (background):** a much smaller group. Further Maths was about **2.3% of A-level entries in 2026**, roughly 21,000 entries pooled across three boards. Grades are high: nearly three in ten got an A* in 2026. On one board in 2026 the A* boundary sat around three-quarters of total marks.
- **What makes them different:** they take two maths A-levels, so maths can be **8–10 hours a week of lessons**. Some schools can't offer it, so some students get a few hours of outside or online tuition. They're the core applicants for competitive maths, CS and economics degrees. Many are also preparing for **admissions tests (TMUA in October or January, STEP in June)** on top of A-levels. Their identity is often "the maths one". That makes falling behind or getting stuck feel personal.
- **Also in this pool:** students sitting the **Advanced Extension Award** in maths (late June), and Scottish students on **Higher / Advanced Higher Maths** (Scottish exams run **26 April – 3 June 2027**, results **Tuesday 10 August 2027**). A smaller number take the IB. Scottish pupils often start university at 17, into a four-year honours degree.

### C. Maths undergraduates (Years 1–3 BSc, Year 4 for integrated master's MMath/MSci; four years by default in Scotland)

- **Age:** 18–22, up to 23 on four-year courses. There are some mature students and a sizeable group of international students.
- **How many (background):** on the order of 10,000 new entrants a year across maths and statistics *(order of magnitude: check)*.
- **What makes them different:** the **problem sheet** (also called the "example sheet" or "problem set") runs the week. A new sheet comes out each week or fortnight per module, often with 4–5 modules at once. The jump from A-level is brutal. A-level rewards method. University maths is proofs: definitions, epsilon-delta, "show that", "prove that". Many first years who coasted to A*s hit a wall in **Analysis** and **Linear Algebra** in their first term. At collegiate universities, sheets feed into small-group **tutorials or supervisions**, where you hand work in beforehand and get questioned on it. Elsewhere they feed **problem classes** and a marked homework that counts for a small slice of the module. Exams are usually **closed-book, 2–3 hours**, and count for most of each module mark. Typesetting in **LaTeX** turns up in year 2 or 3 for projects.

### D. Economics undergraduates (Years 1–3; four years in Scotland or with a placement)

- **Age:** 18–22. Economics has a large international cohort.
- **How many (background):** on the order of 10,000+ new entrants a year *(check)*.
- **What makes them different:** they live between two kinds of work. One is **maths and statistics**: micro, macro, econometrics, mathematical economics, problem sets with Lagrangians and regressions. The other is **essays and policy writing**. Many arrive without Further Maths, and some without A-level Maths at less mathematical programmes, and get ambushed by the first-year quantitative methods module. Econometrics means coding in a statistics language and interpreting output. Many are aiming at finance, consulting or the civil service, so **spring weeks, insight days, internships and grad-scheme applications** run alongside the degree from first year. The application cycle often matters to them as much as the grades.

### E. Computer science undergraduates (Years 1–3, Year 4 for MEng/MSci; plus a placement year for many)

- **Age:** 18–23. This is the biggest group of the five.
- **How many (background):** computing is one of the largest subject areas in UK higher education, on the order of 30,000 entrants a year *(check)*. Applications have been under pressure as students worry about AI and the junior tech job market *(reported widely; not re-checked this cycle)*.
- **What makes them different:** their open "set" is as likely to be **code** as maths. That means programming coursework, automated test suites, lab exercises and group software projects, alongside maths-heavy modules (discrete maths, logic, algorithms, probability, theory of computation) that end in written exams. Many sandwich courses include a **placement year (year in industry)** between second and final year. The hunt for it starts in the autumn of second year: online assessments, coding tests, technical interviews, lots of rejection. Students also do the **final-year project**, which means a build, a report and often a demo or viva.

### Across all five

- Most undergraduates work. About **two-thirds of full-time UK undergraduates have term-time paid jobs**, roughly 14 hours a week among those who work. The average study week is near 29 hours (national academic experience survey, 2026). In the same 2026 survey, students' rating of value for money reached a decade high. Wellbeing is still **lower than in the general population**.
- **Generative AI is everywhere:** in a 2026 national survey, about **95% of full-time undergraduates** said they use AI in at least one way. Roughly a third say they now mostly use AI rather than traditional methods for some tasks. For a problem-sheet audience that is the live tension: the answer is a prompt away, and the exam is not.
- The thread for all of them: **the set is still open.** There is always a sheet, a coding exercise, a past paper or a chapter of exercises that isn't done.

## 2. Their year, month by month

The year runs September 2026 to August 2027. University dates vary by institution. What follows is the typical shape, not a promise.

### September 2026
- **What's happening:**
  - **A-level:** school term starts in the first week. Year 12 meets A-level maths and the "it's not GCSE" shock: surds, proof, algebraic fractions at speed. Year 13 is told predicted grades are being set over the next few weeks. UCAS applications for 2027 entry opened to submit in September. Those applying to Oxford or Cambridge, or to any course needing the TMUA, had to **book the TMUA by 28 September** (October sitting).
  - **University:** freshers' week is mid-to-late September (some start in early October). The first sheets land in week 1 or 2. Some departments give first years a short maths check test. **Second-year CS students start placement applications**, and many big employers open in September and October.
- **Worries:** "Am I behind already?" (Year 12). Predicted grades (Year 13). The personal statement. Getting a placement before everyone else does. Money: the first student loan instalment arrives at the start of term, and rent has already gone out.
- **Instead of studying:** freshers' events, society fairs, moving in, finding shifts, setting up a new room, applying for jobs.

### October 2026
- **What's happening:**
  - **15 October, 18:00:** UCAS early deadline (Oxford, Cambridge, most medicine, dentistry and vet courses).
  - **TMUA sitting 12–16 October** at computer test centres: two 75-minute papers, multiple choice.
  - Many universities have sheet 2 or 3 due. The first marked homework comes back with red pen on the "obvious" step. Spring-week and internship deadlines for economics and maths students start rolling.
  - Clocks go back on Sunday **25 October**: dark at 5pm from here on.
  - English school half-term falls in late October *(dates vary by local authority)*.
- **Worries:** whether the TMUA went badly. Whether you've submitted UCAS with the right predicted grades. Whether everyone else in the problem class understands it.
- **Instead of studying:** Halloween, nights out, the first cold of the term, admin, placement online assessments, part-time shifts.

### November 2026 (now)
- **What's happening:**
  - **TMUA October results: 16 November.** Oxford makes shortlisting decisions in November and interviews in December. The January TMUA booking window is open (closes **21 December, 18:00**).
  - At university, many have a reading or consolidation week around week 6. Mid-term coursework deadlines bunch up. **Sheets pile up**: week 6–8 sheets are the hardest in first term because the material has built on itself.
  - In schools, some Year 13s have mocks in late November or early December; many sit them in January instead.
  - Economics students juggle spring-week applications. CS second years are still hunting placements, and the first technical interviews are happening.
- **Worries:**
  - January exams are suddenly within sight.
  - "I've done the sheets with the solutions open and I don't think I can do them alone."
  - Placement rejections arriving by email.
  - Year 13s waiting on offers. Year 12s getting their first real test results.
- **Instead of studying:** bonfire night, work shifts, Christmas jobs starting, a cold that won't shift, scrolling in the dark afternoons.

### December 2026
- **What's happening:**
  - University term ends around mid-December (dates vary).
  - **Scottish universities often hold first-semester exams in December before Christmas** *(check by institution)*, so Scottish students revise in the same weeks everyone else is at Christmas parties.
  - Oxford and Cambridge interviews in early-to-mid December. Last sheets, last coursework of term, CS group project milestones. School term ends just before Christmas.
  - Jan TMUA booking closes 21 December.
- **Worries:** "Revision over Christmas" is the plan, and everyone knows how that ends. Interview nerves. Unfinished coursework hanging over the break.
- **Instead of studying:** Christmas socials, going home, family, a retail or hospitality job at its busiest, sleeping.

### January 2027
- **What's happening:**
  - **TMUA January sitting 4–8 January.** The **UCAS main deadline is Wednesday 13 January, 18:00.**
  - **January exam season** at many English and Welsh universities, typically mid to late January, often straight after the break, sometimes in the first week back. Some universities don't have January exams and sit everything in summer.
  - Many Year 13s sit **mocks** in January. Oxford and Cambridge decisions arrive in January.
  - Semester 2 starts at the end of January at many places.
- **Worries:** this is the month the open set becomes an exam. "I understood it in the lecture" stops counting. Mock grades. Offers or rejections.
- **Instead of studying:** recovering from Christmas, moving back to halls, January money gap (no loan instalment until the next term).

### February 2027
- **What's happening:**
  - **TMUA January results: 8 February.** First-semester **exam results** come back at many universities, sometimes not until mid-February.
  - **JCQ entry deadline for summer exams: 21 February** (schools enter candidates).
  - **UCAS Extra opens 25 February** for applicants with no offers.
  - English half-term mid-February *(varies)*. New modules start, new sheets. CS group projects grind on. Internship offers for summer get decided.
- **Worries:** seeing January marks. Retakes. "First term's methods are already gone." Year 13: offers and conditions, picking a firm and an insurance choice.
- **Instead of studying:** a new term's social calendar, applications, shifts.

### March 2027
- **What's happening:**
  - **STEP registration opens 1 March.**
  - School deadlines for the A-level CS **NEA** fall around now, usually spring of Year 13 *(school-set; check)*.
  - University coursework deadlines bunch before Easter. **Easter Sunday is 28 March 2027**, so the Easter vacation runs roughly late March into April *(dates vary)*.
  - Placement offers are mostly settled. People without one start to panic or give up on it.
- **Worries:** coursework collisions. Easter "being for revision". STEP prep starting late.
- **Instead of studying:** the end of term, going home, a job, holidays for some.

### April 2027
- **What's happening:**
  - The Easter break is the main revision period for summer exams, at A-level and at university.
  - **Scottish National Qualifications exams start Monday 26 April.**
  - University summer term starts mid to late April. Often it's mostly revision lectures and the odd final sheet.
- **Worries:** how much is left. Whether past papers from before syllabus changes count. Year 12: end-of-year exams coming.
- **Instead of studying:** the first warm days, family, holiday jobs, "productive" planning that replaces actual practice (colour-coded timetables).

### May 2027
- **What's happening:**
  - **A-level exams start Monday 10 May.** **STEP entries close 4 May.**
  - Applicants who applied by the January deadline get **university decisions by 12 May**.
  - **University summer exams** run through May and into June. Bank holidays are 3 and 31 May.
  - Year 12 internal exams at some schools (others in June or July). The next cycle of UCAS applications opens.
- **Worries:** order of papers, gaps between them, the first maths paper. "What if the boundaries go up." For undergrads: the module they did first and haven't touched since October.
- **Instead of studying:** study leave on the sofa, heat, other subjects' exams.

### June 2027
- **What's happening:**
  - A-level maths and further maths papers fall across late May and June *(exact days by board; check)*.
  - **STEP Paper 2: Monday 14 June. STEP Paper 3: Monday 21 June.**
  - Most boards finish A-levels by **Friday 18 June**, one by 21 June. Some papers (including the Advanced Extension Award) run to **Tuesday 22 June**. **Contingency day: Wednesday 23 June.**
  - **Scottish exams finish 3 June.** University exams end, and results for progression come out in late June or July.
  - Summer internships start for some.
- **Worries:** STEP after a full A-level series. Whether you've done enough for a first or a 2:1. Whether you're progressing.
- **Instead of studying:** done with exams, festivals, prom, leavers' events, jobs.

### July 2027
- **What's happening:**
  - University results and progression boards. Graduation ceremonies.
  - **Clearing opens in early July** *(one source gives 2 July; check)*. **30 June** was the last date for normal applications.
  - Year 12s start drafting personal statements, plan admissions-test prep and do summer schools.
  - Placement students are midway through their year. Interns are working.
- **Worries:** failed modules, resits, results not yet out. Year 12: "I should start TMUA prep".
- **Instead of studying:** holidays, full-time summer work.

### August 2027
- **What's happening:**
  - **Scottish results: Tuesday 10 August.**
  - **A-level results day: Thursday 12 August**, with UCAS updating that morning. **STEP results also come out on 12 August.**
  - Clearing. Universities run **resits / reassessment** for failed modules in August *(dates vary)*.
- **Worries:** results morning. Missing a condition. Resit exams without teaching.
- **Instead of studying:** results, Clearing calls, shopping for halls, last shifts before term.

## 3. The courses and the assessments

These are background, for picking true subjects. **The account attaches no exam to a card.** The account file says so, and it holds.

### A-level Mathematics (linear, two years)
- **Format:** **three written papers of 2 hours each, 100 marks each (300 total)**, sat in May–June of Year 13. One board's papers are Pure Mathematics 1, Pure Mathematics 2, and Statistics & Mechanics. Others split pure content across all three with statistics and mechanics in sections *(paper titles vary by board; check spec)*.
- **Content:** pure (proof, algebra, functions, coordinate geometry, sequences and series, trigonometry, exponentials and logs, differentiation, integration, numerical methods, vectors) plus statistics (including a **large data set** students must know before the exam) and mechanics (kinematics, SUVAT, forces, Newton's laws, moments).
- **Rules:** a **formulae booklet** is provided and an approved calculator is allowed on all papers. There is **no coursework / NEA**.
- **Marking:** **M marks** (method), **A marks** (accuracy, dependent on method), **B marks** (independent). Some marks are follow-through (ft). Grade boundaries are set after marking.
- **What's hardest:**
  - "Show that" and proof questions, where the answer is given and every step has to be written.
  - Long multi-part questions where part (c) depends on (a).
  - Integration techniques under time pressure.
  - Statistics wording and hypothesis-test conclusions written "in context".
  - Mechanics diagrams.
  - Running out of time on the last question.
  - The large data set, which some students never look at until the week before.

### A-level Further Mathematics
- **Format:** depends on the board. Some sit **four 90-minute papers** (two compulsory core pure plus two chosen options such as further pure, further statistics, further mechanics or decision maths). Others sit **three 2-hour papers** *(check spec)*. All exams, no coursework.
- **Content:** complex numbers, matrices, proof by induction, further calculus, polar coordinates, hyperbolic functions, differential equations, plus the options.
- **What's hardest:**
  - Volume of content with fewer teaching hours.
  - Being taught Further alongside or ahead of the A-level Maths topics it depends on.
  - Very high grade boundaries, where one silly slip costs a grade.
  - Options taught by whoever is available.

### Admissions tests (for competitive maths, CS and economics courses)
- **TMUA** (Test of Mathematics for University Admission):
  - **Two papers, 75 minutes each (2h30 total)**, multiple choice, taken on computer at test centres.
  - Paper 1 is applying maths. Paper 2 is mathematical reasoning: logic, proof, spotting the error in an argument.
  - Scored on a **1.0–9.0 scale**. There is no pass mark.
  - Sittings: **12–16 October 2026** and **4–8 January 2027**. Results 16 November 2026 and 8 February 2027.
  - From the 2026 round, Oxford uses the TMUA for maths, CS and joint courses, and **the old maths test (MAT) no longer runs**. Students still find old MAT papers online and ask whether to use them.
  - **Hardest:** speed, plus Paper 2's logic, which A-level never teaches ("necessary vs sufficient").
- **STEP** (Sixth Term Examination Paper):
  - **STEP 2 and STEP 3, 3 hours each**, long-form written. Each paper has 12 questions, and candidates focus on about six.
  - Graded **S, 1, 2, 3, U**. Usually part of a conditional offer from one collegiate university, and used by a few others.
  - **14 June and 21 June 2027.** Results 12 August.
  - **Hardest:** questions you can't start; scoring a fraction of a question; doing it in the middle of A-level exams.
- **Advanced Extension Award:** a harder single maths paper in late June. Few take it.

### A-level Computer Science and Economics (feeders)
- **CS:**
  - Two written or on-screen exam papers. One board's on-screen paper uses **skeleton code released in advance**.
  - Plus the **NEA**, a programming project worth about **20%**, written up as analysis, design, technical solution, testing and evaluation. School deadlines usually fall in spring of Year 13.
  - **Hardest:** NEA scope creep and the write-up, which takes longer than the code.
- **Economics:**
  - Typically three 2-hour papers with data-response and long essays. Students talk in terms of knowledge, application, analysis, evaluation.
  - **Hardest:** evaluation in essays, diagrams drawn correctly at speed.

### Scotland
- **Higher Maths and Advanced Higher Maths** (also Applications of Maths, Statistics and Mechanics at Advanced Higher). Exams run **26 April – 3 June 2027**, results 10 August.
- Higher maths has a **non-calculator paper and a calculator paper**.

### Undergraduate maths
- **Problem sheets / example sheets:**
  - Weekly or fortnightly per module, usually 5–10 questions, the later ones harder.
  - Sometimes formally assessed (a small percentage, or "submit to pass"). Sometimes marked by a postgraduate tutor for feedback only. Discussed in **problem classes, tutorials or supervisions**.
  - **Model solutions** are released after the deadline, or sometimes never.
  - **Hardest:** starting the proof; not knowing whether your proof is a proof; wasting an evening on one question.
- **Exams:**
  - Mostly **closed-book written papers, 2–3 hours**, worth most or all of the module mark. Usually in January and/or May–June.
  - Questions mix **bookwork** (state a definition, prove a theorem from lectures) with unseen problems.
  - **Hardest:** recalling exact definitions and proofs; transferring a method to a new setup.
- **Class tests and online quizzes:** weekly or in-term tests in some first-year modules.
- **Projects:** second-year group projects and final-year individual projects or dissertations, often typeset in LaTeX, sometimes with a presentation.
- **Programming components:** Python, R or similar in numerical analysis and statistics modules.
- **Degree classification:** First (70+), 2:1 (60–69), 2:2 (50–59), Third (40–49). First year often only needs a pass (about 40%) to progress at many institutions and doesn't count toward the final class. **It still matters for internship and placement applications**, because employers ask for first-year marks. Later years are weighted more heavily.

### Undergraduate economics
- **Problem sets** in micro, macro and maths for economists, often with **weekly or fortnightly seminars or classes**, sometimes assessed.
- **Econometrics:** computer labs and coursework in a statistics package or language, where you run regressions and interpret coefficients, standard errors and significance.
- **Essays and policy briefs** in applied modules.
- **Exams:** written, 2–3 hours, mixing multiple choice (in first year), short problems and essays. Some modules use timed online exams.
- **Final-year dissertation or extended project** at many universities.
- **Hardest:**
  - The maths step-up for those without Further Maths.
  - Knowing when an essay needs a model and when it needs words.
  - Econometrics output.
  - Juggling internship applications with deadlines.

### Undergraduate computer science
- **Programming coursework and labs:**
  - Weekly lab exercises, often **auto-marked against test cases**, plus larger assignments with deadlines and plagiarism / AI-use checks.
  - **Hardest:** debugging at 1am; the hidden test cases you fail; starting late because "it's just coding".
- **Written exams** for theory-heavy modules (discrete maths, algorithms, data structures, theory of computation, networks, databases). Some are on paper, some on computer, some multiple choice.
- **Group software engineering project** (usually second year): a team, a client or brief, sprints, a report, a demo. **Hardest:** teammates who vanish; merge conflicts; one person doing it all.
- **Final-year individual project:** a build plus a substantial report, sometimes a viva or demo.
- **Placement year:** not usually graded as a module in the same way. Often pass/fail or a small credit with a report *(varies; check)*.

### Late penalties and extensions
Universities apply late-submission penalties. Students can apply for extensions or mitigating circumstances (the names vary by institution). This matters because the "11:59pm deadline" is a real, recurring moment.

## 4. A day in their life

### Day 1: Year 13, school day in November (Further Maths, applying for maths)
- **07:00** Alarm. Snooze. Scroll short videos in bed until 07:25.
- **07:45** Bus. Earbuds in. Glances at a TMUA results countdown post. Results are on the 16th.
- **08:40** Registration. Someone asks about predicted grades.
- **09:00–10:00** Further Maths: complex loci. Copies examples. Gets it while the teacher does it.
- **10:00–11:00** Physics.
- **11:20–12:20** Free period. Means to do the maths homework (an exercise from the textbook plus two past-paper questions). Ends up in the common room talking about offers. Does one question.
- **12:20** Lunch.
- **13:10–14:10** Maths: hypothesis testing. Gets confused by "in context".
- **14:10–15:10** Free. Library. Opens a past-paper aggregator site, starts a 2019 paper, gets stuck on question 4, opens the mark scheme "just to check the first line", reads the whole solution and moves on. Feels it went well.
- **15:30** Home. Snack. Sofa.
- **16:30–18:00** Part-time shift at a café (two evenings a week).
- **18:30** Dinner.
- **19:30–20:15** Group chat: someone has posted a screenshot of tomorrow's homework question. Twelve messages about it, no one has done it.
- **20:15–21:30** Actual work: further maths sheet, half done. Uses an AI chatbot to "explain" question 6. Copies the method out.
- **21:30–23:30** Bed, phone. Thinks about the January mocks and decides to "start revising properly next week".
- **Where the study went:** about 2 hours on paper. Most of it was with an answer visible.

### Day 2: First-year maths undergraduate, lecture day (week 7)
- **08:45** Wakes up late for a 9am, watches the recording later (doesn't).
- **10:00–11:00** Linear Algebra lecture. Writes everything down, understands about 60%.
- **11:00–12:00** Analysis lecture. The proof on the board looks clean. Doesn't know why the epsilon was chosen that way.
- **12:00–13:00** Lunch in halls. The flatmate group chat is planning Thursday.
- **13:00–14:00** Probability lecture.
- **14:00–15:00** Problem class. The postgraduate tutor goes through last week's sheet. Realises the "obvious" step they wrote lost them marks.
- **15:00–17:00** Library with two coursemates on this week's Analysis sheet. Spends 40 minutes on question 2 together, 20 minutes chatting. Someone says "just look at the textbook example". They adapt it.
- **17:00–18:30** Gym, food.
- **19:00–22:00** "Sheet time" in their room: Linear Algebra sheet, due Friday 5pm. Does Q1–3. Q4 is a proof they can't start. Checks a forum, finds a similar question, kind of gets it. Stares.
- **22:00–00:30** Gives up on Q4. Plays games. Reads half a lecture note. Decides to do Q4 tomorrow between lectures.
- **Where the study went:** 3 hours of lectures that felt like learning. About 4 hours of sheet work, maybe 90 minutes of it on their own from blank.

### Day 3: Second-year CS student, coursework deadline day (with a placement interview)
- **08:00** Wakes up to a Teams-style message from the group project team: "who's doing the tests?"
- **09:00–11:00** Algorithms lecture (dynamic programming). Half-listens while fixing a bug on the laptop.
- **11:00–12:00** Coffee. Online assessment for a placement: 2 coding questions, 70 minutes. Gets one fully, partial on the other.
- **12:00–13:00** Lab: auto-marked exercise. Passes 8/10 tests. Can't see the hidden two.
- **13:00–14:00** Lunch at the desk. Mock interview prep on a coding-interview practice site.
- **14:00–15:00** Video interview for a placement: technical questions. Freezes on a recursion question they could do yesterday.
- **15:00–23:00** Individual coursework due 23:59. Debugging. Stack traces. Asks an AI chatbot. Gets code that "works" but uses a library they weren't allowed. Rewrites. Group chat: "has anyone else got a segfault on part 3?"
- **23:52** Submits. **23:58** Realises the wrong zip was uploaded. Resubmits at 23:59:40.
- **00:30** Sleeps. The discrete maths sheet due Friday is still unopened.
- **Where the study went:** almost all of it went to deadlines and applications. Nothing went to the exam modules.

### Day 4: Second-year economics student, work shift day
- **09:00–10:00** Econometrics lecture: heteroskedasticity. Records it.
- **10:00–12:00** Library: spring-week application for a bank, with motivational questions and a numerical reasoning test. Takes longer than planned.
- **12:00–13:00** Micro seminar. Hasn't finished the problem set. Sits quietly hoping not to be asked about Q3 (the Lagrangian one).
- **13:00–14:00** Lunch, emails.
- **14:00–15:00** Econometrics lab: runs the regression from the handout. Doesn't fully know what the output means but copies it into the coursework template.
- **16:00–22:00** Shift at a supermarket. Rent is due.
- **22:30–23:30** Tired. Opens the macro reading. Reads the same page three times.
- **23:30** Bed.
- **Where the study went:** about 2 hours of real study. The shift ate the evening, and applications ate the morning.

### Day 5: Placement-year student (CS or maths), workday
- **07:30** Commute. Reads work Slack-type messages. No sheets this year.
- **09:00–17:30** Works as a junior developer or analyst.
- **18:30** Dinner. Thinks about final year: the project proposal is due in spring, and final-year module choices are coming.
- **20:00** Sees a former coursemate post about January exams. Feels relief and a creeping fear of having forgotten everything from second year.
- **Where the study went:** nowhere, which is the point. Final year will start cold.

### Day 6: Sunday before January exams (any undergrad)
- **10:30** Wakes up. "Revision day."

## 7. Beliefs and myths

1. **"If I understood it in the lecture, I can do it."** *False.* Following a worked argument isn't the same as producing it. Studies of "fluency" show familiarity makes material feel known without improving recall.
2. **"Rereading my notes is revising."** *Mostly false.* Major reviews of study techniques rate rereading and highlighting as low-usefulness compared with practice testing and spaced practice.
3. **"Doing the sheet with the solution open still counts."** *Half true.* Studying worked examples helps beginners. But it doesn't build the ability to start from blank, and that's what exams test.
4. **"Practice from blank (testing yourself) works better than review."** *True.* The testing effect is one of the most replicated findings in learning research.
5. **"Spacing beats cramming."** *True* for long-term retention. Cramming can work for a test the next morning and fades fast. Hard for maths because methods need to be rebuilt, not just recognised.
6. **"Mixing topics (interleaving) feels worse, so it's worse."** *False.* Mixed practice feels harder and often gives better results on tests where you have to choose the method, and maths studies show this. Blocked practice ("ten integration-by-parts in a row") feels better and transfers less.
7. **"You can't cram maths."** *Half true.* You can cram definitions and bookwork. You can't cram problem-solving fluency.
8. **"Past papers are all you need."** *Half true.* They're the best practice for format and timing. If you do them from the mark scheme, or before you know the content, they're mostly reading.
9. **"Flashcards don't work for maths."** *Half true.* They work well for definitions, theorem statements, conditions and "which method when" cues. They don't replace doing full questions.
10. **"Some people are just maths people."** *Mostly false.* Prior knowledge and practice explain a lot. Mindset interventions have mixed evidence, but the belief that ability is fixed isn't supported.
11. **"First year doesn't count."** *Half true.* At many universities first year only needs a pass to progress. But employers and placement applications often ask for first-year marks, and later modules build on it.
12. **"You need a 2:1 for any good job."** *Half true.* Many graduate employers still screen at 2:1. Some have dropped grade requirements. Competitive finance and tech roles may look harder.
13. **"An A* in Maths needs 90%."** *False / it varies.* Boundaries move each year and vary by board. In 2026 one board's Further Maths A* sat around three-quarters of total marks. **Never state a boundary on a card.**
14. **"A-level maths has coursework."** *False.* It's all exams. A-level CS has an NEA (about 20%). Maths doesn't.
15. **"You need Further Maths for a top maths degree."** *Mostly true.* Most competitive maths courses require or strongly prefer it. Some accept strong students without it.
16. **"The TMUA is just A-level maths."** *Half true.* Paper 1 draws on A-level content. Paper 2 tests logic and proof reasoning that A-level barely covers.
17. **"You need a tutor for STEP."** *False.* Free official self-study support exists. Many students prepare with past papers alone. That said, access to support varies by school.
18. **"The old maths admissions test (MAT) is still the Oxford test."** *False.* From the 2026 round it was replaced by the TMUA. Old papers still get recommended as practice, but they're a different test.
19. **"CS is mostly coding."** *False.* Degrees have a lot of discrete maths, logic, algorithms and theory, mostly examined on paper.
20. **"Economics is basically essays."** *False* at most UK universities. Micro, macro and econometrics are maths- and statistics-heavy.
21. **"Grinding coding-interview questions gets you an internship."** *Half true.* It helps with online assessments. Applications, timing and luck matter a lot.
22. **"AI can do my problem sheets."** *Half true.* It can produce plausible solutions. It often gets proofs subtly wrong. It can't sit your closed-book exam. Undisclosed use can breach university rules.
23. **"Watching lectures at 2× saves time."** *Half true.* It saves time watching. Without practice afterwards you don't remember much more either way.
24. **"All-nighters before an exam help."** *False.* Sleep supports memory consolidation, and sleep loss hurts problem-solving.
25. **"I learn better with music on."** *Half true.* It depends on the person and the task. Lyrics tend to hurt reading and problem tasks more.
26. **"I'm a visual learner."** *False* as a basis for study. The "learning styles" matching hypothesis isn't supported by evidence.
27. **"Making a beautiful timetable is a good start."** *Half true.* Planning helps. Rebuilding the plan instead of studying is a classic avoidance.
28. **"If I miss a day, the plan is ruined."** *False.* Rescheduling a missed session works better than abandoning the plan. That's all-or-nothing thinking, not how memory works.
29. **"Copying notes out neatly helps me remember."** *Mostly false.* Copying is low-effort. Rewriting from memory, then checking, helps.
30. **"Getting it wrong in practice means I'm bad at it."** *False.* Errors followed by correction are part of how retrieval practice works.

# offload.mentalhealth (the time account): dossier

Written 7 November 2026 from research. It is for the team and the writer. The numbers here are background only. Never put one on a card or in a film unless that week's brief includes it. Name no creator, account, brand, school, university or person.

A note on what this covers. The audience is UK students of any subject who struggle with time, planning and starting. Some of them have ADHD. Many are waiting for an assessment, many have no formal label, and many never will have one. The account is about time. It never names a condition as theirs, never asks, and never treats. Everything below is written to that rule.

---

## 1. Who they are

What links all of them is **not ability**. It is the gap between knowing what to do and starting it. The morning is usually fine. The afternoon goes. The work happens in a burst near the deadline, and then they are exhausted. Some under 18s read this account, so treat every group as mixed in age unless it is clearly over 18.

**How many (background only, never on a card).** About 3–5% of people have ADHD, according to the independent ADHD Taskforce report for NHS England (Part 1, April 2025; final report 6 November 2025). Many more struggle with starting and with time without meeting any threshold. UK higher education has roughly 2.9 million students. Each year group at school is roughly 600,000–700,000 young people in England. These are rounded from memory and need checking before use. The point is that the audience is very large, and most of it will never hold a formal label.

### 1a. GCSE students (Years 10–11 in England and Wales; Years 11–12 in Northern Ireland). Age 14–16. Under 18.
- They study 8–10 subjects at once. Every one of them ends in exams in May and June 2027 (England: Monday 10 May to Friday 18 June).
- Year 10 is "it doesn't count yet". Year 11 is mocks, more mocks, coursework in practical subjects, and adults saying "this is the most important year of your life".
- Their time is managed by others (timetable, bells, parents), so the problem shows up **at home**: homework, revision, the evening, the weekend.
- Phones are banned or restricted in a lot of schools, so the phone is all the more pulled to after school.
- Some resit English or maths in the November series. That is happening right now.
- **What makes them different:** they are under 18. They cannot pick their subjects or their timetable, and they rarely plan beyond "next test". Their planner was probably given to them by school. Many are waiting a long time for a child assessment, and parents often lead that process. Be strictly age-appropriate.

### 1b. Sixth form and college: A-levels, BTEC Nationals, T Levels, IB (Years 12–13). Age 16–18.
- **A-level:** usually 3 subjects (sometimes 4, plus an EPQ). They are linear, so almost everything is decided in the summer exams of Year 13. Year 12 often feels empty, and then Year 13 lands all at once.
- **BTEC Nationals and similar vocational courses:** units with coursework assignments and internal deadlines, plus external exams in January and May/June windows. Assignment deadlines come all year, so the starting problem shows up every few weeks, not once.
- **T Levels:** two-year technical courses with exams, an employer-set project, and an industry placement of at least about 315 hours (around 45 days). That means days at a workplace and days at college.
- **IB Diploma** (a small group): an Extended Essay, internal assessments in every subject, a TOK essay and exhibition. It is heavy on coursework.
- Year 13 also brings UCAS. The early deadline (medicine, dentistry, vet, Oxbridge) was **15 October 2026**. The main equal-consideration deadline is **6pm, 13 January 2027**. One source says 14 January; most, including UCAS-date guides, say 13 January. Treat it as 13 and check UCAS. The personal statement is now written as answers to three set questions rather than one free essay, starting with 2026 entry. Confirm the current format before referring to it.
- More free periods and more independent study time mean more afternoons that can disappear.
- **What makes them different:** for the first time the timetable has gaps, and nobody fills them for them. Many have part-time jobs. Many are mid-assessment for ADHD as they move from child to adult services.

### 1c. Scotland: National 5, Higher, Advanced Higher (S4–S6). Age 15–18.
- The awarding body is now **Qualifications Scotland**, renamed from SQA.
- The 2027 exam diet runs **Monday 26 April to Thursday 3 June**. Results day is **Tuesday 10 August 2027**.
- Prelims (Scotland's mocks) usually fall between December and February, depending on the school.
- Coursework, assignments and portfolios have earlier deadlines, often March to April.
- **What makes them different:** they start exams about two weeks before England. Their results come two days before A-level results. They say "prelims", not "mocks", and "S5", not "Year 12". Their school holidays are also different: summer starts late June, and they go back mid-August.

### 1d. Undergraduates (first year to final year, including placement years). Age 18–22 mostly, plus mature students.
- **First year:** often "doesn't count" or counts very little towards the degree. That makes it the year the starting problem grows unchecked. There is no timetable beyond about 8–15 contact hours, and the rest is "independent study".
- **Second year:** it starts to count. Often there are internship or placement applications on top.
- **Placement or year abroad** (on sandwich courses; also nursing, teaching, social work and allied health placements): a full-time working day, then any academic work happens in the evenings.
- **Final year:** the dissertation or final project, plus modules, plus graduate job applications. The degree classification hangs over everything: First, 2:1, 2:2, Third.
- In 2026, independent study averages about **11.1 hours a week**, down from 13.3 in 2024 (UK undergraduate experience survey 2026).
- Paid term-time work is now normal. In 2025, 68% of full-time undergraduates worked during term, and those who worked averaged about 13 hours a week. One 2025 study of students at four universities found a total weekly load of around 50 hours including travel, of which about 17 hours was paid work.
- Scottish degrees usually last four years. Integrated master's courses (MEng, MChem, MMath and so on) also last four years.
- **What makes them different:** they are adults who manage their own time completely, often for the first time, and often while living away from home. They can access university disability services. Some have support plans. Some are waiting for an assessment, and some are told that support does not depend on a formal label (several universities accept a GP letter). Disabled Students' Allowances exist, and guides put the 2026/27 maximum in England at about £27,783 a year. Never give eligibility advice. Point them to their university's disability service.

### 1e. Postgraduates (one-year master's, PGCE, PhD). Age 21–30+.
- **A one-year taught master's** has autumn and spring modules, then a dissertation of often 10,000–20,000 words, due around August to September. That gives a summer with no structure at all.
- **A PGCE** (teacher training) means school placements, assignments and lesson planning every evening.
- **A PhD** has years of unstructured time, which is the hardest starting problem there is.

### 1f. Mature, part-time and distance learners. Age 21–60+.
- Many study with an open-access distance university or on part-time evening courses. They fit study around jobs and children.
- Their "afternoon that disappeared" is often the evening after the kids go to bed.
- Many first wonder about ADHD in adulthood, often after a child of theirs is assessed.
- **What makes them different:** they feel guilty about time as well as stressed by it. Their deadlines are module assignments, and their exams or end-of-module assessments land in May and June.

### 1g. A cross-cutting group: the waiting
- Some areas report average adult ADHD waits of around two and a half years. Others report averages of more than seven years (area tracker by a UK ADHD charity, autumn 2026).
- The Taskforce called NHS ADHD waiting times "unacceptably long".
- Right to Choose providers in England can be faster, but some local NHS areas have capped or paused referrals.
- Many in this audience are, right now, between "I think I might…" and an appointment that could be years away. Content that helps **today**, without needing a label, matters most to them.

## 2. Their year, month by month

Dates are for England unless marked. Universities vary. Treat university dates as typical, not fixed. Where a date is unconfirmed, it says so.

### September
- **What's happening:** schools go back in the first week, from about 1–3 September 2026. University welcome week (freshers) is usually mid to late September, and teaching starts in the last week of September or the first of October. Final-year undergraduates get the dissertation brief and supervisor allocations. Master's students hand in dissertations (often early to mid September). Late resits and referrals finish. Clearing closes, with the final UCAS application date for 2027 entry on 23 September 2027. UCAS applications for 2028 entry open the spring before. Student loan maintenance payments arrive at the start of term.
- **Worried about:** the fresh-start pressure ("this year I'll be organised"), new timetables, living alone, money, Year 11 and Year 13 being told "this is the year".
- **Doing instead of studying:** buying a new planner, setting up a new notes system, freshers' events, finding a job, decorating their room, colour-coding a timetable they won't follow.

### October
- **What's happening:** first assignments are set. Year 13 personal statements go into UCAS (early deadline 15 October 2026). Year 11 have their first unit tests. Half term is usually the last week of October (in 2026, around 26–30 October in many English areas). The clocks went back on Sunday 25 October 2026. October is ADHD Awareness Month, which brings a lot of content about the condition (the account takes part only as Offload, and only about time). University reading weeks start in week 6 at some universities.
- **Worried about:** the first essay, the "I'm already behind" feeling around week 4, freshers' flu, homesickness, money.
- **Doing instead of studying:** Halloween, socials, half-term lie-ins, "I'll catch up in reading week".

### November (now)
- **What's happening:** the **GCSE November resit series** in English and maths sits in early to mid November. Exact 2026 dates were not checked, so confirm with JCQ. University reading weeks are common in early November. Mid-module deadlines stack up in weeks 7–9. Year 11 and Year 13 mock timetables are announced, with many mocks running late November to January depending on the school. Year 13 apply for apprenticeships and degree apprenticeships. Second-year students apply for summer internships and placement years (many close before Christmas). It is dark by about 4:15pm, and Black Friday is at the end of the month.
- **Worried about:** three deadlines in one week, the first returned marks ("I got a 58"), mocks, a reading week that disappeared, dark afternoons.
- **Doing instead of studying:** napping when it gets dark, Christmas plans, the work rota, Black Friday scrolling, "I'll do a full reset on Monday".

### December
- **What's happening:** the last teaching week of university is usually the second week of December. Many universities have **end-of-semester-one deadlines** in December, and some (more often in Scotland) hold exams before Christmas. Schools break up around 18 December 2026. Year 11 and Year 13 mocks are often just before or just after Christmas. Scottish prelims start in December at some schools. Mock results may come out the week before Christmas.
- **Worried about:** January exams, Christmas "revision" that never happens, mock grades going home to parents, money after the term.
- **Doing instead of studying:** going home, shifts (retail at Christmas means long hours), family, sleep, "I'll revise from the 27th".

### January
- **What's happening:** **university exam period**, usually the second to fourth week of January. Schools go back around 4 January 2027. Mocks are taken or returned. The **UCAS main deadline is 6pm, 13 January 2027**. The BTEC January external exam window runs. Some A-level and BTEC resits happen. Second semester starts late January or early February, with new modules and new timetables. January is also the season of "new year, new me" and the media's "Blue Monday" (avoid using that).
- **Worried about:** exams with no structure in the two weeks before, the Christmas revision that didn't happen, semester-one results, seeing friends' "I've done 6 hours" stories.
- **Doing instead of studying:** making a new revision timetable (again), rewriting notes neatly, a gym kick, watching lectures at 2× speed the night before.

### February
- **What's happening:** semester-one marks and grades come back. Semester two gets going. February half term is **15–19 February 2027** in many English areas. Year 13 receive university offers. **UCAS Extra opens 25 February 2027.** Scottish prelims finish. Placement and internship interviews are on.
- **Worried about:** marks that "count", offers and rejections, the long dark stretch, the dissertation that has "started" in name only.
- **Doing instead of studying:** half-term trips, comparing offers, "I'll start revision properly at Easter".

### March
- **What's happening:** NEA and coursework deadlines arrive at school (internal deadlines are usually earlier than the board's, which tends to be mid-May). Access arrangements in the JCQ system have a spring deadline, usually around 21 March for the summer series. Confirm the 2027 date, and send people to the school's SENCo, not to us. GCSE art and design 10-hour exams happen around March to April in many schools. University assignments due before Easter. Final-year dissertation drafts go to supervisors. The clocks go forward on 28 March 2027, and Easter Sunday is the same day.
- **Worried about:** coursework that has to be finished, "Easter is the last chance", the first proper revision timetable, dissertation word counts.
- **Doing instead of studying:** perfecting coursework they already finished while avoiding the coursework they haven't, making revision resources rather than using them.

### April
- **What's happening:** Easter holidays are around **26 March to 9 April 2027** in many English areas (they vary). Easter revision sessions run at schools. **Qualifications Scotland exams start Monday 26 April 2027.** GCSE language speaking assessments run in a window around April to May (schools choose dates within the board window). Universities often have Easter vacation, then the last teaching weeks. Final-year dissertations are often due in April or early May.
- **Worried about:** the "start revising properly at Easter" plan colliding with Easter itself, a dissertation deadline, the first "the exams are in X weeks" moment.
- **Doing instead of studying:** Easter family time, shifts, the first sunny days, "productive procrastination" (cleaning, new stationery).

### May
- **What's happening:** **GCSEs and A-levels start Monday 10 May 2027.** Study leave for Year 11 and Year 13 starts at many schools (from mid-May or late May). University exam period usually runs from mid-May to early June. BTEC May/June external window. Many NEA marks go to the boards by mid-May. Mental Health Awareness Week is in May (the 2027 date is not checked).
- **Worried about:** exams every day, a gap between exams with no structure, papers that go badly, comparing answers after exams, hay fever.
- **Doing instead of studying:** after-exam debriefs in group chats, recovering after each paper, sleeping between papers, sunny-day drinks for university students.

### June
- **What's happening:** May half term (around **31 May to 4 June 2027**) falls in the middle of GCSEs and A-levels. Qualifications Scotland exams end **3 June 2027**. GCSEs and A-levels end **18 June 2027** (a contingency day after this was not checked). University exams end. Progression boards meet. University results and degree classifications come out (mid-June to early July). Year 12 start A-level content for Year 13 and get NEA briefs; some sit internal end-of-year exams.
- **Worried about:** the last papers, degree classification, "what if I failed", leaving home (Year 13), resits (university).
- **Doing instead of studying:** prom, festivals, jobs, the end-of-exam feeling of "I've got nothing to do now" (which is also a time problem).

### July
- **What's happening:** graduation ceremonies. University resit and reassessment notices go out (most resits are in August). Schools break up around mid to late July in England. Scotland has been off since late June. Students on placements start them. Master's students are deep in dissertations. Year 12 write personal statements. Clearing opens (Clearing usually opens in early July).
- **Worried about:** resits, the master's dissertation, the summer job, results waiting.
- **Doing instead of studying:** holidays, festivals, full-time jobs, "I'll do my personal statement in August".

### August
- **What's happening:** **Qualifications Scotland results Tuesday 10 August 2027. A-level results Thursday 12 August 2027.** Clearing and adjustment follow. **GCSE results Thursday 19 August 2027.** University resits and referrals usually come in mid to late August. Master's dissertations are due around late August to mid September. Scottish schools return mid-August.
- **Worried about:** results day, Clearing, resits, the next year.
- **Doing instead of studying:** results-day nerves, Clearing calls, preparing to move out, a resit they can't make themselves start.

## 3. The courses and the assessments

Formats below are for the most common specifications in England. Boards differ in paper length and structure. When a post is about a specific paper, check the board's specification. Never quote a single board as if it were all of them.

### GCSE (grades 9–1)
- **Written papers:** almost all marks in almost all subjects. Usually 2–3 papers per subject, sat in May to June. Students find it hardest to keep revising 8–10 subjects in parallel, and they hate a revision plan that collapses if one day goes wrong.
- **Maths:** 3 papers of 1h30 each. Paper 1 is non-calculator. Higher tier (grades 4–9) or Foundation tier (1–5). The hardest part is the long multi-step questions at the end of a paper, which are worth the most and arrive when time is running out.
- **English Language:** 2 papers of about 1h45. Reading (unseen extracts) and writing (creative and opinion). The hardest part is the time-pressure in reading questions and planning creative writing in minutes.
- **English Literature:** 2 papers (about 1h45 and 2h15 on the most common specification). Shakespeare, a 19th-century novel, a modern text, a poetry anthology and unseen poetry. Closed book: quotations must be memorised. The hardest part is memorising quotations for several texts.
- **Combined Science (double award):** 6 papers of 1h15 each on the most common specification. Biology, chemistry and physics, two papers each. **Required practicals** are done in class and examined in the written papers, not marked as coursework. Triple science (separate sciences) is 6 longer papers. The hardest part is the sheer volume, and remembering practicals done in Year 9 or 10.
- **Languages (French, Spanish, German and so on):** listening, reading, writing and a **speaking exam** done in school with a teacher, usually around April to May. New specifications were first examined in 2026. The hardest part is speaking in front of a teacher, and vocabulary volume.
- **NEA (non-exam assessment):** a large share in some subjects. In design and technology it is about half the qualification. In art and design it is a coursework portfolio plus a 10-hour **externally set task**. Drama has devising and performance. PE, food and music each have practical components. The hardest part is long-term projects with self-managed deadlines — the exact place where starting fails.
- **Mocks:** set internally, usually once in Year 10 (summer) and once or twice in Year 11 (November to February). Predicted grades and set placements often follow. Students hate mock results being sent home.
- **Resits:** students without a grade 4 in English or maths continue studying them after 16 and resit. November and summer series.

### A-level
- **Linear:** usually 2–3 written papers per subject at the end of Year 13. Papers are often around 2 hours each, sometimes 2h30 or 3h. Graded A*–E. In Northern Ireland the AS still counts towards the A-level (40% on some specifications; check).
- **Sciences:** 3 papers each, plus the **practical endorsement** (pass or not classified, reported separately), which builds on about 12 required practicals over two years. Practical questions are also in the papers.
- **Maths:** 3 papers of 2 hours. Pure, statistics and mechanics. Further Maths has 4 papers on most specifications.
- **NEA:** English Literature (often about 20%, a comparative essay), History (often about 20%, a historical investigation of 3,000–4,000 words), Geography (20%, an independent fieldwork investigation), Computer Science (20%, a programming project), as well as art, D&T, drama, music and PE. Internal deadlines usually fall between the spring of Year 12 and the spring of Year 13. Marks go to the board around mid-May. The hardest part is a long piece of independent work with no timetable — the starting problem at its purest.
- **EPQ (Extended Project Qualification):** a project with a 5,000-word report or an artefact, a production log and a presentation. Worth half an A-level in UCAS points. The hardest part is the log, which requires keeping track of process over months.
- **Mocks:** often in November to January, sometimes again in March. They feed predicted grades and university references.

### BTEC Nationals, Cambridge Technicals and similar vocational courses
- **Coursework units** with assignment briefs, internal deadlines and internal verification. One resubmission is allowed under strict rules. They are graded Pass, Merit, Distinction (D*).
- **External assessments:** set tasks or exams in January and May/June windows. Some are supervised over several hours or several days.
- The hardest part is a steady stream of deadlines all year, so an "afternoon that disappeared" turns into a missed unit.

### T Levels
- **Core component:** exams plus an employer-set project.
- **Occupational specialism:** practical assessment.
- **Industry placement:** at least about 315 hours.
- The hardest part is switching between placement days and college days, and doing project work in between.

### Scotland: National 5, Higher, Advanced Higher
- **Exams:** in the diet (2027: 26 April to 3 June). Plus **coursework**: assignments, portfolios, performances and practicals, often written up under controlled conditions in school. Advanced Higher includes a large project or dissertation in many subjects.
- **Prelims:** school-set mock exams from December to February. They are used as evidence and for predicting.
- Graded A–D, plus No Award.

### University (undergraduate)
- **Structure:** modules (often 15, 20 or 30 credits each, 120 credits a year). Two semesters, or three terms at some universities. First year often counts 0% towards the classification, or very little. Second and final years are weighted (for example 33/67 or 40/60, depending on the university).
- **Pass marks and classes:** usually 40% to pass a module. First = 70+, 2:1 = 60–69, 2:2 = 50–59, Third = 40–49.
- **Essays and reports:** word limits (often 1,500–3,000 words) with penalties outside them. Submitted online through a plagiarism checker on the VLE, usually by midday or a set hour. **Late penalties** are often a percentage per day or a cap at the pass mark; rules vary.
- **Exams:** in-person written exams (two to three hours) in January and May to June. Some are online, open-book, 24-hour or 48-hour take-home papers. MCQ exams in some subjects. The hardest part of a 24-hour paper is that it has no structure and is easy to start late.
- **Problem sheets:** weekly in maths, physics, engineering, economics and computer science. Often submitted online by a set time, sometimes worth a small percentage. The hardest part is opening it when it is already due.
- **Lab reports, presentations, posters, portfolios, reflective logs, group projects:** spread across all courses.
- **The dissertation or final-year project:** often 8,000–12,000 words in humanities and social sciences, plus a research proposal and ethics approval earlier. Usually due around April to May. Students describe the hardest part as "it's due in April but the brief comes in September".
- **Extensions and mitigating circumstances** ("ECs", "mit circs"): different processes at each university. Some allow short self-certified extensions. Support plans can include extra time in exams or flexible deadlines. Always point to the university's own process.
- **Resits and reassessment** ("referral"): usually in August. The mark for a resit is often capped at the pass mark.
- **Placements:** sandwich-year placements are assessed by reports and reflections. Nursing, teaching, social work and allied health placements have competency documents and practice assessment.

### Postgraduate
- **Taught master's:** coursework and exams, pass mark usually 50%, Merit at 60%, Distinction at 70%. A dissertation over the summer.
- **PGCE:** school placements, assignments, and evidence against Teachers' Standards.
- **PhD:** annual reviews, an upgrade or confirmation review, a thesis and a viva.

### Exam arrangements (school and college)
- JCQ access arrangements cover extra time (25%, and more in rare cases), supervised rest breaks, a reader, a scribe, a word processor and a separate room.
- From the March 2026 JCQ update, schools are expected to consider supervised rest breaks before applying for 25% extra time for some candidates.
- Access arrangements are needs-led and decided by the school's SENCo with evidence. Never suggest who qualifies.

## 4. A day in their life

Composite days built from the patterns above. Times are typical, not measured.

### Day 1. A Year 11 school day in November (mocks in three weeks)
- **07:10** The alarm is snoozed three times. Phone in bed.
- **08:30–15:10** School. Six lessons. The phone stays in the bag. Mostly fine: the structure does the starting for them.
- **15:30** Home. "I'll start at 4."
- **15:35–17:00** Snack, phone, a video "for a minute", a group chat about a mock timetable. It is dark at 16:15.
- **17:00** Opens the science revision guide. Answers a message. Closes the guide.
- **17:30–18:30** Dinner. A parent asks "have you revised?" "Yeah, a bit."
- **18:30–20:30** Starts a revision timetable on paper with colours for every subject. It looks perfect. No revision done.
- **20:30–21:15** Homework that is due tomorrow — done fast, in the burst.
- **21:15–00:30** Phone in bed. "Tomorrow I'll follow the timetable."
- **Where the study time went:** into making the plan, and into the gap between getting home and starting.

### Day 2. A Year 13 day with free periods (NEA due before Christmas, UCAS by 13 January)
- **08:40–10:40** Two lessons.
- **10:40–12:40** Two free periods. Goes to the common room "to do the NEA". Opens the document and reads it from the top. Changes the font. A friend arrives. The free periods are gone.
- **12:40–15:30** Lunch and lessons.
- **16:00–20:00** A shift at a supermarket (four hours on a weekday).
- **20:30** Home. Tired. "Too late to start anything properly."
- **21:00–23:30** Answers one of the UCAS questions and deletes it. Watches a "how I got into…" video. Starts the NEA at 23:00 and writes 300 words in a burst.
- **Where the study time went:** the free periods — the most valuable two hours of the day, lost to restarting.

### Day 3. A second-year undergraduate lecture day (with a part-time job)
- **09:00** The lecture is recorded, so they stay in bed. "I'll watch it later." (It joins a backlog of six.)
- **11:00–13:00** A seminar, the one compulsory thing. Hasn't done the reading; skims it on the bus.
- **13:00–14:00** Lunch with friends.
- **14:00–18:00** **The afternoon disappears.** Library booked for "a deep work session". Opens the laptop and checks email, the VLE and the group chat. Starts the essay plan. Fetches a coffee. Rewrites the essay title. Looks at the assessment brief. Checks the deadline again. A friend messages. A video. At 17:45, they have the title page.
- **18:00–23:00** A shift at a bar or restaurant.
- **23:30** Home. Opens the essay. 200 words. Bed at 02:00.
- **Where the study time went:** a four-hour block broken into thirty starts.

### Day 4. A Saturday shift day (any age 16+)
- **08:00–16:00** An eight-hour shift.
- **16:30** Home. Exhausted. "I deserve a rest."
- **17:00–22:00** Rest that doesn't feel like rest: scrolling with the essay open in another tab, guilty.
- **22:00** Decides Sunday will be the big study day. Plans eight hours.
- **Where the study time went:** nowhere — but the evening was lost to guilt, not rest. Sunday's plan is too big to start.

### Day 5. The Sunday before January exams (a first-year undergraduate)
- **10:30** Wakes up. Exam on Tuesday at 09:30.
- **11:00–13:00** Makes a "final revision plan" with every topic in the module. Too many.
- **13:00–14:00** Lunch. Messages in the course group chat: "has anyone actually started?" (relief), then "I've done all the past papers" (panic).
- **14:00–17:00** Watches lecture recordings at 2× speed, from week 1. At 17:00 they are on week 3 of 11.
- **17:00–19:00** Panic, then dinner, then a long phone break.
- **19:00–02:00** The burst. Skims slides, makes rushed flashcards, does one past paper without the mark scheme. Bed at 02:00.
- **Monday** is a repeat. Tuesday's exam is after four hours' sleep.
- **Where the study time went:** into planning everything instead of starting one thing, and into the pace of the recordings.

### Day 6. A Christmas holiday day (Year 13 or university)
- **10:00** The plan says "revise 10–1". The house is busy.
- **10:00–13:00** Family, breakfast, a walk, presents, "I'll do the afternoon".
- **14:00–17:00** Opens the notes at the kitchen table. Interruptions every ten minutes. Goes to the bedroom; phone. Gets about 40 minutes done.
- **Evening** Family film, a night out, a Christmas party.
- **Where the study time went:** there was no fixed point in the day to start from. "Holiday" became "every day is the same", and every day was "tomorrow".

### Day 7. An exam-season day in May (GCSE, between papers)
- **09:00–10:45** Exam (a science paper).
- **11:00** Group chat about the paper. "Did anyone get question 6?" Panic about one answer.
- **12:00** Home on study leave. Next exam tomorrow (maths). "Rest first."
- **12:00–16:00** Lunch, a nap, scrolling. The afternoon goes.
- **16:00–19:00** Opens a past paper. Does half. Checks the mark scheme. Panics. Watches a walkthrough video instead of finishing.
- **19:00–23:00** Dinner, a little more revision, an early night that turns into midnight.
- **Where the study time went:** the after-exam recovery that turned into the whole afternoon, and the post-mortem on the paper already done.

### Day 8. A final-year dissertation day (no timetable at all)
- **No lectures.** The whole day is "dissertation day".
- **09:00** Plans to start at 09:00. Starts at 11:30.
- **11:30–13:00** Reads one article. Downloads seven more. Re-reads the supervisor's feedback.
- **13:00–17:00** Lunch, a laundry run, emails, a job application ("this is also important"). Writes 150 words at 16:30.
- **17:00–22:00** The evening goes to friends and dinner.
- **Where the study time went:** a whole day with no start point. The bigger the empty day, the harder the start.

**The thread in every day:** the problem is the gap between classes, the empty afternoon, the gap after an exam, the day with no shape. Structured time goes fine. Unstructured time disappears.

## 5. How they actually study

No brand names: describe tools by type.

### Flashcards and spaced-repetition apps
- **Uses:** a free spaced-repetition app with algorithmic scheduling (popular with university students), shared-deck flashcard websites (popular in schools), and paper flashcards.
- **Love:** feeling of progress, mobile, "I can do it on the bus", decks shared by others.
- **Hate:** making cards takes forever. Review counts pile up after a break ("I opened it and had 1,200 reviews due"). Ready-made decks don't match their course. They skip days, then can't face the backlog.
- **Time-wasters:** decorating cards, making cards instead of reviewing them, making cards for everything.

### Past papers, mark schemes and examiner reports (school and college)
- **Uses:** past papers from exam board websites, topic-sorted past questions from revision sites, examiner reports.
- **Love:** "past papers are the best revision", knowing what the questions look like.
- **Hate:** too few papers for new specifications, looking up the mark scheme too early, a bad mark on the first try.
- **Time-wasters:** doing the same easy paper again, "reading" a paper instead of doing it timed, saving the papers "for when I'm ready" (never).

### Revision websites and video tutorials
- **Uses:** free revision sites with topic notes and quizzes (with paid tiers), paid exam-question banks sorted by topic, video walkthroughs of maths papers, subject channels by teachers, "GCSE in one night" videos.
- **Love:** explains it better than school, free, short.
- **Hate:** autoplay, the comments, getting lost from one video to the next.
- **Time-wasters:** watching videos as revision without testing themselves, "the night-before full-course" video.

### Lecture recordings (university)
- **Uses:** lecture capture on the VLE.
- **Love:** "I can catch up at 2×", pausing.
- **Hate:** the backlog. Ten recordings unwatched by week 8 is a classic. Watching them feels like revision but is mostly passive.
- **Time-wasters:** rewatching without notes, saving them "for revision week".

### Notes apps and planners
- **Uses:** an all-in-one notes and database app with "aesthetic" templates, tablet handwriting apps, a free cloud calendar, paper planners, bullet journals, wall planners, whiteboards, sticky notes.
- **Love:** the fresh start, a plan that looks good.
- **Hate:** keeping it up. A planner abandoned in week 3. A system too elaborate to update.
- **Time-wasters:** **building the system instead of doing the work**: dashboards, colour codes, templates, the third new planner of the term. This is one of the biggest.

### AI chatbots
- **Uses:** explaining topics, summarising readings, generating quiz questions, "help me plan my week", and (at school and university, against the rules) writing work. A 2025 UK student survey reported that the vast majority of undergraduates now use generative AI in some way (the exact figure was not re-checked).
- **Love:** fast, available at 2am, doesn't judge.
- **Hate:** wrong answers, fear of being flagged for misconduct, unclear university AI rules.
- **Time-wasters:** chatting about the work instead of doing it, generating a perfect plan they won't follow.

### Focus tools
- **Uses:** a focus-timer app that grows a tree, Pomodoro timers, app blockers and screen-time limits, "study with me" live streams and timelapses, body-doubling video calls with strangers, white noise, lo-fi playlists, library bookings.
- **Love:** body doubling and "study with me" work for many people in this audience. Someone else working makes starting easier.
- **Hate:** turning off the screen-time limit with one tap, the timer going off mid-flow.
- **Time-wasters:** choosing the playlist, setting up the timer, "study with me" videos watched without studying.

### Group chats and forums
- **Uses:** course group chats, class chats, chat servers for courses or subjects, and the big UK student forum (for exam threads, grade boundary predictions and results-day threads).
- **Love:** "has anyone started?" relief, sharing resources, memes after exams.
- **Hate:** panic spreading at 11pm, people bragging about hours, post-exam "what did you get for 6?".
- **Time-wasters:** reading 200 messages, refreshing the exam thread.

### Methods by evidence (background for the writer)
- **Active recall and spaced practice** (retrieval, flashcards, blurting, past questions) are well supported.
- **Re-reading, highlighting and copying notes neatly** feel good but do relatively little.
- **The one-big-session approach** (cramming) works short term and is quickly forgotten.

### Habits that waste their time (summary)
1. Making the plan instead of starting the work.
2. Restarting the system after every bad day.
3. "Productive" procrastination: cleaning, tidying, making resources.
4. The "rest first" break that becomes the afternoon.
5. Starting from the beginning every time (first page of the essay, first week of lectures, first topic of the subject).
6. Waiting for a big block of free time that never comes.
7. Revising the subject they like, avoiding the one they need.

## 7. Beliefs and myths

| # | Belief | Verdict | Evidence in brief |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "I work better under pressure." | **Half true** | Deadlines do make starting easier for many people, but last-minute work is usually of lower quality and costs sleep. The deadline helps starting, not doing. |
| 2 | "If I can't do a full day, there's no point." | **False** | Short sessions add up. Spaced practice works better than one long block. |
| 3 | "Re-reading my notes is revision." | **Mostly false** | Re-reading feels familiar but tests memory little. Retrieval practice works better. |
| 4 | "Highlighting helps me remember." | **Mostly false** | Research reviews find highlighting has low usefulness on its own. |
| 5 | "I need the perfect plan before I can start." | **False** | The plan is not the work. A plan that moves when a day goes wrong is worth more than a perfect one. |
| 6 | "Missing one day ruins the plan." | **False** | It is one day. A plan that reshuffles keeps going. The "what the hell" effect (giving up after one slip) is the real danger. |
| 7 | "Cramming works." | **Half true** | It can get you through tomorrow's exam, but most of it is forgotten quickly. Linear A-levels and finals reward long-term memory. |
| 8 | "All-nighters are worth it before an exam." | **False** | Sleep supports memory consolidation, and sleep loss harms attention and recall. |
| 9 | "Other people are doing 8–10 hours a day." | **Mostly false** | UK undergraduates average about 11 hours of independent study a week (2026). Reported hours in group chats are often exaggerated. |
| 10 | "Starting is a willpower problem: I'm lazy." | **False** | Difficulty starting is common and has many causes. Small, concrete first steps help more than willpower. The account never says why it happens to them. |
| 11 | "Multitasking saves time." | **False** | Switching between tasks has a cost, and work done with lots of switching takes longer. |
| 12 | "Studying with music helps." | **Half true** | It depends on the person, the music and the task. Lyrics tend to interfere with reading and writing. |
| 13 | "I can revise with my phone next to me if I don't touch it." | **Half true** | Some studies suggest a phone in sight can reduce attention. Results are mixed. Out of the room is safer. |
| 14 | "Making flashcards is studying." | **Half true** | Making them involves some processing. Most of the value comes from testing yourself with them. |
| 15 | "Spaced repetition is only for languages and medicine." | **False** | Spacing helps memory in all subjects. |
| 16 | "Past papers are the best revision." | **Mostly true** | Practice testing is well supported and shows the format. But papers alone can leave gaps in content, and they need marking against the mark scheme. |
| 17 | "First year doesn't count, so it doesn't matter." | **Half true** | It often counts little or nothing towards the class, but it must be passed, and the habits and knowledge carry into the years that do count. |
| 18 | "I'll start properly at Easter." | **Half true** | Easter is a common start for exam revision, but May exams leave about 4–6 weeks. Earlier, smaller starts help. |
| 19 | "A new planner or app will fix it this time." | **False** | The tool changes, the starting problem doesn't. A tool helps if it reduces the next decision. |
| 20 | "I need a long block to get into the zone." | **Half true** | Deep work does need time. But waiting for a long free block is a common reason not to start at all. |
| 21 | "Rest is wasted time." | **False** | Rest and sleep support memory and attention. Guilty rest doesn't feel like rest, so a planned break works better. |
| 22 | "Learning styles: I'm a visual learner." | **False** | Matching teaching to "learning styles" has not been shown to improve learning. |
| 23 | "You can't get support without a formal label." | **Half true** | DSA usually needs evidence. But some universities offer support on a GP letter, and JCQ access arrangements are needs-led. Point to the service; don't advise. |
| 24 | "Extra time is an unfair advantage." | **False** | Access arrangements exist to remove a disadvantage. They are decided by evidence and are not available on request. |
| 25 | "Working 10+ hours a week at a job always wrecks grades." | **Half true** | Paid work is linked to less study time and lower engagement in UK surveys, but most students now do it. The question is how the remaining hours are used. |
| 26 | "The night before is when I actually learn it." | **Half true** | The night before is when the pressure starts. Recall from one night fades fast. |
| 27 | "I'm behind everyone." | **Usually false** | The group chat shows the most confident voices. Many are equally behind. |
| 28 | "Pomodoro (25/5) is the only right way." | **False** | Short blocks help many people start. The length matters less than having a clear start and a clear stop. |
| 29 | "Revising a subject I like counts the same." | **Half true** | It counts, but avoiding the hard subject is a common trap. |
| 30 | "AI can make my revision plan for me, so I'm sorted." | **Half true** | A plan helps, but plans don't start sessions. Also, AI can generate inaccurate content. |

## 9. Feelings

### What they fear
- Being found out: that the work isn't done, by a teacher, supervisor, parent or friend.
- Results day, and the email or page saying the grade.
- Being "lazy" — and being told so.
- Letting their family down, or wasting money at university.
- That it will always be like this.
- Resits, a capped mark, repeating a year.
- That other people find this easy.

### What they're proud of
- The night they finally started and wrote 1,500 words.
- Handing something in early, even once.
- A good mark on work done in a burst ("and I did it in one night").
- A streak of days, however short.
- Asking for help (an extension, a support plan, a chat with a tutor).
- A week where the plan more or less held.

### What makes them feel behind
- The group chat ("done all the past papers").
- Videos of people studying 10 hours a day, clean desks, 5am routines.
- A missed day, and a plan that now looks impossible.
- Counting the remaining weeks.
- A lecture backlog with a number on it.
- Starting later than everyone else in the house or the library.

### What makes them feel on top of it
- Knowing what the next block is, without deciding.
- One thing done before lunch.
- A day with a shape: a class, a block, a break.
- Someone else working next to them.
- Something finished and handed in.
- A plan that bent instead of breaking.

### The emotional moments of the year
- **September:** the fresh-start hope ("this year will be different").
- **Late October to November:** the first "I'm behind" week, the dark afternoons (now).
- **December:** the guilt of the Christmas revision that didn't happen.
- **January:** the panic of exams and the UCAS deadline, the "new year" restart.
- **March:** "it's real now", coursework and the count of weeks.
- **May to June:** exam days, after-exam crashes, the gap between papers.
- **August:** results days. Joy, relief or shock, and Clearing.
- **Any time:** the evening they look back and the afternoon is gone.

## 10. What they watch

### Formats that work with this audience
- **The timeline skit:** "15:30: I'll start at 4. 16:00: I'll start at 5. 21:40: …" Time stamps on screen. Instantly recognisable, and that's what makes people share it.
- **The two-voice everyday moment:** two people (housemates, siblings, a couple), one everyday scene about time. In Signals (2026-11-05) this format did up to 27× usual views on UK accounts. Keep it about the afternoon, never about a condition.
- **POV text-on-screen over a simple shot** (a desk, a laptop, a phone face down): "POV: you cleared your whole Saturday to revise".
- **The numbered series** ("Starting, ep. 1", "ep. 2"): brings people back. A charity account reached up to 41× usual views with this shape.
- **The reply-to-a-comment video:** answering a real comment about time. Never about someone's condition.
- **The "one small thing for tonight" video:** a single next step, done on screen in under 30 seconds.
- **The quiet "study with me" or timelapse:** desk, timer, real time passing. Works because it feels like company.
- **Before and after a single block:** "16:00 nothing, 16:25 one paragraph".
- **Calendar or season moments:** clocks going back, mock week, UCAS deadline, the first day of exams, results day.

### Tones that work
- Dry, honest, slightly self-deprecating, on their side.
- Calm, low-energy and specific, not motivational.
- Funny about the moment, never about the person.
- "Me too", not "here's what you're doing wrong".

### What makes them save
- One concrete thing they can do tonight.
- A short list or a template they want to come back to.
- A series they want to follow.

### What makes them share
- "This is literally you" — sending it to a friend, a sibling or a group chat.
- Something that describes their afternoon exactly.

### What makes them comment
- Their own version ("the way I'm watching this instead of my essay").
- A time or a number from their own day.
- Questions ("what do you do when…").
- **Care:** comments can turn personal ("I think I have…", "I'm on a waiting list…"). The account does not take up those threads as cases, and stores comments as themes only.

### What makes them scroll past
- Hustle and productivity-guru content: 5am routines, 10-hour study days.
- Perfect desks, perfect planners, pastel "aesthetic" systems.
- Lectures, being told off, long intros.
- Anything that feels like a brand pretending not to be one.
- Diagnostic quizzes and "signs you have…" content (also forbidden here).
- Toxic positivity: "just believe in yourself!"
- Slang used wrong, or by someone who obviously isn't one of them.

## 11. Where Offload fits

Only what product-facts.md allows. Posts on this account **do not describe how Offload works** (account rule). This map is for the team: it decides which pains Offload honestly fits, and keeps the writer from promising more.

### Pains Offload fits

| Pain (from §6) | What Offload actually does (plain words) |
|---|---|
| Making a third revision timetable instead of revising (#5, #34) | It builds a plan for the term from the student's own materials and exam dates, and puts study sessions on a calendar. They don't have to make the plan themselves. |
| A missed day breaks the whole plan (#19, #45) | When sessions are missed, they can be moved into free slots. It does not wipe the rest of the term. The student is told when sessions are moved. |
| Coming back after a week off to an impossible backlog (#4, #39) | After time away, catch-up can run so the calendar is ready, with a notice of what moved. |
| "I'll start at 4", deciding what to do (#1, #7, #35) | Each Offload block in their calendar links straight to that session. Approved wording: "You just click the link in your calendar and show up." That fits the account's line: Offload as the next block of time. |
| Shifts and lectures that eat the week (#16) | With Google Calendar connected, it places study around busy time already in their calendar and does not deliberately double-book it. |
| Re-reading and highlighting that feel like revision (beliefs #3, #4) | Its study methods are recall-based: flashcards with self-rating, blurting, Feynman or stem completion, exam-style questions generated from their materials, and mind-map gap-fill. |
| Flashcard reviews piling up, or forgetting what was learned (#4) | Flashcards use spaced repetition, so reviews come due based on memory. Weak recall can bring the material back sooner and can book catch-up time. |
| Not knowing what to do today | A dashboard shows today, what's due and the current phase. |
| New topics each week | New materials can be added weekly, and the plan grows without wiping completed work. |

### Pains Offload does not solve (say so; don't imply it)
- **The phone.** Offload does not block apps or screens.
- **The feeling itself:** guilt, overwhelm, fear. Offload is a study system, not support for mental health. No treatment claim.
- **Waiting lists, assessments, support plans, DSA, extensions, access arrangements.** Nothing to do with Offload. Point to the school's SENCo, the university's disability service, or the GP.
- **Writing the essay, the NEA, the dissertation.** Offload is about learning and recalling material for exams. It does not write, draft or mark work. AI marking of official scripts is not shipped.
- **Full past papers and mock exams.** Exam papers are "coming soon" and switched off in the beta. Do not promise past papers.
- **Exam-focus scheduling.** Switched off in the beta.
- **Money, shifts, family, housing.** Offload plans around a calendar; it can't free up time that isn't there.
- **Instant syncing of every calendar change.** Sync is not instantaneous for every external edit.
- **A guarantee of starting.** It puts the next block in the calendar. The student still has to show up.

### Things not to say (not in product-facts.md)
- Any price, trial length or tier details beyond "trial, then subscription".
- The names of phases beyond Learn, Test and Retain as listed. No promises about what FSRS changes on a card, what exactly rescheduling moves, what goes into remediation, or which calendars sync beyond Google Calendar.
- "An AI tutor", "it knows your syllabus", "made for ADHD", "clinically proven".
- Any feature not in the allowed list.

- **Line:** "The afternoon disappeared."
- **Goal:** the day is too many starts, and Offload is the next block of time.

## 12. Care

### From safety.md (non-negotiable)
- **Both stamps before the pack opens.** Nothing posts without a yes.
- **About time only.** Not treatment, not an assessment.
- **No "you have".** Never name a condition as the viewer's. Never "this is so ADHD", never "your brain is broken", never a quiz, never "signs you might…".
- **No treatment claim.** No medication, therapy, or "fix". Never use the words on the banned list (safety.md) — the filter drops the theme, and the writer does not put them back.
- **Under 18s read this.** Educational, age-appropriate, syllabus-true. No student names. No commenter profiles. Comments stored as themes only. No sexual or romantic bait. No fake grades.
- **Comments are not cases.** A comment like "I'm on a waiting list" or "I think I might have it" is never turned into a video about that person, never answered with advice about their condition, and never quoted with a handle.
- **If the line is distressing, the caption points to help and stops.** It does not continue the story.
- **Ads:** an ambassador post begins with "Ad". Other posts never say "Ad". Never invent an ambassador.

### From the research
- **The waiting.** Many viewers are waiting years for an assessment. Don't joke about waiting lists, don't describe services as "fast", and never suggest routes (private, Right to Choose, a specific provider). If it comes up, the line is: support may be available; ask your school, college, university or GP.
- **Eligibility.** Never say who qualifies for DSA, extra time, rest breaks or support plans. These are decided by evidence and by the institution. Point to the SENCo or the disability service.
- **Shame.** Their biggest fear is being "lazy". Never imply laziness, never show "you just need discipline". The joke is always the afternoon, never the person.
- **Comparison.** Avoid hours-studied numbers, streaks as a measure of worth, perfect desks. They make this audience feel behind.
- **Results-day and exam-season distress.** Around exam days and results days, some viewers will be in a bad way. Light, kind, no pressure. If a post's subject could hit hard (failing, resits, Clearing), consider whether to post at all.
- **Sleep and health.** Never glamorise all-nighters, skipping meals or caffeine-heavy studying.
- **Self-harm and crisis.** If any comment suggests risk, do not engage with the story. Follow the team's escalation process. The caption or reply points to help: in the UK, **999** in an emergency, **NHS 111** (in England, option 2 for urgent mental health help), the free 24-hour listening line **116 123**, and, for under-19s, the free children's helpline **0800 1111**. Check the current wording with the team before using these.
- **Awareness months and days.** Take part only as Offload, only about time, never as a voice on a condition. October (ADHD Awareness Month) has just ended. Mental Health Awareness Week (May) falls in exam season, so be extra careful then.
- **AI and integrity.** Don't suggest that Offload (or anything) writes coursework. Universities and exam boards treat it as misconduct.
- **Uncertainty.** If a date or rule above is marked "check", check it before it goes near a card.

### Quick self-check before anything goes to the stamps
1. Is it about time, and only time?
2. Could a 14-year-old watch it?
3. Does it name, hint at or joke about a condition as the viewer's?
4. Does it promise anything Offload doesn't do, or describe how it works?
5. Is there a number on it that isn't in the week's brief?
6. If someone in a bad place saw it, would the caption point to help and stop?

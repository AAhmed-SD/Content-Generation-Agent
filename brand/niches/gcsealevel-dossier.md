## 1. Who they are

The audience is students in England and Wales taking GCSEs (usually ages 14 to 16) and A-levels (usually 16 to 18). It also includes students aged 16 and over who are resitting GCSE English Language or maths. Most of them are under 18. Many are 14 or 15. Treat every post as if a 14-year-old will see it, because one will.

The common thread: **too much specification, not enough evenings, and a plan that falls apart the first time they miss a night.**

### Year 10 (age 14 to 15): GCSE year one
- **Where:** secondary schools and academies. Exams are two years away.
- **How many (background):** one national year group in England is roughly 650,000 to 700,000 pupils, and the secondary bulge is near its peak around now **[check]**. Add Wales.
- **What makes them different:** GCSE options have just started. Year 10 is the first year in which "it counts", but there's no deadline they can feel yet. They get end-of-year exams in June or July, often called "Year 10 mocks", and for many that's the first scare. Work experience, where the school still runs it, often falls in the summer term. They are the most distractible group and the least likely to search for revision content in autumn. They do respond to "start now, small" ideas and to content about how hard the jump into GCSE work is.

### Year 11 (age 15 to 16): GCSE exam year
- **Where:** the same schools. Most take 8 to 10 GCSEs. Some take triple science, which means three separate science GCSEs, and some take combined science, a double award graded as a pair such as 6-5 or 7-7.
- **How many (background):** the same cohort size. JCQ reports several million GCSE entries a year across England, Wales and Northern Ireland **[check the latest]**.
- **What makes them different:** every subject feels urgent at the same time. They sit mocks, often in November or December, sometimes in January, and many schools run a second round in February or March. Post-16 applications go out in autumn and winter, and sixth-form entry often depends on getting a grade 6 or 7 in the subjects they want to take further. English and maths have a threshold: grade 4 is the "standard pass" and grade 5 the "strong pass". Below a 4 means more English or maths after 16. Speaking exams, coursework (called NEA in some subjects) and art exam days come before the written exams. Then study leave, the exams, prom, and the "longest summer". This group searches hardest and saves the most.

### Year 12 (age 16 to 17): A-level year one / Lower Sixth
- **Where:** school sixth forms, sixth-form colleges and general FE colleges. Most take three A-levels, a few take four. Some also take an EPQ, a 5,000-word project or an artefact with a write-up, and some take Core Maths.
- **What makes them different:** the jump from GCSE is a shock. There's far more content, they're expected to work alone, and they have free periods for the first time. Most A-levels are linear, so everything is examined at the end of Year 13. Year 12 doesn't feel high-stakes, yet the end-of-Year-12 exams often decide predicted grades. Part-time jobs, driving lessons (17th birthdays), relationships and social life all speed up. University research and early personal-statement drafts start in the summer term.

### Year 13 (age 17 to 18): A-level exam year / Upper Sixth
- **Where:** as above.
- **How many (background):** A-level entries in England run to several hundred thousand a year, from roughly a third to two-fifths of the cohort **[check]**.
- **What makes them different:** they carry two calendars at once, applications and revision. Applications for medicine, dentistry, veterinary science and the two ancient universities closed on 15 October 2026. The main deadline for equal consideration is in January 2027: the admissions service moved it to mid-January for 2026 entry, so expect around 14 January 2027 **[check]**. Interviews, offers and admissions tests run through autumn and winter. NEA deadlines fall in spring. Mocks are usually in January, sometimes December. The offer (firm and insurance choices) hangs over the whole summer. Many are 18 by the exams, but plenty are still 17. Some go to apprenticeship assessment centres instead of, or alongside, university applications.

### Post-16 resit students (age 16 to 19, sometimes older)
- **Where:** mostly FE colleges, some sixth forms. They're often on vocational courses (BTECs, T Levels, apprenticeships) or doing A-levels with a resit on top.
- **The rule (background):** under the condition of funding, students who got a grade 3 must keep studying GCSE English and/or maths. Those with grade 2 or below may take another qualification instead. The details have been changing **[check the current rules]**.
- **What makes them different:** they're sitting the November resits **right now**, in early to mid-November 2026, with results on 14 January 2027. Others will resit in the summer series. Many have been studying the same maths since Year 9 and feel stuck and embarrassed about it. They're more likely to have jobs, to commute, and to be the first in their family on this route. Handle with care: never imply that a resit means failure.

### Cross-cutting groups (never as labels)
- **Boards:** five boards across England and Wales (they're listed in the account file). The same subject has different papers, different names for practicals and different question styles on each board. Name a board only when the brief does. On this account that means AQA, and only when the brief already uses it.
- **Wales:** different qualification reforms are running in Wales. New "Made for Wales" GCSEs began first teaching in September 2025, so some 2027 papers may be new **[check by subject]**. Wales also has a skills baccalaureate. Don't assume England's details apply in Wales.
- **Access arrangements:** Ofqual's figures for 2024 to 2025 put 25% extra time at between 16.6% and 25.5% of students (account file). Readers, scribes, word processors, rest breaks and separate rooms are all common. Never assume who has them. Never use them as a label.
- **Other realities:** young carers, students who work, students who fast in Ramadan (around February to March 2027 **[check]**), students with SEND, students with no quiet room, students with no laptop. Some can afford tutors and many can't.

## 2. Their year, month by month

Term dates vary by local authority and trust. The dates below are typical for England in 2026 to 2027 **[check locally]**.

### September (2026)
- **Happening:** term starts in the first week. Year 10 begin their options and Year 12 begin their A-levels. Year 11 and Year 13 get their "this is the year" assemblies. Year 13 meet personal-statement and reference deadlines set by school, often late September or early October for early applicants. Post-results reviews of marking from the summer are being settled **[check deadlines]**. Resit students enrol and are told they're resitting.
- **Worried about:** whether they chose the right A-levels (some swap in the first fortnight). The step up. Whether they've forgotten everything over summer. For Year 13: predicted grades are being set now.
- **Instead of studying:** new timetable, new friends, new sixth-form freedom, the first part-time job shifts, settling-in socials, autumn TikTok trends.

### October (2026)
- **Happening:** the 15 October deadline for early applications (medicine, dentistry, vet, the two ancient universities), plus admissions tests for some courses around October and November **[check]**. Year 11 attend sixth-form and college open evenings. Half term is usually the last week of October (around 26 to 30 October 2026). Resit students get their last push before November.
- **Worried about:** a personal statement that still reads badly, the first real assessment grades, being behind before Christmas.
- **Instead of studying:** Halloween, half term, "I'll start properly after half term", late-night gaming, football season, shifts.

### November (2026), now
- **Happening:** **November resits** in GCSE English Language and maths, early to mid-November 2026, with results on 14 January 2027. **Mock season begins.** Many Year 11 and some Year 13 sit mocks from mid-November into December. Bonfire Night has just passed. Interview invitations arrive for some Year 13s. Sixth-form application forms open.
- **Worried about:** a full spec they haven't seen since Year 10. The first time they ever revise properly. "What if my mock decides my prediction?" Resit students: "Not again."
- **Instead of studying:** making the revision timetable (for hours), colour-coding notes, watching other people's study vlogs, "study with me" videos, group chats about which topics will come up, the dark evenings.

### December (2026)
- **Happening:** mocks continue in many schools. Christmas concerts, non-uniform days, the last week of term (around 18 December). Holidays run from about 21 December to 1 January. Interviews for some courses. Mock results start coming back just before the break or just after.
- **Worried about:** a bad mock grade on paper. Telling parents. A Christmas break that "should" be revision.
- **Instead of studying:** Christmas, family, jobs (retail shifts get heavier), sleeping in, the "between Christmas and New Year, what day is it" fog.

### January (2027)
- **Happening:** back around 4 to 5 January. **Year 13 mocks** in many schools, often in January's first three weeks. Year 11 mocks or mock results. The main application deadline in mid-January **[check exact date]**. **Resit results on 14 January.** Vocational students sit January exam windows. Sixth-form offers start.
- **Worried about:** mock grades turning into predictions. The resit result. Dark mornings, no energy. "The exams are in May, that's basically now."
- **Instead of studying:** New Year "this time it's different" plans, which fade by the third week. Gym starts. Phone again.

### February (2027)
- **Happening:** half term is usually mid-February (around 15 to 19 February). Ramadan probably starts in early or mid-February 2027 **[check]**. NEA drafting in earnest: history, English Literature, geography, computer science, DT, art. Second mocks for some Year 11s. Year 13 university offers come in. Year 11s get sixth-form offers and conditions.
- **Worried about:** the coursework draft due after half term. The spec still feels endless. Mock feedback that says "more evaluation" with no explanation of how.
- **Instead of studying:** half term trips, catching up on sleep, gaming, Valentine's Day (keep it out of content).

### March (2027)
- **Happening:** NEA final deadlines inside school (the board deadline for submitting marks is usually mid-May **[check]**). GCSE art and DT pieces are in progress. MFL speaking prep begins. Easter is early: Good Friday is 26 March 2027 and Easter Sunday is 28 March, so many schools break in late March **[check]**. Year 13 choose their firm and insurance offers.
- **Worried about:** too many deadlines at once. Easter "being the big revision break" and arriving with no plan.
- **Instead of studying:** finishing coursework (which is technically work but crowds out revision), the clocks going forward, spring sport.

### April (2027)
- **Happening:** Easter holidays run into early or mid-April. Back for the summer term. **Art and design exam days**: the externally set task done in supervised hours (about 10 hours for GCSE, about 15 for A-level **[check]**). **MFL speaking exams** typically run late April to mid-May. Science practical work gets signed off. Revision lessons, after-school sessions, Saturday sessions in some schools.
- **Worried about:** "I wasted Easter." Speaking exams. Running out of time on whole topics.
- **Instead of studying:** sunny evenings, final-year socials, shirt-signing plans, prom outfits, driving tests.

### May (2027)
- **Happening:** **The summer series starts on Monday 10 May 2027** (JCQ common timetable). The papers run in morning sessions (usually 9:00) and afternoon sessions (usually 13:30) **[check start times locally]**. Year 11 study leave starts mid-May in many schools. Whit half term is around 31 May to 4 June 2027 and lands in the middle of the exams. Eid al-Adha is likely mid-May 2027 **[check]**. NEA marks go to the boards.
- **Worried about:** the first paper, timetable clashes, two exams in one day, "Paper 2 was diabolical", heat in the hall, exams going wrong on the day.
- **Instead of studying:** post-mortems in group chats after each paper, exam-reaction TikToks, sleeping between papers, panic-watching videos the night before.

### June (2027)
- **Happening:** exams continue until **Friday 18 June 2027**, with a contingency day on **Wednesday 23 June**. Year 11 leavers' day, shirt signing, prom (late June). Year 12 end-of-year exams, usually June or July, often used for predicted grades. Year 10 end-of-year exams and work experience.
- **Worried about:** the last paper. "Did I fail that?" Year 12s: predictions start here, and nobody told them how much it counts.
- **Instead of studying:** finishing, celebrating, "summer", the first paid jobs at 16.

### July (2027)
- **Happening:** the summer term ends around 20 to 23 July **[check]**. Year 12 attend university open days, summer schools, work experience, early personal-statement drafts and medicine admissions-test prep. Year 10 face the "next year is Year 11" talk. Year 11 and Year 13 have long summers.
- **Worried about:** results (a low hum in the background). Year 12s: university choices and the new personal-statement format (three structured questions **[check]**).
- **Instead of studying:** holidays, festivals, jobs, driving, sleep.

### August (2027)
- **Happening:** **A-level results day** is a Thursday in mid-August, and **GCSE results day** is the following Thursday **[check the 2027 dates in the JCQ key-dates document]**. Clearing. Sixth-form enrolment. Reviews of marking. Resit decisions. Grade boundaries are published on results day.
- **Worried about:** missing the offer, missing grade 4 in maths or English, telling family, comparing with friends.
- **Instead of studying:** everything. Then a late-August panic about Year 12 or Year 13 starting.

## 3. The courses and the assessments

Formats differ by board and subject. Below are the common shapes. Check the specific spec before naming any paper, any number of marks or any timing on a card.

### GCSE grading
- Grades run from 9 to 1 (U is unclassified). 4 is a "standard pass" and 5 a "strong pass". Combined science gives a double grade, such as 5-4.
- **Tiers:** maths, the sciences and some languages offer Foundation and Higher papers. Foundation caps at grade 5. Higher covers 9 to 4, with an "allowed 3" if a student just misses. Students worry about tier entry: "If I go higher and fail, do I get a U?"

### GCSE written papers (core subjects)
- **Maths:** three papers, one non-calculator, all the same length (1 hour 30 minutes on most boards **[check]**). Marking uses method and accuracy marks. "Show that" questions and multi-step problem solving are hard. A formulae sheet has been allowed in recent series **[check whether it continues for 2027]**.
- **English Language:** two papers on most boards. Reading questions on unseen extracts, then a writing task (descriptive or narrative on one paper, viewpoint on the other). There's also a spoken-language endorsement, graded separately and not part of the 9 to 1 grade. Students struggle with the timing, with language versus structure questions, and with writing "creatively" under time pressure.
- **English Literature:** usually two papers, **closed book**. Shakespeare, a 19th-century novel, a modern text, an anthology poetry cluster and unseen poetry. Quotations have to be memorised. Spelling, punctuation and grammar are marked on some questions. The hardest thing is remembering quotations for every theme and every character.
- **Combined science:** six shorter papers. **Separate sciences:** six longer papers, two per science. Required practicals are examined in the written papers (different boards use different names). Students struggle with "6-mark" extended answers, practical method questions and maths in physics.
- **Humanities:** history (sources, interpretations, extended questions), geography (fieldwork questions in the papers, resources, extended answers), RS (beliefs, evaluation questions). Mark schemes are level-based, with marks for spelling, punctuation and grammar on some questions.
- **Modern foreign languages:** new specifications were taught from 2024, with the first exams in 2026, so 2027 is only the second year. There are four skills papers, listening, speaking, reading and writing, and the new elements include dictation, reading aloud and set vocabulary lists **[check by board]**. The speaking exam is recorded and marked externally. Students find speaking the most frightening.

### GCSE coursework (NEA) and practical assessment
- **Practical subjects:** DT, food, drama, music, art, PE and others. Coursework is typically between 30% and 60% of the grade, depending on the subject **[check]**. Art has a portfolio plus an externally set task done over supervised hours. Food has investigation and practical tasks. Drama has devising and performance. PE has practical performance and analysis.
- **Marking:** usually by teachers, then moderated by the board.
- **What's hard:** deadlines collide with exams. Coursework can't be rushed in the way an exam crammer can. Folders that are "nearly done" never close.

### A-level grading and papers
- Grades run A* to E. Most subjects are linear, with **two or three written papers, typically around 2 hours each, all at the end of Year 13**. Some subjects have a fourth component **[check by subject]**.
- **Maths:** usually three papers covering pure, statistics and mechanics, plus a large data set. **Further maths** has extra papers. What's hard: proof, long multi-step questions, losing marks through presentation, and "applied" contexts.
- **Sciences:** papers combine content with practical skills, plus a separate **practical endorsement** (pass or not classified) that sits outside the A* to E grade. The endorsement needs a minimum set of practicals done and recorded. What's hard: long-answer synoptic questions, essay-style questions in biology, maths in chemistry and physics.
- **Essay subjects** (history, English, politics, sociology, psychology, economics, RS and others): extended essays under timed conditions, levels-based marking against assessment objectives. What's hard: "evaluate", "to what extent", using scholarly views, and timing.

### A-level NEA
- **Common examples:** a history independent study (several thousand words), an English Literature comparative essay, a geography independent investigation using fieldwork, a computer science programming project, plus large coursework in DT, art and drama.
- **Marking:** teachers mark it, then the board moderates it. Coursework is usually 20% of the grade, more in practical subjects **[check by subject]**.
- **What's hard:** project management, word counts, sources, deadlines that fall at the same time as mocks and applications.

### The EPQ
- A project of about 5,000 words, or an artefact plus a write-up, with a production log and a presentation. Worth half an A-level in tariff points **[check]**. Students find it hard to keep it moving while A-level revision piles up.

### Mocks (PPEs / trial exams)
- These are school exams in exam conditions using past or practice papers. They're marked by teachers to the board's mark scheme, with boundaries the school picks or borrows from previous years. They land November to January, and some schools hold a second round in February or March. Students find them high-stakes because predictions, sixth-form places and sets can follow from them, yet "it's only a mock".

### Resits
- **November series** (English Language and maths only, for students aged 16 and over): results on 14 January 2027.
- **Summer series:** the same papers as everyone else.

### Practice assessments in school
- End-of-topic tests, retrieval quizzes, homework platforms, booklets of exam-style questions and timed essays. Teachers often give "personalised learning checklists": the spec, topic by topic, which students rate red, amber or green.

### Marking and command words
- Mark schemes give an answer, or indicative content and levels. Assessment objectives (AO1, AO2, AO3) decide what earns marks.
- Command words (state, describe, explain, compare, evaluate, analyse, to what extent) change what a full-marks answer looks like.
- **What students find hardest:** knowing the content but not knowing what the examiner wants. They also run out of time, or score badly on extended answers even when they understood the topic.

## 4. A day in their life

### A. Year 11, school day during mock season (Tuesday, mid-November)
- **6:45** Alarm. Phone. Snooze. Group chat: "what's on today", "is the bio mock today or tomorrow".
- **7:30** Bus. Quizlet-style flashcards half open, mostly scrolling.
- **8:30** Form time, then a mock in the hall: 1 hour 45 minutes of English Literature. Can't remember the second quotation they needed.
- **10:30** Break. Post-mortem: "did you do the theme question?"
- **11:00 to 3:15** Lessons as normal, plus "go over this tonight".
- **3:30** Home. Snack. Phone. "Twenty minutes."
- **5:00** Homework platform for maths. A 30-minute task takes an hour.
- **6:30** Dinner.
- **7:30** "Revision." Opens the timetable. It's already wrong because yesterday was missed. Spends 20 minutes rewriting it.
- **8:00 to 9:00** Makes notes on the topic for tomorrow's mock. Notes look nice. Recalls little.
- **9:00 to 11:30** Phone, gaming, TikTok "what I eat before exams". Bed late.
- **Where the time went:** about 45 useful minutes, mostly homework. Re-planning and copying ate the rest.

### B. Year 13, frees and a part-time job (Thursday, January)
- **8:30** Free period. In the common room. Says hello to everyone. Opens the laptop.
- **9:30 to 12:30** Two lessons. Questions on the NEA draft.
- **12:30** Lunch, plus UCAS-chat panic about who has had which offer.
- **1:30** Another free. Meant to do NEA references. Does a practice paper question, checks the mark scheme. Gets distracted by interview prep.
- **3:15** Off to work: shift until 8:30pm in retail or hospitality.
- **9:00** Home, eats, tired.
- **9:45** Opens flashcards: 210 due. Gives up at 40.
- **10:30 to 12:30** Phone in bed.
- **Where the time went:** two frees "for study" turned into maybe an hour. The job takes the evening. Flashcard reviews pile up and become a guilt number.

### C. The Sunday before mocks (Year 11)
- **10:30** Up late. "Big session today."
- **11:00** Breakfast, shower, finds the "perfect" spot to work.
- **12:00** Watches a "how to revise" video for 25 minutes.
- **12:30** Starts a new notes page. Picks a favourite topic.
- **1:30** Lunch. Phone.
- **2:30** Past paper. Hits a hard question, stops, checks the mark scheme early.
- **3:30** Family visit or a sports match.
- **6:00** Panic: five subjects still untouched.
- **7:00** Makes a plan for next week instead of revising.
- **8:00 to 10:30** Half-hearted flashcards, watching something.
- **Where the time went:** probably 2 to 3 real hours, often on the topic they already knew.

### D. Easter holiday day (Year 13)
- **9:00** Up because of the school's "Easter timetable".
- **9:30 to 12:00** Library or kitchen table. Past-paper questions. First session goes well.
- **12:00** Lunch, scrolls for an hour.
- **1:30 to 3:00** Chemistry topic they hate. Watches a video explanation at 1.5x speed. Makes notes.
- **3:00** Friends invite them out. Goes.
- **7:00** Dinner.
- **8:00** "One more session." Opens the mark scheme and checks answers. Gets a lower score than expected.
- **9:00** Feeling bad. TikTok.
- **Where the time went:** 4 to 5 hours of decent work, but no recovery plan if tomorrow fails. One bad day often wipes out two more.

### E. Exam day on study leave (Year 11, May)
- **6:30** Up early. Can't eat.
- **7:15** Reads quotation cards, or formulae, over breakfast.
- **8:15** In school. Lines up outside the hall. Last-minute cramming with friends.
- **9:00 to 10:45** The paper.
- **11:00** Out. "Did you do question 5?" Group chat already busy.
- **12:30** Home. Sleeps for an hour.
- **3:00** Starts on the next subject: in two days.
- **4:00 to 7:00** Past paper, mark scheme, topic gaps.
- **8:00** Exam-reaction TikToks, memes.
- **10:00** "Final tips for tomorrow's paper" videos, then bed.
- **Where the time went:** decent hours, but post-exam doom and recovery eat the afternoon.

### F. Resit student at college with a job (November)
- **7:00** Up. Commute by bus.
- **9:00 to 12:00** Vocational lessons.
- **1:00 to 2:30** Maths resit lesson, again. Same topics as Year 10.
- **3:00** Leaves for a shift.
- **8:00** Home, tired.
- **9:00** Half an hour of practice questions. Gets stuck on algebra and stops.
- **Where the time went:** almost all of it to college and work. The resit gets the leftovers.

### G. Year 12, ordinary school day (October)
- **8:30 to 3:30** Lessons and frees. Two frees spent chatting.
- **4:00** Home. Notes for one subject.
- **5:30** Gym or a job shift.
- **7:30** Homework essay or a problem set.
- **9:00** Social life, driving theory, online.
- **Where the time went:** not much revision. "There's no exam this year", except the June exam that sets predictions.

## 5. How they actually study

All tools are described by type here. Don't name them on a card.

### Flashcard apps (spaced repetition and quiz decks)
- **Love:** fast, done on the phone, shared decks, feel productive.
- **Hate:** a mountain of due cards after a missed week. Shared decks don't match the spec. Making cards takes longer than learning them. They flip without really trying to recall.
- **Waste:** copying the textbook onto cards, "decorating" cards, clicking "easy" to get through.

### Free revision websites (notes, topic questions, past papers by board)
- **Love:** free, organised by board and spec, past papers and mark schemes in one place.
- **Hate:** too much choice, wrong board, outdated papers from old specs.
- **Waste:** downloading everything, doing the easy topic again.

### Video explainers and "study with me" streams
- **Love:** a clear explanation, someone in the room with them.
- **Hate:** autoplay, hours lost.
- **Waste:** watching instead of doing questions, re-watching "how to revise" videos.

### Homework and quiz platforms set by school
- **Love:** short tasks, instant marking.
- **Hate:** forced, codes to copy, repetitive, sometimes feel like busywork.
- **Waste:** guessing until the answer goes green.

### Past papers and mark schemes
- **Love:** "closest thing to the real exam", teachers' favourite method.
- **Hate:** marking takes ages, mark schemes are cryptic, they run out of papers, new specs have few papers.
- **Waste:** opening the mark scheme before finishing, doing whole papers without fixing the gaps.

### Blurting, mind maps, notes apps, cue cards
- **Love:** blurting is quick and shows gaps. Mind maps feel creative.
- **Hate:** blurting feels bad because it shows gaps. Mind maps take forever.
- **Waste:** beautiful notes that are never tested.

### Revision guides, workbooks, knowledge organisers
- **Love:** short, ordered by spec, schools often give them.
- **Hate:** dense, boring, re-reading feels useless.
- **Waste:** highlighting everything.

### AI chatbots
- **Use:** explaining a topic, writing practice questions, rewording, "summarise this".
- **Hate:** wrong answers, not matching the spec or the board's style.
- **Risk:** use for NEA can break the rules.

### Group chats and study servers
- **Love:** support, shared resources, "which topics are coming up".
- **Hate:** panic spreading, rumours about papers, comparison.
- **Waste:** hours of chatting about revision.

### Tutoring
- **Love:** accountability, explanation.
- **Hate:** cost. Not everyone has it, and that inequality is felt.

### Timetables and planners
- **Love:** making them.
- **Hate:** keeping them. One missed day breaks the whole plan.
- **Waste:** rebuilding the plan instead of doing it.

### Habits that waste their time
- Making a new timetable instead of studying.
- Revising favourite topics.
- Re-reading and highlighting.
- Long sessions with no recall.
- Phone in reach.
- Copying notes neatly.
- Watching study content instead of doing it.
- "I'll start tomorrow."
- All-nighters before mocks.
- Comparing hours with friends.

## 6. Pains, ranked

1. Opening the spec checklist for one subject and seeing 40 red topics, then realising there are nine more subjects.
2. Missing one evening, and the whole colour-coded timetable is wrong by Tuesday.
3. Sitting down at 7:30pm and spending the first 40 minutes deciding what to revise.
4. Coming back after a week off and seeing 600 flashcards due.
5. The mock result on paper, two grades below the target written on the front of the exercise book.
6. "We've finished the content" in March, and they've forgotten September.
7. Knowing the topic in class and blanking on the 6-mark question.
8. Opening the mark scheme and realising the answer needed "evaluation" they don't know how to write.
9. The night before a paper with three topics never touched.
10. The NEA draft due on the same week as two mocks.
11. Remembering a quotation but not which character said it.
12. The English Literature paper being closed book, with 15 poems to know.
13. Doing a whole past paper and having no time left to mark it.
14. Group chat at 11pm: "has anyone even started chem?" Everyone has.
15. A Sunday planned as a "big session" ending at 4pm with one topic done.
16. A part-time shift on the night they planned to revise.
17. The resit maths topic they've failed three times, again.
18. The teacher saying "just do past papers" when they've done all the ones for the new spec.
19. Picking up the phone "for one second" and losing an hour.
20. The Easter holiday that ended with "I did nothing".
21. Two exams on one day, and dinner skipped between them.
22. Writing a beautiful set of notes and recalling nothing from them a week later.

## 7. Beliefs and myths

1. **"Re-reading notes is revising."** Mostly false. It feels fluent but retrieval practice beats re-reading.
2. **"Highlighting helps."** Mostly false. It has low benefit unless paired with recall.
3. **"Flashcards are best."** Half true. They work with active recall and spacing, not passive flipping.
4. **"Past papers are the best revision."** Half true. They're strong when marked and gaps are fixed, but weak as the only method.
5. **"Cramming the night before works."** Half true. It can help short-term recall but forgets fast and costs sleep.
6. **"All-nighters help."** False. Sleep supports memory consolidation and performance.
7. **"I'm a visual learner."** False. Learning-styles matching has weak evidence.
8. **"Spaced repetition works."** True. Distributed practice is one of the strongest findings in learning research.
9. **"Testing yourself is just for checking."** False. Retrieval itself strengthens memory.
10. **"Making notes is learning."** Half true. Generating helps, but copying out doesn't.
11. **"Mocks don't matter."** Half true. They don't count on the certificate, but often shape predictions, sets and sixth-form places.
12. **"Grade boundaries are fixed."** False. They change each series.
13. **"You need to revise 8 hours a day."** False. Consistent shorter sessions often beat marathon days, and the right amount varies.
14. **"You can't revise for English."** False. Quotations, structures, techniques and timing can all be practised.
15. **"Maths is just practice."** Mostly true. Practice with feedback, mixed topics and recall of methods.
16. **"If I start early, I'll forget it by May."** Half true. Without review, yes. With spaced review, early study pays off.
17. **"The exams this year will be harder/easier."** Unknowable. Boundaries adjust to difficulty.
18. **"Revising with music helps."** Half true. It depends on the person. Lyrics can distract during reading tasks.
19. **"Phones don't distract me."** Mostly false. Their presence alone can lower attention for many.
20. **"Leaked papers are real."** Almost always false. Rumours spread every year. Sharing leaked papers is malpractice.
21. **"The topic that came up last year won't come up again."** False. Patterns are not reliable.
22. **"A grade 4 means you've failed."** False. 4 is a standard pass.
23. **"You can't get a 9 in Foundation."** True. Foundation caps at grade 5.
24. **"Long sessions are better."** Half true. Deep focus helps, but breaks and spacing matter.
25. **"Mixing topics in one session is confusing."** Half true. It feels harder, but interleaving can improve later performance in subjects like maths.
26. **"Making a timetable is the hard part."** False. Keeping to it, and recovering when it breaks, is the hard part.
27. **"If I don't feel stressed, I'm not working hard."** False. Some stress can help, but chronic stress harms sleep and performance.
28. **"Teachers mark mocks easier/harder than the real thing."** Varies. Schools use different boundaries.

## 11. Where Offload fits

Use only product-facts.md, and remember the account rule: **do not describe how Offload works** on cards or in films. The note below is background for the team.

### Pains Offload maps to

1. **Too much spec for one evening.** Offload turns a student's materials and exam dates into a term plan with study sessions placed on a calendar.
2. **Missed evenings breaking the plan.** Offload moves overdue work into free slots and tells the student when sessions have moved. It doesn't wipe the rest of the term.
3. **Not knowing what to revise tonight.** The plan, the spaced-repetition flashcards and the calendar decide what's due.
4. **Flashcard mountains after a week off.** Spaced-repetition reviews come due "when memory pressure says so", and catch-up can run after time away.
5. **Notes that never get tested.** Offload has recall methods built in: flashcards, blurting, Feynman / stem completion, mind-map gap-fill and generated exam-style questions.
6. **Weak topics staying weak.** When performance is weak, Offload can create remediation cards and book re-learn time.
7. **Rebuilding the timetable over and over.** The plan is "living" and reschedules itself.
8. **Calendar clashes.** With Google Calendar connected, Offload places study around existing busy time and doesn't double-book on purpose.
9. **Starting a session takes effort.** Each block in the student's calendar links straight into the session. Say it like this: "You just click the link in your calendar and show up."
10. **New material each week.** Weekly additions grow the plan without wiping completed work.

### Pains Offload doesn't solve
- **Past papers and mock suites:** Offload has no full past-paper or mock exam suite in beta. The exam papers feature is marked "coming soon".
- **Exam marking:** no AI marking of official exam scripts.
- **NEA or coursework writing.**
- **Phone distraction.**
- **No quiet place to work.**
- **The cost of tutoring.**
- **Exam-day nerves.**
- **Mental health support.**

### Notes for the team
- Offload's stated primary users are "University / college / A-Level-style students". GCSE use isn't listed explicitly.
- Under-18s may not be able to use a Google account or connect a calendar. Don't assume they can.
- The spaced-repetition method, FSRS, is only described as applying to flashcards. Don't say more.
